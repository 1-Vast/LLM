identify efficacious drug combinations from the plethora of safe drugs that could hypothetically exhibit antiviral activity. The infeasibility of extensive combinatorial screens triggered the need for new methods that would require substantially less screening than an exhaustive evaluation. Outside of biology, there has been much interest in how areas of machine learning, including active learning and sequential model optimization, can be utilized to efficiently explore large spaces of possibilities through the intelligent acquisition and interpretation of data. Revised 2023 Aug 30; Accepted 2023 Sep 6; Collection date 2023 Oct 23.</p></sec></notes></front><body><sec id="sec1" disp-level="1"><title>Introduction</title><p id="p0050">Drug combinations are an important therapeutic strategy for treating diseases that are subject to evolutionary dynamics, in particular cancers and infectious diseases.<xref rid="bib1" ref-type="bibr"><sup>1</sup></xref><sup>,</sup><xref rid="bib2" ref-type="bibr"><sup>2</sup></xref> Conceptually, as tumors or pathogens are subject to change over time, they may develop resistance to a single agent<xref rid="bib3" ref-type="bibr"><sup>3</sup></xref>—motivating one to target multiple biological mechanisms simultaneously.<xref rid="bib4" ref-type="bibr"><sup>4</sup></xref> Discovering synergistic drug combinations is a key step toward developing robust therapies, as they hold the potential for greater efficacy while reducing dose and hopefully limiting the likelihood of adverse effects. For example, in a drug repurposing scenario (i.e., uncovering new indications for known drugs), the ReFRAME library of <inline-formula><mml:math xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="M6" altimg="si1.gif"><mml:mrow><mml:mo linebreak="goodbreak" linebreakstyle="after">∼</mml:mo></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula>12,000 clinical-stage compounds<xref rid="bib5" ref-type="bibr"><sup>5</sup></xref> leads to <inline-formula><mml:math xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="M7" altimg="si1.gif"><mml:mrow><mml:mo linebreak="goodbreak" linebreakstyle="after">∼</mml:mo></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula>72 million pairwise combinations; this does not appear tractable with standard high-throughput screening (HTS) technology—even at a single dose.<xref rid="bib6" ref-type="bibr"><sup>6</sup></xref> Moreover, with patient-derived organoids (PDOs) being examined as a biomarker within personalized medicine clinical studies,<xref rid="bib7" ref-type="bibr"><sup>7</sup></xref><sup>,</sup><xref rid="bib8" ref-type="bibr"><sup>8</sup></xref> the search space expands further to identify efficacious drug combinations specific to the mutation profile in question.</p><p id="p0055">With the recent COVID-19 global health crisis, there has been the need for rapid drug repurposing that would allow for expedited and derisked clinical trials. Due to the complexity of selecting drug combinations and the minimal training data publicly available, studies have typically been limited toward monotherapy repurposing from a variety of angles—often involving artificial intelligence (AI) techniques to provide recommendations.<xref rid="bib9" ref-type="bibr"><sup>9</sup></xref> The dearth of drug combination datasets is due to the large combinatorial space of possible experiments available—ultimately limiting the quality of drug synergy predictions. Moreover, databases of drug combinations are biased toward suspected synergistic agents, and thus making predictions outside the scope of the training dataset can be challenging.</p><p id="p0060">The goal of this work is to discover synergistic drug combinations while only requiring minimal wet-lab experimentation. One cost-efficient tool at our disposal is sequential model optimization (SMO), whereby a machine learning (ML) model selects experiments (i.e., pairs of drugs) that it would like to be evaluated (in this case, for drug synergism). Both highly informative experiments (“exploration”) and experiments that double down on promising data-driven hypotheses (“exploitation”) can be selected.<xref rid="bib10" ref-type="bibr"><sup>10</sup></xref> Between rounds of experimental evaluation, the model is iteratively adapted to new observations (via model training), which allows performance to gradually improve. This SMO process allows for queries that are more and more enriched with highly synergistic combinations, ultimately leading to reduced experimentation when compared to an exhaustive search.</p><p id="p0065">There have now been a number of approaches for predicting the effects of and subsequently prioritizing drug combinations.<xref rid="bib11" ref-type="bibr"><sup>11</sup></xref> Classic bioinformatics approaches have focused on using ML and network statistics over specified features of drugs (e.g., molecular fingerprints<xref rid="bib12" ref-type="bibr"><sup>12</sup></xref>), cell lines (e.g., transcriptomics, copy-number variations<xref rid="bib13" ref-type="bibr"><sup>13</sup></xref>), and interactome topology between biomolecules (e.g., protein-protein interactions, chemical-genetic interactions,<xref rid="bib14" ref-type="bibr"><sup>14</sup></xref> or gene regulatory networks<xref rid="bib15" ref-type="bibr"><sup>15</sup></xref>). Initiatives such as the Dialogue on Reverse Engineering Assessment and Methods (DREAM) have led to a plethora of methods being benchmarked against one another in prospective challenges through the generation of novel datasets.<xref rid="bib16" ref-type="bibr"><sup>16</sup></xref> Complex deep learning architectures, which have set state-of-the-art performance across a number of domains,<xref rid="bib17" ref-type="bibr"><sup>17</sup></xref> have been used to predict both adverse drug-drug interactions<xref rid="bib18" ref-type="bibr"><sup>18</sup></xref><sup>,</sup><xref rid="bib19" ref-type="bibr"><sup>19</sup></xref> and synergistic drug combinations.<xref rid="bib20" ref-type="bibr"><sup>20</sup></xref><sup>,</sup><xref rid="bib21" ref-type="bibr"><sup>21</sup></xref><sup>,</sup><xref rid="bib22" ref-type="bibr"><sup>22</sup></xref> Sequential approaches, wherein several rounds of selection are performed, have also been explored in the context of drug combinations; for example, Kashif et al.<xref rid="bib23" ref-type="bibr"><sup>23</sup></xref> have proposed a heuristic-based (as opposed to a model-based) exploration strategy.</p><p id="p0070">We present a SMO platform that can guide wet-lab experiments: RECOVER, a deep learning regression model that predicts synergy using molecular fingerprints as inputs. To motivate the use of RECOVER, we demonstrate a real-world use case whereby one observes both: a <inline-formula><mml:math xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="M8" altimg="si1.gif"><mml:mrow><mml:mo linebreak="goodbreak" linebreakstyle="after">∼</mml:mo></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula>5–10<inline-formula><mml:math xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="M9" altimg="si2.gif"><mml:mrow><mml:mo linebreak="goodbreak" linebreakstyle="after">×</mml:mo></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> estimate for the enrichment of synergistic drugs identified using SMO when compared with selecting drug combinations at random and a <inline-formula><mml:math xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="M10" altimg="si1.gif"><mml:mrow><mml:mo linebreak="goodbreak" linebreakstyle="after">∼</mml:mo></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula>3<inline-formula><mml:math xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="M11" altimg="si2.gif"><mml:mrow><mml:mo linebreak="goodbreak" linebreakstyle="after">×</mml:mo></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> improvement when compared with selecting drugs in a single batch using a pretrained model. We then perform a retrospective validation to benchmark the performance of our model and understand its generalization abilities using the DrugComb database—largely pertaining to cancer cell line data.<xref rid="bib24" ref-type="bibr"><sup>24</sup></xref> Thereafter, we evaluate our SMO pipeline <italic>in silico</italic>, which allows the model to select the most relevant data points to be labeled in order to discover the most promising combinations while reducing model uncertainty. Finally, we test RECOVER prospectively in an <italic>in vitro</italic> experimental setting, whereby we discover novel synergistic combinations active against a breast cancer model cell line, MCF7, which is also represented within our training dataset.</p><p id="p0075">With an SMO platform available in conjunction with an appropriate <italic>in vitro</italic> assay, one has a powerful tool to rapidly respond to a future public health crisis. To encourage use by the scientific community, we detail a configuration that can be trained on a personal computer or laptop without requiring dedicated computational infrastructure. Remarkably, high predictive power is not a prerequisite for such an SMO system to be utilized effectively. In fact, as we are trying to identify pairs of drugs in prospective experiments that have more extreme synergy scores than those drug combinations evaluated within previous experiments (i.e., our training dataset), we cannot necessarily expect to have high predictive power. However, we achieve our ultimate goal: the identification of highly synergistic drugs—not building highly accurate ML models. This work forms a proof-of-concept demonstration of RECOVER—which should then motivate greater community adoption of the method and extensions thereof.</p></sec><sec id="sec2" disp-level="1"><title>Results</title><sec id="sec2.1" disp-level="2"><title>RECOVER: SMO platform for rapid drug repurposing</title><p id="p0080">RECOVER is an open-source SMO platform for the optimal suggestion of drug combinations (see <xref rid="fig1" ref-type="fig">Figure 1</xref>). Unfortunately, different laboratories use different dose intervals for each drug, and typically, each drug combination shows a synergistic effect at a specific dose-pair interval. Therefore, the arithmetic mean is highly sensitive to the chosen dose interval and is thus why we choose to prioritize a max-pooling strategy as in <xref rid="fd2" ref-type="disp-formula">Equation 2</xref>. Unless explicitly stated otherwise, a synergy score refers to a global max-pooled Bliss score.</p><p id="p0090">In addition to the prediction of synergy, RECOVER estimates the uncertainty associated with the underlying prediction. More precisely, for a given combination of drugs, RECOVER not only provides a point estimate of the synergy but estimates the distribution of possible synergy scores for each combination, which we refer to as the predictive distribution. We define the model uncertainty as the standard deviation of the predictive distribution.</p><p id="p0095">An acquisition function is used to select the combinations that should be tested in subsequent experiments.<xref rid="bib25" ref-type="bibr"><sup>25</sup></xref> This acquisition function is designed to balance between <italic>exploration</italic>, prioritizing combinations with high model uncertainty, whereby labeling said points should increase predictive accuracy in future experimental rounds; and <italic>exploitation</italic>, the selection of combinations believed to be synergistic with high confidence.</p><p id="p0100">In summary, this SMO setting consists of generating recommendations of drug combinations that will be tested <italic>in vitro</italic> at regular intervals. At each step, RECOVER is trained on all the data acquired up to that point, and predictions are made for all combinations that could be hypothetically tested experimentally. The acquisition function is then used to provide recommendations for <italic>in vitro</italic> testing. The results of the experiments are then added to the training data for the next round of experiments, and the whole process repeats itself.</p><sec id="sec2.1.1" disp-level="3"><title>Task variations</title><p id="p0105">We note that there are two separate but related frameworks in which RECOVER can be utilized.</p><p id="p0110">In the preclinical framework, RECOVER can be used to recommend drug combinations expected to be effective within a single specified cell model system: the model is asked to provide synergy predictions from inputs <inline-formula><mml:math xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="M25" altimg="si12.gif"><mml:mrow><mml:mo stretchy="true">(</mml:mo><mml:mrow><mml:msub><mml:mi>d</mml:mi><mml:mn>1</mml:mn></mml:msub><mml:mo>,</mml:mo><mml:msub><mml:mi>d</mml:mi><mml:mn>2</mml:mn></mml:msub></mml:mrow><mml:mo stretchy="true">)</mml:mo></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> for drugs <inline-formula><mml:math xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="M26" altimg="si3.gif"><mml:mrow><mml:msub><mml:mi>d</mml:mi><mml:mn>1</mml:mn></mml:msub></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> and <inline-formula><mml:math xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="M27" altimg="si4.gif"><mml:mrow><mml:msub><mml:mi>d</mml:mi><mml:mn>2</mml:mn></mml:msub></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> and to subsequently provide recommendations in the same format. Due to the severe batch experiments between studies in the public domain, as shown in <xref rid="mmc1" ref-type="supplementary-material">Figure S1</xref>F, models fail to generalize to data coming from a different study, as shown in <xref rid="mmc1" ref-type="supplementary-material">Figure S2</xref>B (study transfer task). As such, should we want to utilize publicly available resources, we will have to incorporate such data intelligently. To this end, we investigated using transfer learning, wherein one trains a model on a large dataset (known as pretraining) and thereafter refines the model on a smaller dataset (known as fine-tuning)—typically with some aspect of the task or the data changed between the two instances. We show that this is possible and beneficial (compared to not leveraging existing data) in an SMO setting between the O’Neil et al.<xref rid="bib31" ref-type="bibr"><sup>31</sup></xref> and NCI-ALMANAC studies (see <xref rid="mmc1" ref-type="supplementary-material">Figure S4</xref>E). Remarkably, even with minimal correlation between studies, we are able to observe the benefits of transfer learning in this scenario. These findings suggest that we use transfer learning within prospective experiments.</p></sec><sec id="sec2.3" disp-level="2"><title>Prospective use of RECOVER enriches for selection of synergistic drug combinations</title><p id="p0180">From the <italic>in silico</italic> results, we now test RECOVER prospectively using a cancer cell model, leveraging publicly available data for pretraining. Using the insights from <xref rid="sec2.2" ref-type="sec">retrospective testing of RECOVER informs the design of future experiments</xref>, the queriable space of drug combinations was designed to include drug pairs where only one compound was already seen by the model during pretraining—with a second compound not seen before. For details about the model used to generate recommendations, see <xref rid="sec4.4.6" ref-type="sec">recommendation generation</xref> in the <xref rid="sec4.4" ref-type="sec">method details</xref>. The MCF7 cell line was used to generate <inline-formula><mml:math xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="M38" altimg="si15.gif"><mml:mrow><mml:mn>6</mml:mn><mml:mo linebreak="goodbreak" linebreakstyle="after">×</mml:mo><mml:mn>6</mml:mn></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> dose-response matrices (see <xref rid="sec4.4.11" ref-type="sec">experimental protocol</xref> for details).</p><p id="p0185">We perform multiple rounds of RECOVER-informed wet-lab experiments and observe sequential improvements in performance. The rounds of experiments are described as follows.</p><list list-type="label" id="ulist0020"><list-item id="u0040"><label>(1)</label><p id="p0190">Calibration. The initial round of experiments was performed to supplement publicly available data with 20 randomly selected unseen drug combinations. Furthermore, we confirmed the previous <italic>in silico</italic> result that we could not predict synergy scores (prior to transfer learning adaptation) through selecting 5 highly synergistic combinations selected by RECOVER. In addition, 5 more drug combinations were selected by a graph neural network (GNN) model in the style of Zitnik et al.<xref rid="bib18" ref-type="bibr"><sup>18</sup></xref> that we did not develop further due to the computational overhead. It was also specified that each drug should appear in, at most, a single drug combination queried.</p></list-item><list-item id="u0045"><label>(2)</label><p id="p0195">Diversity. Thereafter, drug combinations are selected using model predictions in conjunction with the upper confidence bound (UCB) acquisition function. To ensure that we quickly observe all single drugs at least once (as we showed that the model cannot generalize well to combinations involving unseen drugs), we select our batch of experiments as follows. First, we rank combinations according to their acquisition function score. We then find the first combination that involves a drug that has not yet been used (or that is involved in one of the combinations from the current batch) and add it to the batch. We repeat this until we have 30 combinations in the batch.</p></list-item><list-item id="u0050"><label>(3)</label><p id="p0200">SMO search. RECOVER is now free to select any drug pairs of interest for testing, with the requirement that any single drug may be selected no more than 5 times (to avoid oversampling and depletion of chemical stock). Three such rounds have been performed in this manner.</p></list-item></list><p id="p0205">The search space was constructed as follows. The NCI-ALMANAC includes 95 unique drugs that were employed in combinations tested on the MCF7 cell line (see gray area in <xref rid="fig4" ref-type="fig">Figure 4</xref>B). We chose to deprioritize drugs without a well-characterized mechanism of action (MoA) to facilitate biological interpretation and validation of the results (see light blue area in <xref rid="fig4" ref-type="fig">Figure 4</xref>B). To achieve this, drugs in NCI-ALMANAC were annotated with known targets extracted from the ChEMBL drug mechanism table: 54 drugs matched with at least one known target were thus selected. An additional 54 drugs were selected by clustering drugs with known MoAs that are included in the DrugComb<xref rid="bib24" ref-type="bibr"><sup>24</sup></xref> database but not in NCI-ALMANAC. Hence, a search space including a total of 2,916 drug combinations was obtained (see the white area in <xref rid="fig4" ref-type="fig">Figure 4</xref>B). From the study transfer task in <xref rid="mmc1" ref-type="supplementary-material">Figure S2</xref>A, we know that this would otherwise be an impossible task and thus motivates the calibration round. After the calibration round, one expects that the systematic biases learned by the model during pretraining are minimized. At this point, the model is in a scenario akin to task (ii.) in <xref rid="fig3" ref-type="fig">Figure 3</xref>A. From our prospective use of RECOVER, we not only discover highly synergistic drug combinations but also demonstrate that high predictive power is not strictly necessary to identify synergistic drug combinations.</p></sec><sec id="sec2.4" disp-level="2"><title>Discovery and rediscovery of novel synergistic drug combinations</title><p id="p0230">In <xref rid="mmc1" ref-type="supplementary-material">Data S1</xref>, we provide detailed information on our experimental results using the Combenefit package<xref rid="bib35" ref-type="bibr"><sup>35</sup></xref> (including single-agent dose-response curves, combination dose-response surfaces, and synergy levels) for the 14 most synergistic drug combinations (from the <inline-formula><mml:math xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="M45" altimg="si1.gif"><mml:mrow><mml:mo linebreak="goodbreak" linebreakstyle="after">∼</mml:mo></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula>150 tested), with alfacalcidol and crizotinib achieving a max Bliss score above 90. Of note, we rapidly discover drug combinations with similar mechanisms and efficacy to those already in clinical trials. Namely, within the first SMO search round we found (1) alisertib and pazopanib and (2) flumatinib and mitoxantrone. The concentration intervals for the drugs used in both drug combinations that show synergy are consistent with therapeutically relevant plasma concentrations<xref rid="bib36" ref-type="bibr"><sup>36</sup></xref><sup>,</sup><xref rid="bib37" ref-type="bibr"><sup>37</sup></xref> or as observed in <italic>in vivo</italic> animal experiments (flumatinib).<xref rid="bib38" ref-type="bibr"><sup>38</sup></xref></p><p id="p0235">Pazopanib inhibits angiogenesis through targeting a range of kinases including vascular endothelial growth factor receptor (VEGFR), platelet-derived growth factor receptor (PDGFR), c-KIT, and fibroblast growth factor receptors (FGFRs); in contrast, alisertib is a highly selective inhibitor of mitotic Aurora A kinase. In an independent study, the combination of alisertib and pazopanib has successfully completed phase 1b clinical trials for advanced solid tumors.<xref rid="bib36" ref-type="bibr"><sup>36</sup></xref> The combination of flumatinib and mitoxantrone appears to be linked to a similar mechanism but does not seem to have been studied in the biomedical literature. To this end, we have presented the SMO toolbox RECOVER for drug combination identification. We have demonstrated its ability to generalize to combinations involving one unseen drug, and crucially, we have shown the benefit of repeated experimentation via an integrated wet-lab/dry-lab system. We showcase a general methodology, consisting of careful analysis of the properties of our ML pipeline—such as its out-of-distribution generalization capacities—to help us design key aspects of our prospective experiments, to eventually ensure a smooth and successful interaction between the SMO pipeline and the wet lab. Highly synergistic drug combinations have been identified, and the resulting learned embeddings appear to capture both structural and biological information. RECOVER can quickly (in our prospective experiments: &lt;5% of the total search space evaluated) identify patterns in the drug-drug landscape of synergies, in order to provide recommendations significantly enriched for synergism and alleviate the need for exhaustive studies. We provide commentary on key aspects on our approach covering datasets, computational methodology, wet-lab techniques, and evaluation metrics.</p><p id="p0255">We note the considerable difficulties of working with publicly available datasets with discrepancies in the data generation process. Inconsistent media between multiple labs, the presence of <italic>de novo</italic> mutations within immortalized <italic>in vitro</italic> cell models, and differences in experimental protocols limit ease of data integration between laboratories.<xref rid="bib41" ref-type="bibr"><sup>41</sup></xref> In particular, systematic biases limit generalizability of model predictions to subsequent prospective experiments. Within oncology, protein-coding mutations may drive resistance to any one chemotherapeutic agent but also large-scale gene dosing changes from non-coding mutations,<xref rid="bib42" ref-type="bibr"><sup>42</sup></xref> copy-number variations,<xref rid="bib43" ref-type="bibr"><sup>43</sup></xref> and aneuploidy.<xref rid="bib44" ref-type="bibr"><sup>44</sup></xref> These issues have been somewhat alleviated through careful choice of metric to optimize (e.g., max pooled Bliss synergy scores have reduced sensitivity to selected drug concentration ranges, compared to averaged scores) and only using publicly available data for pretraining (when compared with using these data for prediction without adaptation).</p><p id="p0260">From a computational perspective, we experimented with a range of more complicated models. For example, we considered using GNNs to model biomolecular interactions,<xref rid="bib45" ref-type="bibr"><sup>45</sup></xref> which have numerous benefits including greater biological interpretability and incorporation of prior knowledge, namely drug-target and protein-protein interactions. However, these models only resulted in marginal increases in performance while requiring substantially more computational resources. We believe that the limited diversity of the dataset and the simplicity of the task, a one-dimensional regression, did not allow these more advanced approaches to reach their full potential. Therefore, we prioritized a strategy that could be run quickly for rapid turnaround of recommendations for experimental testing.</p><p id="p0265">When considering an SMO setting, we are required to collapse highly complex information into a single number to be optimized (i.e., a synergy score). While there is an opportunity to improve choices of metric (synergy scores may not reflect absolute cell viability), assay readouts that better characterize cell state (compared with cell viability) may provide a stronger starting point. In particular, an omics readout, through transcriptomics<xref rid="bib46" ref-type="bibr"><sup>46</sup></xref> and/or single-cell profiling,<xref rid="bib47" ref-type="bibr"><sup>47</sup></xref><sup>,</sup><xref rid="bib48" ref-type="bibr"><sup>48</sup></xref> and high content imaging<xref rid="bib49" ref-type="bibr"><sup>49</sup></xref> provide a much higher-dimensional measurement of cell state. Furthermore, derived properties from these readouts may be more interpretable, e.g., pathway activation<xref rid="bib50" ref-type="bibr"><sup>50</sup></xref> or extracellular signaling.<xref rid="bib51" ref-type="bibr"><sup>51</sup></xref> Remarkably, even while only using cell viability as a readout, we achieved significant progress in identifying novel synergistic drug combinations.</p><p id="p0270">Furthermore, the usual metrics for the evaluation and training of regression models may not reflect well the efficiency of models in iterative settings. This is due to the fact that, in our SMO setting, only the prediction of extreme values is important. This work provides an example of this effect: model performance on prospectively queried combinations was modest, but a substantial enrichment was achieved. Some metrics have been proposed to focus specifically on the prediction of extreme values.<xref rid="bib52" ref-type="bibr"><sup>52</sup></xref> Developing training objectives that specifically aim at maximizing SMO performance will be the object of future work.</p><p id="p0275">From the systematic screen by Jaak et al.,<xref rid="bib33" ref-type="bibr"><sup>33</sup></xref> they conclude that synergy between drugs is rare and highly context dependent. RECOVER provides a means to identify such synergies while requiring substantially less screening than an exhaustive evaluation; thus, we expect that RECOVER and similar such systems may have a role to play when addressing diverse application areas such as personalized cancer treatment and novel emergent infectious disease such as the COVID-19 pandemic.</p><sec id="sec3.1" disp-level="2"><title>Limitations of the study</title><p id="p0280">In addition to the points mentioned above, a few restrictions were necessary in the name of feasibility concerning the validation experiments. In particular, only one cell model was used for validation, and the exhaustive evaluation of every possible drug combination was not performed. With regard to the downstream analysis, while we investigated the relationship between drugs and their mechanisms of action, many such mechanisms are not fully elucidated. Finally, our investigation into the relationship between the structural similarity of drug pairs, their synergy, the associated model error, and model uncertainty is preliminary in nature.</p></sec></sec><sec id="sec4" disp-level="1"><title>STAR★Methods</title><sec id="sec4.1" disp-level="2"><title>Key resources table</title>

Identifiers are listed in the <xref rid="sec4.1" ref-type="sec">Key resources table</xref>.</p></sec><sec id="sec4.4" disp-level="2"><title>Method details</title><sec id="sec4.4.1" disp-level="3"><title>Model description</title><p id="p0305">We frame the problem of pairwise drug synergy prediction as a regression task <inline-formula><mml:math xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="M47" altimg="si22.gif"><mml:mrow><mml:mo stretchy="true">(</mml:mo><mml:mrow><mml:mrow><mml:mo stretchy="true">{</mml:mo><mml:mrow><mml:msub><mml:mi>d</mml:mi><mml:mn>1</mml:mn></mml:msub><mml:mo>,</mml:mo><mml:msub><mml:mi>d</mml:mi><mml:mn>2</mml:mn></mml:msub></mml:mrow><mml:mo stretchy="true">}</mml:mo></mml:mrow><mml:mo>,</mml:mo><mml:mover accent="true"><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">s</mml:mi><mml:mo>ˆ</mml:mo></mml:mover></mml:mrow><mml:mo stretchy="true">)</mml:mo></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula>: given a pair of drugs <inline-formula><mml:math xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="M48" altimg="si23.gif"><mml:mrow><mml:msub><mml:mi>d</mml:mi><mml:mn>1</mml:mn></mml:msub><mml:mo>,</mml:mo><mml:msub><mml:mi>d</mml:mi><mml:mn>2</mml:mn></mml:msub></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula>, we aim to predict their (pooled) level of synergy, <inline-formula><mml:math xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="M49" altimg="si24.gif"><mml:mrow><mml:mover accent="true"><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">s</mml:mi><mml:mo>ˆ</mml:mo></mml:mover></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula>. Our proposed architecture is an end-to-end deep learning model trained with a <italic>mean square error</italic> (MSE) criterion.</p><p id="p0310">Our model can be decomposed into two modules. First, a <italic>single drug</italic> module, <italic>E</italic>, which produces representations (or embeddings) for the drugs based on their chemical structure information. The embeddings from a pair of drugs are used as input to the <italic>combination</italic> module <italic>P</italic>, which directly estimates the synergy score; see <xref rid="mmc1" ref-type="supplementary-material">Figure S4</xref>A.</p><p id="p0315">Further, uncertainty estimation methods are used in order to estimate the <italic>predictive distribution</italic> of synergies <inline-formula><mml:math xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="M50" altimg="si25.gif"><mml:mrow><mml:mi>p</mml:mi><mml:mrow><mml:mo stretchy="true">(</mml:mo><mml:mrow><mml:mover accent="true"><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">s</mml:mi><mml:mo>ˆ</mml:mo></mml:mover><mml:mo>|</mml:mo><mml:mrow><mml:mo stretchy="true">{</mml:mo><mml:mrow><mml:msub><mml:mi>d</mml:mi><mml:mn>1</mml:mn></mml:msub><mml:mo>,</mml:mo><mml:msub><mml:mi>d</mml:mi><mml:mn>2</mml:mn></mml:msub></mml:mrow><mml:mo stretchy="true">}</mml:mo></mml:mrow></mml:mrow><mml:mo stretchy="true">)</mml:mo></mml:mrow></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> for each drug pair <inline-formula><mml:math xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="M51" altimg="si26.gif"><mml:mrow><mml:mo stretchy="true">{</mml:mo><mml:mrow><mml:msub><mml:mi>d</mml:mi><mml:mn>1</mml:mn></mml:msub><mml:mo>,</mml:mo><mml:msub><mml:mi>d</mml:mi><mml:mn>2</mml:mn></mml:msub></mml:mrow><mml:mo stretchy="true">}</mml:mo></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula>, as opposed to a point estimate. The predictive distributions of drug pairs are given as input to an acquisition function in order to decide which combinations should be tested <italic>in vitro</italic>, balancing between combinations that are informative, i.e., that can reduce the generalization error of the model later on, and combinations that are likely to be synergistic.</p><sec id="sec4.4.2" disp-level="4"><title>Single drug module</title><p id="p0320">Let <inline-formula><mml:math xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="M52" altimg="si27.gif"><mml:mrow><mml:msub><mml:mi>X</mml:mi><mml:mi mathvariant="script">D</mml:mi></mml:msub><mml:mo linebreak="goodbreak" linebreakstyle="after">∈</mml:mo><mml:msup><mml:mi mathvariant="double-struck">R</mml:mi><mml:mrow><mml:msub><mml:mi>n</mml:mi><mml:mi mathvariant="script">D</mml:mi></mml:msub><mml:mo>×</mml:mo><mml:msub><mml:mi>l</mml:mi><mml:mi mathvariant="script">D</mml:mi></mml:msub></mml:mrow></mml:msup></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> denote the matrix of drug features, where <inline-formula><mml:math xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="M53" altimg="si28.gif"><mml:mrow><mml:msub><mml:mi>n</mml:mi><mml:mi mathvariant="script">D</mml:mi></mml:msub></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> is the number of drugs in <inline-formula><mml:math xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="M54" altimg="si29.gif"><mml:mrow><mml:mi mathvariant="script">D</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> and <inline-formula><mml:math xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="M55" altimg="si30.gif"><mml:mrow><mml:msub><mml:mi>l</mml:mi><mml:mi mathvariant="script">D</mml:mi></mml:msub></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> corresponds to the number of raw features that describe each drug. Drug features used in this work include molecular fingerprints<xref rid="bib12" ref-type="bibr"><sup>12</sup></xref> and one-hot encoding of the drugs.</p><p id="p0325">The single drug module can be written as a function <inline-formula><mml:math xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="M56" altimg="si31.gif"><mml:mrow><mml:mi>E</mml:mi><mml:mo>:</mml:mo><mml:mi mathvariant="script">D</mml:mi><mml:mo linebreak="goodbreak" linebreakstyle="after" stretchy="true">→</mml:mo><mml:msup><mml:mi mathvariant="double-struck">R</mml:mi><mml:msub><mml:mi>k</mml:mi><mml:mi mathvariant="script">D</mml:mi></mml:msub></mml:msup></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> where <inline-formula><mml:math xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="M57" altimg="si32.gif"><mml:mrow><mml:msub><mml:mi>k</mml:mi><mml:mi mathvariant="script">D</mml:mi></mml:msub></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> corresponds to the dimension of the output vector representation (or embedding) of each drug. Our single drug module is a simple multi-layer perceptron (MLP) that takes raw features of drugs as input and outputs an updated vector representation; this MLP can be conditioned on cell line (described below).</p></sec><sec id="sec4.4.3" disp-level="4"><title>Combination module</title><p id="p0330">Given a set of drugs <inline-formula><mml:math xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="M58" altimg="si29.gif"><mml:mrow><mml:mi mathvariant="script">D</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula>, the combination module corresponds to a function <inline-formula><mml:math xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="M59" altimg="si33.gif"><mml:mrow><mml:mi>P</mml:mi><mml:mo>:</mml:mo><mml:mrow><mml:mo stretchy="true">(</mml:mo><mml:mtable columnalign="left"><mml:mtr><mml:mtd><mml:mi mathvariant="script">D</mml:mi></mml:mtd></mml:mtr><mml:mtr><mml:mtd><mml:mn>2</mml:mn></mml:mtd></mml:mtr></mml:mtable><mml:mo stretchy="true">)</mml:mo></mml:mrow><mml:mo>↦</mml:mo><mml:mi mathvariant="double-struck">R</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> that maps a pair of drugs to their Bliss synergy score. We remark first that <italic>P</italic> should be agnostic to the order of the two drugs. Hence, the first operation of <italic>P</italic> correspond to a permutation invariant function – such as element-wise sum, mean, or max operations – applied to the two vector representations corresponding to each drug. In this work, we use a bilinear operation defined by a tensor <inline-formula><mml:math xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="M60" altimg="si34.gif"><mml:mrow><mml:mi>B</mml:mi><mml:mo linebreak="goodbreak" linebreakstyle="after">∈</mml:mo><mml:msup><mml:mi mathvariant="double-struck">R</mml:mi><mml:mrow><mml:msub><mml:mi>k</mml:mi><mml:mi mathvariant="script">D</mml:mi></mml:msub><mml:mo>×</mml:mo><mml:msub><mml:mi>k</mml:mi><mml:mi mathvariant="script">D</mml:mi></mml:msub><mml:mo>×</mml:mo><mml:mi>k</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:msup></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula>, where <italic>k</italic> is a hyperparameter corresponding to the dimension of the vector representation of a drug combination. To ensure permutation invariance, we enforce that every slice across the third dimension (denoted as <inline-formula><mml:math xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="M61" altimg="si35.gif"><mml:mrow><mml:msub><mml:mi>B</mml:mi><mml:mi>i</mml:mi></mml:msub></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula>), is a symmetric matrix. Note that we do not enforce <inline-formula><mml:math xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="M62" altimg="si35.gif"><mml:mrow><mml:msub><mml:mi>B</mml:mi><mml:mi>i</mml:mi></mml:msub></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> to be positive definite, hence <inline-formula><mml:math xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="M63" altimg="si35.gif"><mml:mrow><mml:msub><mml:mi>B</mml:mi><mml:mi>i</mml:mi></mml:msub></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> does not necessarily define a scalar product. The output of this permutation invariant function is fed to an MLP that outputs the predicted synergy for the pair of drugs; as before, the MLP can be conditioned on cell lines.</p></sec><sec id="sec4.4.4" disp-level="4"><title>Cell line conditioning</title><p id="p0335">As a drug effect is context dependent, the synergy of a combination of two drugs can be different in experiments using different cell lines. To account for the cell line in our model we condition upon it using FiLM.<xref rid="bib53" ref-type="bibr"><sup>53</sup></xref> In essence, the FiLM approach learns an affine transformation of the activation of each neuron in the MLP.</p><p id="p0340">We denote the matrix of cell line features by <inline-formula><mml:math xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="M64" altimg="si36.gif"><mml:mrow><mml:msub><mml:mi>X</mml:mi><mml:mi mathvariant="script">C</mml:mi></mml:msub><mml:mo linebreak="goodbreak" linebreakstyle="after">∈</mml:mo><mml:msup><mml:mi mathvariant="double-struck">R</mml:mi><mml:mrow><mml:msub><mml:mi>n</mml:mi><mml:mi mathvariant="script">C</mml:mi></mml:msub><mml:mo>×</mml:mo><mml:msub><mml:mi>l</mml:mi><mml:mi mathvariant="script">C</mml:mi></mml:msub></mml:mrow></mml:msup></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula>, with <inline-formula><mml:math xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="M65" altimg="si37.gif"><mml:mrow><mml:mi mathvariant="script">C</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> the set of cell lines, <inline-formula><mml:math xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="M66" altimg="si38.gif"><mml:mrow><mml:msub><mml:mi>n</mml:mi><mml:mi mathvariant="script">C</mml:mi></mml:msub></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> corresponding to the number of cell lines in <inline-formula><mml:math xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="M67" altimg="si37.gif"><mml:mrow><mml:mi mathvariant="script">C</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> and <inline-formula><mml:math xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="M68" altimg="si39.gif"><mml:mrow><mml:msub><mml:mi>l</mml:mi><mml:mi mathvariant="script">C</mml:mi></mml:msub></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> giving the number of raw features for each cell line.</p><p id="p0345">The feature representation of the cell line is either based on a one-hot encoding, or on information about mutations and basal level of gene expression. The former approach relies on having data for each cell line in the training set and cannot generalise to new cell lines; the latter approach makes use of features that represent cell lines.</p></sec></sec><sec id="sec4.4.5" disp-level="3"><title>Searching the space of drug combinations</title><sec id="sec4.4.5.1" disp-level="4"><title>Uncertainty estimation</title><p id="p0350">Estimating the uncertainty of the predictions is a key step toward providing reliable recommendations as well as driving the exploration with SMO. For this purpose, we use a common uncertainty estimation method: deep ensembles.<xref rid="bib54" ref-type="bibr"><sup>54</sup></xref> Given an ensemble of models which differ only in the initialization of the parameters, the predictions of the different models are considered as samples from the <italic>predictive</italic> distribution. In this work, we define uncertainty as the standard deviation of the <italic>predictive</italic> distribution, and can be estimated from the standard deviation between the predictions of the different members of the ensemble. Unless specified otherwise, we use a deep ensemble of size 5 as the uncertainty estimation method in our <italic>in silico</italic> experiments, and of size 36 for generation of wet lab recommendations.</p><p id="p0355">Note that for completeness, we investigated other methods for uncertainty quantification in some of the <italic>in silico</italic> experiments, including direct estimation of the standard deviation of the predictive distribution — in a similar fashion to Direct Epistemic Uncertainty Prediction (DEUP),<xref rid="bib55" ref-type="bibr"><sup>55</sup></xref> see SMO development and evaluation for details.</p></sec><sec id="sec4.4.5.2" disp-level="4"><title>Sequential model optimization</title><p id="p0360">Sequential model optimization (SMO) aims at discovering an input <inline-formula><mml:math xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="M69" altimg="si40.gif"><mml:mrow><mml:msup><mml:mi>x</mml:mi><mml:mo>⋆</mml:mo></mml:msup><mml:mo linebreak="goodbreak" linebreakstyle="after">∈</mml:mo><mml:mi mathvariant="script">X</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> maximizing an objective function <italic>S</italic>:</p><disp-formula id="fd3"><label>(Equation 3)</label><mml:math xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="M70" display="block" altimg="si41.gif"><mml:mrow><mml:msup><mml:mi>x</mml:mi><mml:mo>⋆</mml:mo></mml:msup><mml:mo linebreak="goodbreak">∈</mml:mo><mml:munder><mml:mrow><mml:mi>arg</mml:mi><mml:mspace In the context of drug combinations, <italic>x</italic> corresponds to a pair of drugs, and the objective function <italic>S</italic> corresponds to the synergy score.</p><p id="p0370">SMO has been prospectively applied to: optimize the production of proteins in cell free systems<xref rid="bib56" ref-type="bibr"><sup>56</sup></xref>; determine gene functions in yeast<xref rid="bib57" ref-type="bibr"><sup>57</sup></xref>; enhance the production of fine chemicals in <italic>Escherichia coli</italic><xref rid="bib58" ref-type="bibr"><sup>58</sup></xref>; and to identify inhibitors of <italic>Mycobacterium tuberculosis</italic> growth.<xref rid="bib59" ref-type="bibr"><sup>59</sup></xref></p><p id="p0375">In what follows, <italic>f</italic> refers to an estimator of the objective function <italic>S</italic>. One may notice that several properties of the potential queries <inline-formula><mml:math xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="M75" altimg="si45.gif"><mml:mrow><mml:msup><mml:mi>x</mml:mi><mml:mrow><mml:mo>(</mml:mo><mml:mi>t</mml:mi><mml:mo>)</mml:mo></mml:mrow></mml:msup></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> should be taken into account. Looking for queries which are informative is referred to as <italic>exploration</italic> while looking for queries which are expected to maximize the objective function is called <italic>exploitation</italic>.</p><p id="p0380">The key challenge of SMO is to balance between exploration and exploitation. Whilst not designed for optimal computational efficiency, this procedure ensures that the model is not overfitting on examples that have been acquired early on.</p></sec></sec></sec><sec id="sec4.4.6" disp-level="3"><title>Recommendation generation</title><p id="p0430">In order to generate the recommendations for <italic>in vitro</italic> experiments, we trained 3 models using 3 different seeds on the NCI-ALMANAC study, restricting ourselves to samples from the MCF7 cell line. We refer to these 3 models as pretrained.</p><p id="p0435">Afterward, we <italic>fine-tune</italic> using prospectively generated data. More precisely, the weights of one of the pretrained models were loaded, and some additional training was performed on prospectively generated data only, using early stopping. This fine-tuning process was repeated with 12 different seeds for each pretrained model. The end result being that we obtain an ensemble of 36 fine-tuned models in total.</p><p id="p0440">This ensemble was used to generate predictions <inline-formula><mml:math xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="M97" altimg="si63.gif"><mml:mrow><mml:mo stretchy="true">(</mml:mo><mml:mrow><mml:mover accent="true"><mml:mi>μ</mml:mi><mml:mo>ˆ</mml:mo></mml:mover><mml:mo>,</mml:mo><mml:mover accent="true"><mml:mi>σ</mml:mi><mml:mo>ˆ</mml:mo></mml:mover></mml:mrow><mml:mo stretchy="true">)</mml:mo></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> for all candidate combinations. We then use UCB with <inline-formula><mml:math xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="M98" altimg="si62.gif"><mml:mrow><mml:mi>κ</mml:mi><mml:mo linebreak="goodbreak" linebreakstyle="after">=</mml:mo><mml:mn>1</mml:mn></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> to obtain a score according to which all candidates were ranked.</p></sec><sec id="sec4.4.7" disp-level="3"><title>Dataset processing</title><p id="p0445">Below, the major data types included in the RESERVOIR data repository are briefly described.</p><sec id="sec4.4.8" disp-level="4"><title>Drugs</title><p id="p0450">Data on drugs and biologically active compounds has been extracted from Chembl,<xref rid="bib60" ref-type="bibr"><sup>60</sup></xref> pre-processed and indexed with unique identifiers. A translation engine has been provided such that a compound can be translated to a unique identifier using generic or brand drug names, SMILES strings and Pubchem<xref rid="bib61" ref-type="bibr"><sup>61</sup></xref> CIDs.</p></sec><sec id="sec4.4.9" disp-level="4"><title>Cell line features</title><p id="p0455">Additionally, RESERVOIR retrieved cell line features from the Cancer Dependency Map.<xref rid="bib62" ref-type="bibr"><sup>62</sup></xref> These include genetic mutations, base level gene expression and metadata.<xref rid="bib24" ref-type="bibr"><sup>24</sup></xref></p></sec><sec id="sec4.4.10" disp-level="4"><title>Drug combinations</title><p id="p0460">Literature drug combination data was extracted from DrugComb version 1.5.<xref rid="bib24" ref-type="bibr"><sup>24</sup></xref> Quality control was applied on the experiments in DrugComb. Only blocks (i.e., combination matrices) complying to the following criteria were selected: (a.) filter out erroneous blocks that show very low variance, specifically inhibition standard deviation <inline-formula><mml:math xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="M99" altimg="si64.gif"><mml:mrow><mml:mo linebreak="goodbreak" linebreakstyle="after">≤</mml:mo></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> 0.05, (b.) filter out small blocks less than 3 <inline-formula><mml:math xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="M100" altimg="si2.gif"><mml:mrow><mml:mo linebreak="goodbreak" linebreakstyle="after">×</mml:mo></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> 3 dimensions, (c.) filter out blocks with extreme inhibition values, such that 5% <inline-formula><mml:math xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="M101" altimg="si65.gif"><mml:mrow><mml:mo linebreak="goodbreak" linebreakstyle="after">&lt;</mml:mo></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> [mean pooled growth inhibition] <inline-formula><mml:math xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="M102" altimg="si65.gif"><mml:mrow><mml:mo linebreak="goodbreak" linebreakstyle="after">&lt;</mml:mo></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> 95%.</p><p id="p0465">The dataset used for model pretraining and <italic>in silico</italic> experiments consists of 4463 data points relative to experiments on MCF7 cell line expressed as max Bliss which were reported in the Almanac study. These data correspond to 4271 unique drug combinations made up by 95 unique drugs.</p><p id="p0470">The prediction set for experiment selection was built by taking 54 out of the 95 Almanac drugs for which a mechanism of action (MoA) was annotated in ChEMBL 25.<xref rid="bib60" ref-type="bibr"><sup>60</sup></xref> An additional 54 drugs were obtained by clustering 719 drugs with known MoA that are included in DrugComb but are not part of Almanac. Clustering was performed with the <italic>k</italic>-medoids algorithm as implemented in scikit-learn 0.24.2<xref rid="bib63" ref-type="bibr"><sup>63</sup></xref> (n_clusters = 54, metric = Tanimoto similarity, init = k-medoids++), drugs were encoded by Morgan fingerprints with radius 2 and 1024 bits calculated with RDKit.<xref rid="bib64" ref-type="bibr"><sup>64</sup></xref> A representative compound for each cluster was obtained by taking the cluster centroid.</p><p id="p0475">Three of the centroid drugs were replaced due to lack of availability from commercial vendors or due to poor reported solubility. Replacements for each of the three drugs were selected by taking the nearest analogue (evaluated by Tanimoto similarity) in the same cluster. 54 Almanac and 54 non-Almanac compounds thus selected were used to build a set of 2916 binary combinations made up by one Almanac and one non-Almanac compound.</p></sec></sec><sec id="sec4.4.11" disp-level="3"><title>Experimental protocol</title><p id="p0480">Results for all prospective experiments are included in <xref rid="mmc2" ref-type="supplementary-material">Table S2</xref>.</p><p id="p0485">Compounds were plated as a 6 <inline-formula><mml:math xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="M103" altimg="si2.gif"><mml:mrow><mml:mo linebreak="goodbreak" linebreakstyle="after">×</mml:mo></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> 6 dose-response combination matrix in natural 384 well plates (Greiner), in a serial 1:3 dilutions of each agent (5 concentrations) and only DMSO as the lowest concentration. We used a combination plate layout where six compound pairs could be accommodated on one 384 well plate. A set of control wells with DMSO was included on all plates as negative control. To ensure reproducibility and comparability with the subsequent combination studies, the IC50 of Doxorubicin was used as reference in a 6-point dose response format in each plate as positive (total killing) control. In addition, alfacalcidol and erlotinib were evaluated in multiple rounds (and excluded from our analysis) to ensure consistency in max Bliss synergy scores.</p><p id="p0490">Cells were seeded in white 384-well plates (Greiner) at 1000 cells/well in 50 μL of media using a multidrop dispenser and allowed to attach for 2 h. Compounds from pre-plated matrix plates were transferred to each well using a 100 nL head affixed to an Agilent Bravo automated liquid handling platform, and plates were incubated at 37°C in 5% CO2 for an additional 72 h. To measure the cell viability, CellTiter-Glo reagent (diluted 1:6 in water, Promega) was dispensed into the wells (30 μL), incubated for 3 min, and luminescence was read on a Envision plate reader (PerkinElmer). Final DMSO concentration in assay wells was 0.2%. The assay was performed with 3 biological replicates.</p><p id="p0495">The compound-specific concentration ranges were selected based on their published activities. In brief, dose ranges for each drug were selected based on the highest quality evidence available pertaining to the drug in question. Highest to lowest quality evidence sources were as follows.</p><list list-type="label" id="olist0010"><list-item id="o0010"><label>1.</label><p id="p0500">IC50/range of concentrations available from DrugComb<xref rid="bib24" ref-type="bibr"><sup>24</sup></xref> available in correct cell line (MCF7).</p></list-item><list-item id="o0015"><label>2.</label><p id="p0505">IC50/range of concentrations available for same cell line from any literature source.</p></list-item><list-item id="o0020"><label>3.</label><p id="p0510">IC50/range of concentrations available from DrugComb for a range of cell lines. Mean/median calculated and manually curated to assess any obvious difference for related cell lines.</p></list-item><list-item id="o0025"><label>4.</label><p id="p0515">IC50/range of concentrations available from the literature for any cell line.</p></list-item><list-item id="o0030"><label>5.</label><p id="p0520">Looking at prescribed dose in man and comparing with IC50s for similar prescribed doses for compounds in DrugComb.</p></list-item></list><p id="p0525">For experimental ease, standard sets of concentrations were used for different sets of drugs, and IC50 ranges were rounded appropriately. For some compounds there were also limits for solubility which resulted in adjustment. All compounds were pre-diluted in DMSO to a stock concentration that varied from 10 to 50 mM, depending on the final concentration range required for each compound.</p></sec><sec id="sec4.4.13" disp-level="3"><title>Combenefit preprocessing</title><p id="p0530">We report some detailed results regarding the 14 top scoring prospectively tested combinations in <xref rid="mmc1" ref-type="supplementary-material">Data S1</xref>. Results are shown in <xref rid="mmc1" ref-type="supplementary-material">Figure S2</xref>D. We observe that such randomization has no effect on the default task. This confirms that knowing the identity of the drugs is sufficient in that case.</p><p id="p0730">For other tasks, we can see that performance quickly drops when the randomization percentage increases, meaning that the information contained in drug fingerprints was critical to the performance of the model.</p></sec><sec id="sec4.4.17" disp-level="4"><title>Feature importance study</title><p id="p0735">Through investigation of different drug features, we find a large proportion of the performance of RECOVER can be achieved given the identity of the drugs alone, and that structural information allows for a slight increase in performance. As shown in <xref rid="mmc1" ref-type="supplementary-material">Figure S2</xref>G, the performance of the model is similar whether the one hot encoding of the drug or its Morgan fingerprint is used as input. We notice a slight improvement when using both feature types together. Note that the number of parameters of the model is always the same regardless of the type of feature provided as input. When a feature type is not used, the corresponding part of the drug feature vector is set to zero without changing the underlying dimension.</p></sec><sec id="sec4.4.18" disp-level="4"><title>Upper bounds on model performance</title><p id="p0740">We investigate RECOVER performance with regards to Spearman correlation and <inline-formula><mml:math xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="M138" altimg="si86.gif"><mml:mrow><mml:msup><mml:mi>R</mml:mi><mml:mn>2</mml:mn></mml:msup></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula>. Whilst predictive power appears modest, we are still able to identify highly synergistic drug combinations in simulated SMO experiments, see <xref rid="mmc1" ref-type="supplementary-material">Figure S4</xref>D. Several aspects that may limit predictive power: experimental noise, and nonuniformity of maximum Bliss synergy scores.</p><p id="p0745">In <xref rid="mmc1" ref-type="supplementary-material">Figure S1</xref>B, we note most data points are close to zero, with some examples very far from the mean, i.e., the examples of interest. As an example, let us consider the case of Spearman correlation. Given that the observations are noisy, the observed rank among synergies might be corrupted compared to the true ordering — especially in the region close to zero where the density of examples is very high.</p><p id="p0750">The non-uniformity of synergy scores leads to some difficulties in evaluating fairly the performance of RECOVER. For example, the positive tail of the distribution, which is the region of interest, represent a very small percentage of the total number of examples and thus have a little effect on the value of the aggregated statistic.</p><p id="p0755">In order to get a better understanding of the performance of our model, we compare the reported aggregated statistics to an upper bound which takes into account the presence of noise in the observations in addition to the distribution of synergy scores.</p><p id="p0760">We first evaluate the level of noise by considering all replicates from the NCI-ALMANAC study. Two examples are considered replicates when the same pair of drugs has been tested on the same cell line. We found 1960 triplets <inline-formula><mml:math xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="M139" altimg="si13.gif"><mml:mrow><mml:mo stretchy="true">(</mml:mo><mml:mrow><mml:msub><mml:mi>d</mml:mi><mml:mn>1</mml:mn></mml:msub><mml:mo>,</mml:mo><mml:msub><mml:mi>d</mml:mi><mml:mn>2</mml:mn></mml:msub><mml:mo>,</mml:mo><mml:mi>m</mml:mi></mml:mrow><mml:mo stretchy="true">)</mml:mo></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> that had been tested several times. For each triplet, we computed the standard deviation of the maximum Bliss score across the replicates. We refer to this as the level of noise for a given triplet. We then computed the average level of noise <inline-formula><mml:math xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="M140" altimg="si87.gif"><mml:mrow><mml:mover accent="true"><mml:mi>η</mml:mi><mml:mo>¯</mml:mo></mml:mover></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> across all triplets.</p><p id="p0765">We then estimate the upper bounds on performance. Given an average level of noise η and the distribution of synergy scores in NCI-ALMANAC, we simulated a noisy acquisition process as follows: the synergies from NCI-ALMANAC were considered as the <italic>true</italic> synergies, and noisy observations were obtained by corrupting the <italic>true</italic> synergies with some Gaussian noise <inline-formula><mml:math xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="M141" altimg="si88.gif"><mml:mrow><mml:mi mathvariant="script">N</mml:mi><mml:mrow><mml:mo stretchy="true">(</mml:mo><mml:mrow><mml:mn>0</mml:mn><mml:mo>,</mml:mo><mml:msup><mml:mi>η</mml:mi><mml:mn>2</mml:mn></mml:msup></mml:mrow><mml:mo stretchy="true">)</mml:mo></mml:mrow></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula>. We then considered a <italic>perfect</italic> regression model which fits the noisy observations exactly, and evaluated its performance on the <italic>true</italic> synergies. Upper bounds are defined by the performance of this <italic>perfect</italic> regression model.</p><p id="p0770">Upper bounds have been computed for <inline-formula><mml:math xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="M142" altimg="si86.gif"><mml:mrow><mml:msup><mml:mi>R</mml:mi><mml:mn>2</mml:mn></mml:msup></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> and Spearman correlation using various levels of noise and are reported in <xref rid="mmc1" ref-type="supplementary-material">Figure S2</xref>F. We see that the noisy acquisition process alone leads to significant limitations in the performance that can be reached. While there is still room for improvement, the performance of RECOVER is reasonably close to the hypothetical maximum. For example, RECOVER achieves 0.47 Spearman correlation, while the highest achievable Spearman correlation is estimated to be 0.64.</p></sec><sec id="sec4.4.19" disp-level="4"><title>Performance on the tail of the distribution</title><p id="p0775">We now show that RECOVER achieves good performance on the positive tail of the distribution of synergies, which is necessary to successfully identify highly synergistic combinations within an SMO setting.</p><p id="p0780">Given a model trained on NCI-ALMANAC, we query the top <italic>k</italic> combinations with highest predicted synergy within the test set, and compute the percentage of combinations which are truly synergistic (arbitrarily defined by a maximum Bliss synergy score of above 30) within queried examples. We refer to <italic>k</italic> as the size of the query. In <xref rid="mmc1" ref-type="supplementary-material">Figure S2</xref>E, we report the percentage of synergistic combinations as a function of the size of the query. The percentage of synergistic combinations is superior to the proportion in the whole test set, meaning that the model performs far beyond the level of randomness. For instance, with a query size of 30, we observe a <inline-formula><mml:math xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="M143" altimg="si1.gif"><mml:mrow><mml:mo linebreak="goodbreak" linebreakstyle="after">∼</mml:mo></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> 5-fold enrichment in synergistic combinations. However, this enrichment decreases with the size of the query.</p></sec><sec id="sec4.4.20" disp-level="4"><title>Drug similarity, model uncertainty and error</title><p id="p0785">To help build intuition for the relationship between drug similarity, model uncertainty and model accuracy, we provide some additional results using RECOVER trained on combinations from the ALMANAC database (data pertaining to the MCF7 cell line).</p><p id="p0790">In <xref rid="mmc1" ref-type="supplementary-material">Figure S4</xref>B, we report both model uncertainty (lower left triangle) and mean square error (upper right triangle). Uncertainty is estimated using an ensemble of 20 models. The ordering of the drugs is based on a hierarchical clustering wherein distances between drugs are derived from their Tanimoto similarities. Therefore, the ordering of lines and columns is based on drug structures. Clustering is performed separately for drugs contained within the training/validation set and drugs contained within the test set.</p><p id="p0795">We observe that there is some local consistency in the values of model uncertainty, suggesting that the model is confident in some regions of chemical space, and less confident in other regions of the chemical space. Moreover, we observe columns and rows of high uncertainty, meaning that the model is less confident about specific drugs (regardless of the other drug found in the combination). On average, uncertainty is lowest on the training-validation set, and highest on combinations where neither of the drugs have been seen, <italic>c.f.</italic> marginal distributions of log-uncertainties in <xref rid="mmc1" ref-type="supplementary-material">Figure S4</xref>C (top). Moreover, large model errors seem to preferentially occur in regions of high uncertainty – as shown in <xref rid="mmc1" ref-type="supplementary-material">Figure S4</xref>C. Most importantly, large errors never occur when model is confident (low uncertainty), <italic>c.f.</italic> absence of points in the upper left part of <xref rid="mmc1" ref-type="supplementary-material">Figure S4</xref>C. Furthermore, logged model errors and logged uncertainties are weakly positively correlated (0.22 on the <italic>Training+Validation</italic> split, 0.29 on the <italic>One unseen drug</italic> split, and 0.14 on the <italic>Two unseen drugs</italic> split).</p><p id="p0800">The modeling of structure-activity relationships (SAR) is an extremely difficult task when considering emergent complex phenotypes (e.g., cell death) due to the presence of cliff effects, i.e., there are superficially similar molecules with similar properties, and others with divergent properties. When fixing <inline-formula><mml:math xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="M156" altimg="si97.gif"><mml:mrow><mml:mover accent="true"><mml:mi>σ</mml:mi><mml:mo>ˆ</mml:mo></mml:mover><mml:mo linebreak="goodbreak" linebreakstyle="after">=</mml:mo><mml:mn>1</mml:mn></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula>, <inline-formula><mml:math xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="M157" altimg="si96.gif"><mml:mrow><mml:mi>N</mml:mi><mml:mi>L</mml:mi><mml:mi>L</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula> corresponds to the MSE criterion.</p></sec><sec id="sec4.4.23" disp-level="4"><title>Efficient exploration of combination space</title><p id="p0820">In order to simulate real-world interactions with the wet lab, we start with a set of 30 randomly chosen drug pairs that the pipeline is initially trained on, while the rest of the data is hidden from the pipeline. Hence, these combinations are always part of the visible set. We now perform an <italic>iteration</italic>: we split the visible set into a training and validation set (80/20), train the model using early stopping and then acquire 30 additional drug pairs from the hidden set. The entire procedure is repeated again with the new training set of 60 drug pairs (i.e., <inline-formula><mml:math xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="M158" altimg="si98.gif"><mml:mrow><mml:mn>30</mml:mn><mml:mo linebreak="goodbreak" linebreakstyle="after">+</mml:mo><mml:mn>30</mml:mn></mml:mrow></mml:math></inline-formula>), and then with 90, and so on, until no more drug pairs are left to acquire from the hidden set. A range of methods can be used to generate recommendations for new drug pairs, typically as specified by an <italic>acquisition function</italic>.<xref rid="bib25" ref-type="bibr"><sup>25</sup></xref></p><p id="p0825">We compare acquisition functions by the rate at which they unblind the set of top 1% of synergistic combinations in the NCI-ALMANAC dataset within each synthetic experimental round (or <italic>iteration</italic>). As shown in <xref rid="mmc1" ref-type="supplementary-material">Figure S4</xref>D (left), Greedy acquisition and Upper Confidence Bound (UCB) acquisition, two model-based strategies, perform on par with each other and outperform the random strategy by a large margin. Both approaches discover 80% of the top 1% of synergistic drug pairs in approximately 15 iterations whereas the random strategy discovers less than 20% in the same number of iterations.</p><p id="p0830"><xref rid="mmc1" ref-type="supplementary-material">Figure S4</xref>D (right) presents the average synergy among queried combinations at each iteration of the SMO pipeline. While the average synergy using random strategy is always approximately 10, model-based strategies query batches for which the average synergy can be up to 25. After approximately 15 iterations, the average synergy in the queries starts to decrease, as there are fewer highly synergistic combinations left to query.</p></sec><sec id="sec4.4.24" disp-level="4"><title>Performance of uncertainty driven strategies</title><p id="p0835">When trying to mirror as closely as possible the setting of the <italic>in vitro</italic> experiments, we did not notice any significant difference in performance between the two Model-based acquisition functions Greedy and UCB, as shown in <xref rid="mmc1" ref-type="supplementary-material">Figures S4</xref>D (left). However, depending on the model set up, we can demonstrate that taking uncertainty into account to guide experiments can increase the performance of the pipeline over a naive Greedy acquisition strategy.</p><p id="p0840">In the following, uncertainty was directly estimated using a DEUP-style <italic>uncertainty predictor</italic>, and the task is the prediction of the average Bliss synergy score (instead of maximum Bliss), which corresponds to the average over the dose-response matrix of the concentration specific Bliss scores. In the following, 5 combinations were acquired at a time, instead of 30.</p><p id="p0845">As shown in <xref rid="mmc1" ref-type="supplementary-material">Figure S4</xref>F, UCB can outperform Greedy acquisition, demonstrating the value of taking uncertainty into account in the exploration strategy.</p></sec><sec id="sec4.4.25" disp-level="4"><title>Transfer learning: O’Neil 2016 to NCI-ALMANAC</title><p id="p0850">An important aspect of the SMO pipeline is its ability to leverage publicly available data in order to improve performance in a new experimental setting. To this end, we analyzed the impact of pretraining the model on the O’Neil database before simulating SMO experiments on a subset of the NCI-ALMANAC database.</p><p id="p0855">While the out-of-distribution analysis presented in <xref rid="mmc1" ref-type="supplementary-material">Figure S2</xref>C showed that RECOVER does not generalize well to new experimental settings <italic>without adaptation</italic>, these experiments demonstrate, quite remarkably, that some latent knowledge can still be transferred from one experimental setting to another, resulting in increased performance in the latter setting.</p><p id="p0860">As shown in <xref rid="mmc1" ref-type="supplementary-material">Figure S4</xref>E, the model pretrained on O’Neil outperforms the other model, initialized randomly. The model was first pretrained on the O’Neil study, thereafter, we simulated the SMO process on the subset of NCI-ALMANAC consisting of drug pairs for which at least one of the drugs was included in the O’Neil study. We compared against a model that had not been pretrained. All models are conditioned on cell line, and two different uncertainty estimation methods were tested.</p><p id="p0865">In these experiments, we restricted ourselves to cell lines which were covered in both the O’Neil and NCI-ALMANAC studies. As the MCF7 cell line is not included in the O’Neil study, we could not restrict ourselves to MCF7 as usual. Instead, we performed SMO on all overlapping cell lines, resulting in a space of possible queries which is bigger than in other experiments where only MCF7 was used. This explains why the rate of discovery is slower in this case.</p></sec></sec></sec><sec id="sec4.5" disp-level="2"><title>Quantification and statistical analysis</title><p id="p0870">In order to evaluate the significance of the enrichment in highly synergistic combinations within the queries of RECOVER during the prospective evaluation, we performed t tests and a Kolmogorov-Smirnov test.
