## Question before choosing a method

Can an experimentally controlled change in population context reveal an invariant that predicts whether a drug hit survives a new context? The invariant must restrict more than an arbitrary response surface, and its proposed use must be a still-unmeasured functional decision. A context marker alone is not a mechanism. Directly assaying the target context and ordinary joint dose/history/context inference are mandatory rivals, with the same observations, timing and total assay costs.

These are independent opening hypotheses, not source-supported findings or recommendations:

1. **Finite extracellular drug availability.** Does the population remove or bind a materially finite portion of the applied compound? If so, total cell number per medium volume should control apparent resistance even when local contact/crowding is held fixed. Mass conservation could connect density changes to an effective-dose shift and predict the dose needed in an unmeasured context. Volume scaling / replenishment versus area occupancy could distinguish a drug sink from intrinsic density-dependent resistance. Unknown: human oncology source with explicit density, medium volume, exposure history and functional output; reversible binding alone might equilibrate without a useful simple law. Classical inoculum and target-mediated drug disposition priors may already own the relation.

2. **Support source versus recipient demand.** Does extracellular support depend on producer abundance per recipient, or on producer abundance per medium volume? Mixing experiments that separate total density and producer fraction could distinguish resource dilution from a saturating public support factor. A restrictive relation would require a conserved or source-limited support quantity; merely fitting a latent support variable is insufficient. CAF/contact/dormancy and prevention-versus-maintenance are already owned by PNAS 10.1073/pnas.2423894122 and the archive, and are not new claims here. Unknown: actual support variable and qualifying human functional resource.

3. **Drug-induced release from competition.** A selective drug can reduce total abundance early yet increase the surviving cell type's later growth by removing competitors. Marked cell-type trajectories could connect selective depletion, mixture fraction and delayed response through an explicit interaction law. An early/late sign reversal alone is not distinctive because differential growth and cytostasis can also produce it. A useful relation would have to predict a previously unmeasured mixture/schedule from the same interaction parameters, and compete with ordinary ecological dynamics/adaptive-therapy priors.

4. **Crowding changes target opportunity.** A drug that requires cycling may appear weak at high density because fewer opportunities occur. The interesting possibility would be a context-invariant response conditional on measured division opportunity. However, growth-rate correction and ordinary cycle-mediated susceptibility are strong prior explanations, and the recent scalar-cycle candidate is parked. This line is not to be revived by changing names; a new restrictive biological dependency would be required.
