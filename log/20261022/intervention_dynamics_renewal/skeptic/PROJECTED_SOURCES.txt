      "abstract": [
        "A fraction of tumor cells can survive therapy without acquiring stable resistance mutations and enter a drug-tolerant persister (DTP) state. After drug withdrawal, these cells may resume proliferation and regain drug sensitivity, retain phenotypic features that alter survival or death upon retreatment, or continue to evolve toward stable resistance. The DTP designation describes survival under therapeutic pressure but does not establish whether the associated state persists after treatment ends. The term post-treatment cellular memory is used here operationally for a treatment-associated state that remains detectable after adequate drug washout and can influence a later response. Evidence can be evaluated across three experimental dimensions: persistence after withdrawal, maintenance across subsequent cell divisions or clonal descendants, and a functional consequence upon re-exposure. These dimensions indicate different strengths of support rather than mandatory diagnostic criteria. The review therefore organizes representative studies by what was measured after withdrawal rather than by DTP markers or mechanisms alone. Recent work indicates that AP-1 can contribute to encoding treatment-associated transcriptional states and that CBP/p300 activity can help maintain an established reporter state, including after drug withdrawal; however, molecular persistence does not substitute for demonstrating altered function upon rechallenge with the original agent. Chromatin remodeling, transcriptional state transitions, antioxidant metabolism, autophagy, organelle homeostasis, and lineage plasticity may prolong residual-cell survival. Their biological consequences differ: some states decay after withdrawal, some create time-limited vulnerabilities to ferroptosis or to therapeutic targeting of anti-apoptotic dependencies, and others extend the interval during which genetic resistance can emerge. Lineage tracing, single-cell multi-omics, withdrawal-rechallenge experiments, and longitudinal sampling of minimal residual disease can help distinguish transient stress responses from persistent post-treatment states. These observations provide a rationale for testing whether treatment sequence and interventions during residual disease can be matched to the reversibility and vulnerabilities of residual states."

      "abstract": [
        "Activating mutations in KRAS drive pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) and non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). Although mutant-selective KRAS inhibitors and pan-RAS inhibitors provide clinical benefits, the development of resistance limits durable responses. Here, transcriptomic and proteomic analyses revealed that, despite effective suppression of mutant KRAS signaling, resistant cells sustain cell cycle progression. Distinct orthogonal mitogenic pathways were engaged in a context-dependent manner to bypass KRAS inhibition. While these pathways could be broadly inhibited using the pan-RAS-ON inhibitor RMC-6236, cells remained capable of developing acquired resistance where cell proliferation was uncoupled from RAS signaling. Combinatorial drug screens and genome-wide CRISPR-Cas9 screens showed that perturbing cell cycle nodes via targeting cyclin dependent kinases CDK4/6 and CDK2 restored sensitivity to KRAS/RAS inhibitors. Co-targeting CDK4/6 induced G1 arrest and suppressed E2F-regulated proteins across all resistant models. In contrast, co-targeting CDK2 exerted a broader effect by impairing DNA replication, inducing G2 arrest, preventing mitotic entry, and yielding a more durable cytostatic response that delayed cellular outgrowth after drug withdrawal. Finally, concurrent inhibition of KRAS with either CDK4/6 or CDK2 yielded durable tumor control in vivo in xenografts derived from models with acquired resistance. In conclusion, these findings identify sustained cell cycle activity as a defining feature of resistance to KRAS-directed therapies and establish cell cycle co-targeting as an effective strategy to overcome KRAS/RAS inhibitor resistance."

        "Regenerating tissues like the axolotl (Ambystoma mexicanum) limb draw from many cell types, but isolating specific cell populations remains a bottleneck. Standard approaches rely on enzymatic dissociation and fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS), which are slow, costly, and require specialized instrumentation. We adapted INTACT (Isolation of Nuclei Tagged in specific Cell Types) to the axolotl, generating a transgenic line (INTACT-olotl) expressing Sun1-sfGFP in limb connective tissue, the population that carries limb positional information, dedifferentiates following injury, and forms the majority of the regenerative blastema.
