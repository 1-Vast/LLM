However, existing approaches typically treat simulators as fixed data sources, relying on uniform generation, offline retrieval, or manual parameter alignment by domain experts. We formulate Active Generative Simulation : optimizing a simulator’s data-generation distribution to maximize downstream real-world performance. To solve this problem, we propose MetaGenSim , a meta-learning framework that trains an auxiliary rater network via meta-gradients to automatically discover high-utility data-generation distributions within a user-specified simulation parameter space, without manual parameter alignment. Across simulation and real-world manipulation tasks, MetaGenSim outperforms uniform generation and retrieval baselines, achieves a 150 % 150\% relative improvement in real-world full-task success over uniform generation, and matches expert manual alignment. We further show that distributions curated with a lightweight proxy policy transfer to VLA fine-tuning, and that MetaGenSim can exploit partial alignment through atomic skill stitching and object-receptacle composition when exact sim-and-real task alignment is unavailable.

This induces a utility landscape over parameters, enabling active generation of targeted simulation data that matches the real-world distribution without manually tuning the generation parameters. 1 Introduction While recent large-scale real-robot datasets have advanced generalist policies [ 1 , 2 , 3 ] , relying exclusively on real data remains expensive, labor-intensive, and safety-constrained. Generative simulation offers a scalable alternative data source by procedurally synthesizing scene layouts, assets, and tasks [ 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 ] . We refer to the variables controlling these environments, such as camera poses, object arrangements, visual appearance, and task goals, as data generation parameters . Given data generation parameters, a generative simulator can instantiate environments and generate large numbers of demonstrations with minimal human intervention [ 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 ] . However, the utility of simulation data remains limited by the sim-to-real gap. Domain randomization and system identification can mitigate this mismatch, but often require substantial human effort and cannot eliminate residual gaps [ 14 ] . A promising alternative is sim-and-real co-training , where real demonstrations anchor learning to the deployment domain while simulation provides scale and diversity [ 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 ] . Co-training can outperform training on real data alone [ 17 , 16 , 20 ] , but not all generated data is useful: misaligned camera poses, object layouts, visual factors, or task goals can provide little benefit, and exact alignment may be impossible when the simulator lacks the target setting. This raises a central question: Among the vast space of possible simulation environments, which data should be generated to maximize the performance of a sim-and-real co-trained policy? Current simulation data-generation strategies largely rely on manual heuristics [ 21 , 22 ] or uniform randomization [ 23 , 24 ] . Overly broad training distributions can degrade policy learning when many samples are irrelevant to the target task [ 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 ] . Prior work suggests that task-aware alignment helps [ 16 ] , but manually tuning high-dimensional generation parameters is laborious, requires expert knowledge, and can be misleading when exact alignment is impossible. In this work, we formulate Active Generative Simulation : determining a distribution over simulation parameters such that co-training on the resulting synthetic data maximizes target-task performance. To efficiently tackle this problem, we propose MetaGenSim , a meta-learning framework that uses a sparse probing dataset to train an auxiliary rater network , which assigns utility scores to simulation samples based on their contribution to real-world validation performance, automatically guiding the active generation of a targeted synthetic dataset.
In summary, our core contributions are: • We formulate Active Generative Simulation , shifting simulation data generation from fixed distribution to controllable and targeted synthesis. • We introduce MetaGenSim , a meta-learning framework that trains an auxiliary rater network via meta-gradients to discover high-utility data-generation distributions within a user-specified parameter space, without manual parameter alignment. • We show that MetaGenSim outperforms uniform generation and offline retrieval, achieving a 150 % 150\% relative improvement in real-world full-task success over uniform generation while matching the performance of manual expert alignment. • We demonstrate scalability and generalization of MetaGenSim : proxy-curated datasets transfer to fine-tuning π 0.5 \pi_{0.5} , and MetaGenSim can exploit partially aligned data when exact task alignment is unavailable. 2 Related Works Generative simulation. Recent progress in generative modeling [ 30 , 31 ] , motion synthesis [ 9 , 32 , 33 ] , and procedural environment generation [ 4 , 5 ] has enabled automated simulation pipelines that scale synthetic robot data [ 34 , 7 , 35 , 23 , 36 , 37 , 38 , 39 , 40 ] . These systems can generate assets, tasks, scenes, and demonstrations with limited human intervention. However, most synthesize feasible data passively without optimizing which specific samples best improve a downstream policy. In contrast, MetaGenSim leverages a small set of target-task data to actively guide simulation data generation toward high-utility parameter regions. Sim-and-real alignment and policy co-training. To leverage simulation data for real-robot deployment, policies must overcome the sim-to-real gap [ 24 , 41 , 42 , 43 , 44 , 45 , 46 , 16 , 23 , 17 , 18 ] . Domain randomization perturbs visual and physical properties to encourage robustness [ 24 , 41 , 42 ] , while system identification tunes simulator parameters to match real-world dynamics or appearance [ 46 , 43 , 45 ] . These approaches assume there exist high-fidelity alignments, but tighter matching does not necessarily translate to better policy learning when residual gaps remain [ 44 ] . Recent sim-and-real co-training methods instead mix simulated and real demonstrations, showing strong empirical gains [ 16 , 23 , 17 , 18 , 19 ] . MetaGenSim builds on this co-training paradigm but treats the generative simulator as a controllable distribution, actively optimizing data synthesis rather than manually tuning the simulator to match the real world. Data attribution and retrieval in robotics. Recent work shows that heterogeneous robot datasets can cause negative transfer on specific tasks [ 26 , 28 , 25 , 29 , 47 , 48 , 49 ] . Retrieval and attribution methods address this by selecting useful samples from existing datasets using embedding similarity [ 29 , 50 , 47 , 49 ] , rollout outcomes [ 26 ] , or validation loss [ 25 ] . However, these methods are fundamentally limited by the coverage of the static prior dataset and cannot synthesize new target-relevant data. MetaGenSim is complementary: it uses utility estimation to generate new simulation data tailored to the target task. 3 The Active Generative Simulation Problem We formalize Active Generative Simulation as the problem of optimizing a simulator’s data generation distribution to maximize downstream policy performance on a real-world target task. Rather than treating the simulator as a fixed data source, we formulate it as a controllable generator parameterized by spatial, visual, and task-level variables, such as object placements, camera poses, and textures. By treating data generation itself as an optimization problem, our goal is to actively allocate the simulation budget toward the parameter regions most valuable for sim-and-real co-training. 3.1 Generative Simulation and Co-Training We denote the generative simulator as G : Φ → Ξ G:\Phi\to\Xi , where Φ \Phi is the space of data generation parameters and Ξ \Xi is the space of expert trajectories. The parameter vector ϕ \phi controls variables such as task identity, object placements, camera configuration, and visual appearance.
In our real-world experiments, Φ \Phi includes task mode, table texture (3D), camera yaw (1D), and object placements (4D). We assume that Φ \Phi , its ranges, and the underlying generation pipeline are specified by the user; our goal is to optimize the data-generation distribution within this space rather than to design the space itself. We propose a meta-learning framework for Active Generative Simulation with three stages:
(a) Rater network learning: We meta-learn a rater network that weights simulation samples according to their contribution to policy performance on a held-out validation set of real demonstrations.
(b) Parameter utility landscape: We map these scores back to their corresponding simulation parameters and use kernel density estimation (KDE) to construct a probability landscape that identifies high-utility regions.
(c) Targeted synthesis and co-training: We sample from the constructed distribution to generate a targeted simulation corpus, then co-train the final policy on this synthetic data and few-shot real demonstrations to maximize real-world success. 4 Active Generative Simulation by Meta Learning To approximate the bilevel problem in Eq. Our framework has two stages: (1) learn an auxiliary rater network that scores simulation samples by their contribution to real-task performance [ 51 , 52 ] , and (2) convert these scores into a parameter distribution 𝒮 ^ ϕ \widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{\phi} for targeted data generation.

We bridge this gap by mapping rater scores back to the probing parameters and then constructing 𝒮 ^ ϕ \widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{\phi} . Parameter utility. We define a utility function 𝒰 : Φ → ℝ \mathcal{U}:\Phi\to\mathbb{R} that maps each generation parameter to its estimated contribution to real-world performance. Since the rater R η R_{\eta} assigns scores to individual observation-action pairs, we estimate the utility of a parameter ϕ i \phi_{i} by aggregating the rater scores along the corresponding synthesized trajectory ξ i = { ( o i , t , a i , t ) } t = 1 T i \xi_{i}=\{(o_{i,t},a_{i,t})\}_{t=1}^{T_{i}} : 𝒰 ⁡ ( ϕ i ) = 1 T i ​ ∑ t = 1 T i R η ​ ( o i , t , a i , t ) . \mathcal{U}(\phi_{i})=\frac{1}{T_{i}}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{i}}R_{\eta}(o_{i,t},a_{i,t}). Optionally, this empirical mean can be replaced with a CVaR-style aggregation to provide additional robustness against high variance in the quality of synthesized ( o , a ) (o,a) pairs (see Appendix D ). Since 𝒟 probe \mathcal{D}_{\text{probe}} uniformly covers Φ \Phi , these aggregated scores define a utility landscape used to approximate 𝒮 ϕ ∗ \mathcal{S}_{\phi}^{*} . MetaGenSim consistently outperforms both UniformGen and RealOnly , while remaining close to Oracle , indicating that the learned simulation distributions are useful beyond the proxy policy class used during meta-learning. 5.4 Handling Irreducible Sim-and-Real Misalignment (RQ4) The previous sections show that MetaGenSim can align simulation parameters to real-world tasks despite visual rendering gaps; here, we study a stronger form of misalignment where the exact target task cannot be generated directly in the simulator. We test whether MetaGenSim can synthesize data mixtures that support two forms of generalization: atomic skill stitching for long-horizon behaviors and object-receptacle composition for novel real tasks. Atomic skill stitching for long-horizon behaviors. We first examine long-horizon tasks whose exact unified behaviors are absent from the source task set. For openDrawerPutBowl and putBowlCloseDrawer , full task-level alignment is impossible; useful co-training data must instead come from related atomic tasks. As shown in Figure 3 , MetaGenSim remains effective by using meta-gradient feedback to prioritize necessary source-task components. On putBowlCloseDrawer , for example, MetaGenSim even outperforms Oracle by generating a non-obvious mixture of binBowl , putBowlInDrawer , and closeDrawer . This demonstrates that MetaGenSim can curate targeted data that helps policies stitch reusable simulation skills over time under irreducible task misalignment. We further evaluate whether the generated data supports generalization to the novel real-world task mugTray , which has no exact source-task counterpart. The target task setup is unseen, but it shares the mug-grasping affordance of mugBin and the tray-receiving affordance of bowlTray . As shown in Figure 6 , MetaGenSim automatically identifies and prioritizes these related skills, generating most of its co-training data from mugBin and bowlTray , and achieves higher performance than UniformGen . These results show that MetaGenSim can synthesize reusable simulation-skill mixtures that enable policies to compose learned object and receptacle affordances for novel real-world target setups. 6 Conclusion We introduced MetaGenSim , a meta-learning framework for Active Generative Simulation that optimizes what to simulate for sim-and-real co-training. MetaGenSim learns a target-aware rater from meta-gradients, converts rater scores into data-generation distributions, and synthesizes targeted simulation datasets for improved sim-and-real co-training. Across simulation and real-world manipulation tasks, MetaGenSim improves over real-only training, uniform generation, and offline retrieval, while matching the performance of expert-aligned simulation data. We further show that proxy-curated datasets transfer to VLA fine-tuning, and that MetaGenSim can exploit partially aligned simulation data through atomic skill stitching and object-receptacle composition when exact sim-and-real alignment is unavailable. 7 Limitations User-specified parameter space and generation pipeline. MetaGenSim optimizes the data-generation distribution within a user-specified parameter space Φ \Phi , including its ranges and the underlying generation pipeline (e.g., MimicGen source demonstrations). It can therefore only exploit factors exposed in Φ \Phi and data the pipeline can produce. Unexposed factors, such as background and lighting in our real-world setup, remain unaligned. This parameterization is straightforward in our domains but may not scale to broader simulators that represent tasks, visuals, and configurations differently. Developing a unified parameter space, potentially through learned latent parameters that make the generation pipeline more differentiable, is an exciting future direction. Probing coverage and density estimation. The rater can only score regions covered by the probing dataset, which we sample uniformly in a single round. Sparse probing may miss small high-utility regions, and weighted KDE becomes less reliable as the dimension of Φ \Phi grows. Adaptive or iterative probing that refines the distribution over multiple rounds is a natural extension. Distribution concentration and hyperparameters. We impose no explicit diversity or coverage constraint. Instead, the spread of the generated distribution is controlled by the temperatures and the KDE bandwidth. Overly small bandwidths or overly high temperatures degrade performance (Appendix Table 6 ), and a narrow target dataset can yield a narrow generated distribution. Data Generation Parameters : As discussed in Section 5 , we parameterize the generative simulator using Task (Discrete), Camera Pose (Continuous, 1D), Table Texture (Continuous, 3D), and Object Placements (Continuous, 4D). We still use MimicLabs as our generative simulator, but instead of using MimicLabs datasets, we customize the environment to match the real table height, camera intrinsics, and camera pointing position. Also, we choose one specific lab (Lab3) for our experiments and do not consider lab as a data generation parameter. As can be seen in Figure 8 , the background of the simulation environments are very different from the real environment. Although the parameter space Φ \Phi is continuous, the generative simulator imposes validity constraints. For instance, object and receptacle positions cannot overlap to avoid collisions. Furthermore, the underlying demonstration generator, MimicGen [ 9 ] , requires distinct source demonstrations for different spatial arrangements (e.g., swapping objects from left to right requires separate source demos). To satisfy these constraints when generating 𝒟 probe \mathcal{D}_{\text{probe}} and the UniformGen dataset, we sample parameters from pre-defined disjoint regions. Specifically, object positions are drawn from two distinct configuration settings: (Object 1 on the left, Object 2 on the right) and (Object 1 on the right, Object 2 on the left), where “left” and “right” denote specific 15 ​ cm × 20 ​ cm 15\text{cm}\times 20\text{cm} rectangular zones. Additionally, camera yaw is sampled from 5 disjoint angular bins, and table textures are sampled from regions near black, white, blue, and red. When using MetaGenSim to actively generate data, we do not restrict sampling to these disjoint regions. Instead, we sample continuous values directly from the Gaussian kernels. For object positions, we apply a simple rejection sampling step to prevent objects from being too close to each other or out of the workspace. Baselines and Datasets . We generate simulation demonstrations via MimicGen [ 9 ] . We compare MetaGenSim against the following baselines, controlling for data budgets where applicable: • MetaGenSim (Ours): We first generate a probing dataset 𝒟 probe \mathcal{D}_{\text{probe}} of 800 demos (Visualization in Figure 8 ), which is used alongside 𝒟 R \mathcal{D}_{R} to train the rater network (details in Appendix D ). We then set a generation budget of 800 demos for the active generation phase. Thus, the final MetaGenSim dataset consists of 800 demos sampled from the learned distribution. • Oracle : This dataset contains 800 demos generated with expert-aligned parameters: the correct task, camera extrinsics from the real-world calibration, manually tuned object reset ranges, and manually selected table colors. • UniformGen : To ensure a fair comparison of total simulation samples, UniformGen has access to the 800 probing demos plus an additional 800 uniformly generated demos, totaling 1600 demos. • STRAP and IWR : These retrieval baselines select a subset of data from the 1600-demo UniformGen pool. Further implementation details for retrieval-based methods are provided in Appendix F . We use the same source demos in MimicGen to generate all above simulation datasets. After generating the simulation datasets, we co-train Diffusion Policy [ 55 , 56 ] on a mixture of the generated simulation data and the real-world data 𝒟 R \mathcal{D}_{R} . We utilize a co-training ratio of α = 0.9 \alpha=0.9 , which is determined by empirical findings in [ 17 ] . For details on training and evaluation procedures, please refer to Appendix E . For the real-world stackCube setting with up-and-down object placements (shown in Figure 11 ), we treat the task as fixed rather than a data generation parameter. Consequently, for this specific experiment, the probing dataset consists exclusively of samples from the stackCube task. Appendix C Sim-and-Sim Experiments Setup We conduct controlled experiments in simulation to evaluate the efficacy of our meta-learning algorithm in identifying simulation parameters that result in environments aligned with the target task. These experiments are based on a subset of the MimicLabs dataset [ 54 ] . Domain Gap Construction. To simulate the sim-to-real gap, we manually create domain shifts using the distinct “lab” environments provided in MimicLabs. The dataset contains 8 different labs, each featuring unique background scenes and table textures. For each target task, we designate one lab as the target domain (from which the few-shot target data is drawn) and the remaining 7 labs as the source domains . The generative simulator is constrained to generate data only within the source domains. Consequently, a domain gap is strictly enforced, as the visual appearance (background and texture) of the generated data always differs from the target task. Target Tasks. As shown in Figure 9 , we select 8 target tasks for our evaluation, ranging from short-horizon pick-and-place to long-horizon multi-stage manipulation: • binCarrot : The robot picks up a carrot and places it into a bin. • binBowl : The robot picks up a bowl and places it into a bin. • binMug : The robot picks up a mug and places it into a bin. • openDrawer : The robot pulls a drawer open. • openMicrowave : The robot opens a microwave door. • closeMicrowave : The robot closes a microwave door. • openDrawerPutBowl : A long-horizon task where the robot must first open a drawer and then place a bowl inside it. • putBowlCloseDrawer : A long-horizon task where the robot must place a bowl into a drawer and then close the drawer. Target Dataset : We use 10 demonstrations for each target task, with the exception of openDrawerPutBowl , which utilizes 20 demonstrations. Data Generation Parameters. For the source data generation, we consider a parameter space defined by the following axes: • Atomic Tasks (Discrete): We consider 8 atomic source tasks: binCarrot , binBowl , binMug , openDrawer , closeDrawer , openMicrowave , closeMicrowave , and putBowlInDrawer . Note that while the first six target tasks have direct counterparts in the source tasks, the long-horizon target tasks do not. We expect MetaGenSim to automatically identify useful atomic sub-tasks (e.g., identifying openDrawer and putBowlInDrawer as necessary stages for the openDrawerPutBowl target task). • Labs (Discrete): The 7 available source labs. • Camera Pose (Continuous): The camera pose is represented in spherical coordinates, pointing towards the table center. • Object Spatial Arrangement (Continuous): The reset position of the manipulated objects (e.g., mug, bowl). • Receptacle Spatial Arrangement (Continuous): The reset position of the receptacle (e.g., bin, drawer). Figure 9: Target tasks for sim-and-sim co-training experiments. The MimicLabs dataset structure inherently groups continuous parameters into bins. Camera positions are grouped into 5 bins (each with varying polar and azimuthal angles. A visualization of camera pose ranges can be found in [ 54 , Figure 6] ), object reset positions are grouped into 3 bins (Left, Right, Center), and receptacle positions into 2 bins (Left, Right). Combined with the atomic tasks and labs, this results in approximately 800 distinct parameter bins for the source data. For MetaGenSim , 𝒟 probe \mathcal{D}_{\text{probe}} contains 1600 uniformly generated demonstrations, and the data generation budget is 1600 demonstrations. The primary objective of the sim-and-sim experiments is to verify the Rater Network’s ability to effectively score aligned data (Section 4.1 ). Therefore, we apply a simplified version of MetaGenSim for these specific experiments. Instead of constructing a continuous probability density function (as in Section 4.2 ), we leverage the pre-existing bins described above. We rank the parameter bins according to the CVaR scores of the probing samples and generate data from the top 2% of high-scoring bins. The 𝒟 probe \mathcal{D}_{\text{probe}} and UniformGen datasets are generated uniformly across all bins, while STRAP and IWR retrieve data from the UniformGen dataset. It is important to emphasize that this bin-ranking approach is used only for the sim-and-sim setting to validate the scoring mechanism. The Sim-and-Real experiments (detailed in Appendix B ) utilize the complete version of MetaGenSim . In the real-world setting, we do not group parameters into bins; instead, we perform continuous density estimation (KDE) over the individual data generation parameters to construct and sample from a smooth utility landscape. After the source datasets are generated using different methods, we co-train BC-RNN and Diffusion Policy (DP) [ 55 , 56 ] on the mixture of the generated/retrieved source dataset and the few-shot target dataset. Appendix D Details on MetaGenSim Rater Network Architecture. The Rater network is designed to assign a scalar score to observation-action pairs. It consists of an encoder followed by a MLP head. The encoder utilizes a ResNet18 backbone, identical to the one used in the policy architecture (see Appendix E ). However, unlike the policy, the Rater Network receives both observations and actions as inputs. Specifically, the action vector is concatenated with the low-dimensional observation vector before being processed. The combined features are then passed through an MLP with two hidden layers of size (256, 256) to output the final scalar score. The meta-learning procedure is detailed in Algorithm 1 . Following [ 52 ] , we employ the Adam optimizer [ 58 ] for both the inner (policy update) and outer (rater update) loops. We utilize the differentiable Adam implementation from TorchOpt [ 59 ] to enable higher-order meta-gradient backpropagation. We set the inner learning rate to 1 ​ e − 4 1e-4 (for the policy) and the outer learning rate to 1 ​ e − 5 1e-5 (for the rater). For real-world experiments, we use T = 5 T=5 inner gradient steps and a batch size of 64 for both the training and validation batches; this configuration fits in a single NVIDIA A40 GPU. For simulation experiments, we found that T = 2 T=2 inner steps is sufficient to learn an effective rater. We observed that using BC-RNN as the inner loop policy yields high-quality active generation results, even when the final downstream policy is a Diffusion Policy. We found that using Diffusion Policy directly within the meta-learning inner loop resulted in a “flat” rater score distribution, preventing the network from effectively distinguishing aligned parameters. We hypothesize that this is due to the stochastic noise inherent in the diffusion training, though further investigation is left for future work. Consequently, we use BC-RNN as the inner policy for all experiments. Importance of Co-Training the Rater. Empirically, we found it essential to input both simulation and real-world data into the rater network during the inner loop updates (as shown in Algorithm 1 ). If the rater is trained solely on the simulated probing dataset, it fails to correctly identify aligned parameters, likely due to the lack of grounding in the real-world data distribution. Differences from DataRater [ 52 ] . While our approach is inspired by [ 52 ] , there are three key distinctions: • Domain Application: [ 52 ] was originally proposed for curating data in the language domain (LLMs). We adapt the formulation specifically for the continuous domain of robotics and control. • Cross-Domain Scoring: [ 52 ] focuses on filtering out low-quality in-domain data (where validation data is from the same domain). In contrast, our algorithm solves a cross-domain problem: we score simulation data based on its utility for a real-world target task. • Simplified Meta-Learning: We do not utilize multiple inner models or periodic re-initialization of inner models. We found that a single inner policy model, no re-initialization, is sufficient for identifying high-utility regions in our setting. We do not use the bilevel gradient optimization techniques which is used to reduce RAM usage in [ 52 ] . We employ BC-RNN [ 60 ] and Diffusion Policy [ 55 ] as our imitation learning algorithms. Both architectures utilize a ResNet18 visual backbone. We also condition the policies on language instructions using FiLM layers in the visual backbone. All policies are trained for 500 epochs, with 500 gradient steps per epoch. Optimization is performed using the Adam optimizer [ 58 ] with a learning rate of 10 − 4 10^{-4} .
The policy inputs consist of low-dimensional observations (end-effector position, quaternion, and gripper position), RGB images (captured by agentview and wrist-mounted cameras), and language embeddings. The low-dimensional observations are processed by a MLP and concatenated with image features. Original input images of resolution 84 × 84 84\times 84 are randomly cropped to 76 × 76 76\times 76 during training. The policy output consists of the delta position and rotation for the end-effector, as well as the gripper position. Hyperparameters used for policy training are reported in Table 3 . Simulation Policy Evaluation .
We evaluate policy checkpoints every 50 epochs, performing 50 rollouts per evaluation. For reporting, we select the peak success rate observed during training. All experiments are repeated across three random seeds, with peak success rates reported independently for each seed. In Figures 3 and 12 , the bar heights represent the mean success rate across the three seeds, while the error bars indicate the Standard Error of the Mean (SEM).
