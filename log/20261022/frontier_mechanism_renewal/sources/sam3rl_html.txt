Its tracker, inherited from SAM 2, stores information from past frames in a memory bank, managed by a simple First-In-First-Out rule, allowing temporal consistency in complex videos.
Several recent methods improve SAM 2 with hand-crafted memory update rules, each targeting a specific failure mode, such as distractors, occlusions, or object motion. However, their gains transfer only partially to the stronger SAM 3 backbone.
We instead present a more general, data-driven approach that frames memory control as a sequential decision-making task.
SAM3RL is an extension of the SAM 3 tracker with a lightweight memory controller, trained by reinforcement learning, that decides which frames enter the memory bank in order to maximize tracking performance.
SAM3RL sets a new state of the art on 10 of 12 tracking and video object segmentation benchmarks, outperforming all hand-crafted rules without any runtime overhead.
The same approach applied to SAM 2 gives SAM2RL, which outperforms hand-crafted rules on 9 of the 12 benchmarks, showing the generality of our method across model generations. 1 Introduction Visual object tracking is a fundamental computer vision task in which a tracker is given a target object in the first frame of a video and must localize it, as a box or a segmentation mask, in all subsequent frames. Reliable tracking is a key component of applications such as autonomous driving, robotics, video surveillance, and sports analytics ( Marvasti-Zadeh et al., 2022 ) . The task remains difficult since the target may be surrounded by visually similar distractors, be occluded for long periods, or change its appearance. These challenges are amplified in long videos, where errors can accumulate over time and recovery depends critically on historical context ( Javed et al., 2023 ) . Recent state-of-the-art trackers are built on the Segment Anything Model (SAM) family.
SAM 2 ( Ravi et al., 2025 ) is a promptable video segmentation model that conditions each mask prediction on features from past frames held in a small, fixed-size memory bank .
SAM 3 ( Carion et al., 2026 ) targets promptable concept segmentation, while inheriting the tracker architecture from SAM 2.
The original SAM 2 populates the bank with a simple First-In-First-Out (FIFO) rule that keeps the most recent frames regardless of quality or relevance.
Therefore, a line of follow-up work replaces FIFO with hand-crafted update rules, including motion-aware frame selection ( Yang et al., 2026 ; Chen et al., 2026 ) , distractor-aware memory ( Videnović et al., 2026 ) , and calibrated prototype memory with positional priors ( Xu et al., 2025 ) ; some go further and also select among mask candidates by motion consistency ( Yang et al., 2026 ; Chen et al., 2026 ) or maintain several banks and search over them ( Ding et al., 2025b ) .
SAM 3 also follows this approach and refines FIFO by keeping only frames in which the object is not occluded, plus the most recent frame. However, each of the hand-crafted rules targets a particular failure mode, such as distractors, occlusions, or fast motion. Ideally, the tracker should be robust to all of them at the same time. Moreover, these rules are tied to the model generation they were designed for, as all heuristics developed for SAM 2 transfer poorly to the stronger SAM 3. Their gains on tracking benchmarks are limited, and on video object segmentation they even fall below SAM 3 on most benchmarks; see Figure 1 . The compared methods modify what the tracker stores in the memory bank, and in most cases also mask selection or the number of banks. In contrast, our policy operates with the memory bank only.
The compared methods are evaluated on SAM 2 as published and on SAM 3 after our porting to that backbone. In this work, we show that memory control should be learned rather than designed. We formulate memory control as a Markov Decision Process (MDP), designing the state, actions, and reward, but leaving the choice of what to remember to the agent.
That choice is learned by reinforcement learning (RL) directly from data, without touching the tracker itself.
We introduce SAM3RL , which applies this memory control formulation to SAM 3 and sets a new state
of the art on several tracking benchmarks, most notably on the VOTS and VOT-LT challenges.
It does so at no additional inference cost and, unlike hand-crafted rules, without sacrificing performance on video object segmentation.
Applied to SAM 2, the same recipe yields SAM2RL, which still outperforms hand-crafted rules on most benchmarks, showing the generality of our learned method across model generations. Our contributions are as follows: Learned memory control for SAM-based trackers. We formulate memory bank control in SAM-based trackers as an MDP over a fixed-capacity bank.
The state describes the content of the bank, the action selects the entry to evict,
and the reward scores the resulting prediction against the annotation.
This formulation allows the memory control to be learned instead of being manually designed. State-of-the-art visual object tracker. We present SAM3RL, which extends SAM 3 with a memory controller obtained by optimizing the introduced MDP with RL.
It improves over SAM 3 on 11 out of 12 benchmarks and ranks first on 10 of them.

Together with SAM3RL, this demonstrates the generality of the introduced method. 2 Related Work Visual object tracking (VOT) and semi-supervised video object segmentation (VOS) are conditioned on a target specified in the first frame of a video, which must then be localized in all subsequent frames.
The target is represented by a bounding box in VOT and a pixel-level mask in VOS.
Both tasks require maintaining the target through appearance changes, occlusions,
fast motion, and distractors, which remain the dominant sources of tracking failure ( Kristan et al., 2020 ) . To address these challenges, trackers have long exploited information from previous frames ( Matthews et al., 2004 ; Kalal et al., 2012 ; Oh et al., 2019 ; Cui et al., 2022 ; Han and Huang, 2024 ) . Recently, the SAM 2 foundation model ( Ravi et al., 2025 ) set a new state of the art in tracking.
Its impact is evident in the VOTS 2026 {}_{\text{2026}} challenge, where 92% of submitted trackers were derived from the SAM family ( Kristan et al., 2026 ) .
Central to the SAM 2 design is the memory bank, a fixed-capacity store of past frames on which every prediction is conditioned by memory attention.
The bank holds seven slots, the prompted first frame and the six most recent ones, under a FIFO rule.
Because adjacent frames are highly correlated, FIFO tends to fill the bank with redundant observations rather than the diverse history required to distinguish the target from distractors or recover after occlusions. Consequently, memory design in SAM-based trackers has become an active research area, with follow-up work replacing FIFO with hand-crafted selection rules.
SAMURAI ( Yang et al., 2026 ) uses a Kalman filter ( Kalman, 1960 ) to score motion
consistency, both selecting among per-frame mask candidates and gating memory updates.
DAM4SAM ( Videnović et al., 2026 ) splits the bank into a fixed-interval part and a
part reserved for frames that resolve visually similar distractors. SAMITE ( Xu et al., 2025 ) calibrates stored prototypes and adds positional priors.
HiM2SAM ( Chen et al., 2026 ) combines hierarchical motion estimation with a
long- and short-term split of the bank.
SAM2Long ( Ding et al., 2025b ) keeps several banks in parallel and performs tree search over alternative trajectories. Even SAM 3 ( Carion et al., 2026 ) relies on such a rule, retaining an unprompted frame only when the model is confident that the target is present. However, for older trackers, learned memory control has already been shown to outperform hand-crafted rules ( Choi et al., 2018 ; Yang and Chan, 2018 ; Zhang et al., 2019b ; Dai et al., 2020 ) .
Specifically for SAM 2, we attempted learning the rules instead of designing them in our preliminary work ( Adamyan et al., 2025 ) .
There, we showed the potential of learned memory control by fitting a separate RL agent to each of a handful of training videos.
Agents improve over the hand-crafted rules, but do not transfer to unseen videos.
This demonstrates a performance upper bound rather than a usable tracker.
Here, we develop the full method, a single general-purpose policy that generalizes to unseen videos, extends to SAM 3, and sets a new state of the art in visual object tracking. Once training is complete, the controller π θ \pi_{\theta} is fixed and the same loop is used for inference on unseen videos, with actions taken greedily and no reward computed. 3.1 MDP Formulation An episode of our MDP is one object tracked through a video.

Appendix E ablates the input feature groups, object pointer selection, and the reward normalization. 3.2 Training the Memory Controller Architecture. The controller π θ \pi_{\theta} is a single MLP, which serves as the actor .
All features enter it directly, except the object pointers, which a shared linear projection first compresses to 64 dimensions.
All groups are then concatenated and passed through one hidden layer of 2,048 units and a linear head over the seven actions, about 3.2 M parameters in total.
For training, we add a critic , a second network of the same design with a scalar head, which estimates the expected return, shares no parameters with the actor, and is discarded after training.
Besides s t s_{t} , the critic receives the normalized number of decision steps left in the episode, which it needs to predict undiscounted finite-horizon returns.
This value is unavailable at inference, but since only the actor is deployed, the controller remains fully online and depends on s t s_{t} alone. Training data. We train on the LaSOT training split ( Fan et al., 2019 ) , whose 1,120 sequences are divided into 896 for training and 224 for validation, while the official test set is held out.
LaSOT is annotated with per-frame bounding boxes at 30 FPS.
Since consecutive frames at that rate are nearly identical, an eviction decision changes the bank very little while still consuming a decision step.
We therefore subsample every sixth frame, so that training episodes run at 5 FPS and span 425 decision steps on average.
This also matches the typical annotation rate of VOS datasets. Optimization. We train the controller with Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) ( Schulman et al., 2017 ) , using the undiscounted Monte-Carlo return (i.e. , γ = 1 \gamma=1 ) minus the critic’s predicted value as the advantage.
The return is bootstrapped only when the rollout window truncates an episode, corresponding to λ = 1 \lambda=1 in generalized advantage estimation (GAE) ( Schulman et al., 2016 ) .
Apart from this bootstrap, we keep the critic out of the targets and use it only as a baseline, as it fits the training videos well but transfers poorly to unseen videos.
Each of the 400 iterations collects 376-step rollouts from 256 parallel environments.
Training takes about 24 hours on four H200 GPUs.
We evaluate the greedy policy on the full validation videos every 25 iterations and select the best checkpoint.
All remaining hyperparameters are listed in Table 13 in Appendix D . Inference. The actor picks actions greedily, and its only overhead is a single MLP evaluation per memory update.
To keep the decision rate close to the 5 FPS seen in training, the memory controller operates with a stride s = round ​ ( ν / 5 ) s=\text{round}(\nu/5) , where ν \nu is the frame rate of the benchmark.
The tracker still predicts a mask on every frame, but only every s s th frame triggers a memory update and can enter the bank.
On the remaining frames no memory frame m t m_{t} is formed, so the memory encoder that produces f t f_{t} is skipped altogether.
The 30 FPS VOT and VOTS benchmarks use s = 6 s=6 , while the 5 FPS MOSE v ​ 2 {}_{\text{v}2} uses s = 1 s=1 .

SAM3RL improves over SAM 3 on 11 of 12 benchmarks and ranks first on 10, with the largest gains on VOT(S), at virtually no runtime cost.
It is also the only method that consistently improves on video object segmentation.
Applying the same recipe to SAM 2 yields SAM2RL, which improves over SAM 2 on every benchmark and leads on 9.
These results suggest that memory control should be learned rather than designed, as learned control improves even the strongest trackers and, unlike fixed rules, remains effective across model generations. Limitations. First, the policy is tied to the 5 FPS decision rate seen in training.
Deciding on every frame of 30 FPS benchmarks still improves over SAM 3, but the average gain drops from 2.9 to 1.8 points.
We therefore match the training rate with the stride s s , which requires the frame rate to be known in advance and keeps the skipped frames out of the memory bank.
The former is rarely restrictive in practice, as the frame rate is usually available.
Training at the native rate would remove this restriction, but most decisions would then concern near-identical frames.
Such a policy could nonetheless exploit the skipped frames and improve further on high-frame-rate benchmarks.
Second, the policy controls only the seven frames in the memory bank, while the nine object pointers are filled by a fixed rule.

Appendix C Race Condition in SAM 3 The SAM 3 video predictor offloads per-frame tracking state to pageable host memory
with non_blocking=True and then reads that buffer on the CPU without
synchronizing, which PyTorch documents as undefined behavior. Within a single add_new_points_or_box call, the asynchronous copy of the
conditioning-frame mask is launched and the same tensor is interpolated on the
host a few lines later. If the copy did not land, the host will read the
leftovers of the previous sequence in the process. The corrupted mask is encoded
into the memory bank as frame 0, and because long-term tracking never
re-initializes, the sequence is lost from frame 1 rather than degrading over
time. The failure requires box initialization, CPU state offloading, and a
preceding sequence in the same process. We fix it by forcing CUDA → \rightarrow CPU copies to be synchronous. The impact of the race condition was seen most severely on the VOT-LT 2021 {}_{\text{2021}} challenge, where all methods are box-prompted, and on SAMURAI3, which is also box-prompted on all benchmarks; see Appendix B . All training hyperparameters are listed in Table 13 . Pre-computed embeddings. The image encoder is frozen and its output does not depend on the policy.
Thus, we compute the image embeddings of the whole training corpus once and cache them on disk.
During rollouts, only the tracker components after the image encoder are run. Batch composition. Each rank runs 64 parallel environments, each tracking one object. They come from 16 videos, with each object copied four times.
We truncate the batch to the length of its shortest video, so that all environments are synchronized and process the same frame index at every step. To discard few frames, videos are grouped into batches by similar length, with each length jittered by up to 16 steps so that the same videos are not always grouped together. Truncation. Apart from this batch truncation, episodes are not capped, whereas a rollout collects 376 steps. An episode that crosses the rollout boundary continues in the next iteration, and at the cut its return is bootstrapped with the critic’s value of the last state. Validation score on the LaSOT validation split during training; mean over three seeds with a ± \pm SEM band. The arms differ only in the b t b_{t} normalized reward, r t = q t − b t r_{t}=q_{t}-b_{t} against the raw quality r t = q t r_{t}=q_{t} . Besides the seven memory features, the memory attention of SAM 2 and SAM 3 also reads up to sixteen object pointers, one of which always comes from the conditioning frame; see Section 3 .
SAM 2 takes the remaining pointers from the 15 most recent frames, whereas SAM 3 takes them from the most recent frames whose effective IoU score exceeds a threshold, always including the previous frame.
SAM3RL instead uses a recently evicted rule, which reads the pointers of the conditioning frame and of the six frames currently in the memory bank, and fills the remaining nine slots with the frames most recently evicted from the bank.
We also consider a longest-kept rule, which ranks evicted frames by the number of decisions they remained in the memory bank and selects the pointers of the top nine.
Unlike the rules of SAM 2 and SAM 3, both of our rules depend on the agent’s actions. Figure 7 compares all four rules within SAM3RL, reporting the LaSOT validation score of each rule every 25 training iterations; all other components are kept fixed. After the first 200 iterations, both our action-dependent rules remain above the SAM 2 and SAM 3 rules, showing that the object pointer selection rule affects tracking performance.
Between the two, the recently evicted rule scores higher in most evaluations, but the longest-kept rule performs comparably and their seed bands overlap.
Designing better rules could improve performance further.
Moreover, since learned memory control outperforms hand-crafted rules for memory bank selection, extending the policy to also select object pointers is a natural direction for future work. E.3 Reward Normalization We train SAM3RL against the normalized reward r t = q t − b t r_{t}=q_{t}-b_{t} , which is the difference between the tracking quality q t q_{t} of SAM 3 under our learned memory control and the tracking quality b t b_{t} of SAM 3 under its native memory control.
Since much of the variance of q t q_{t} comes from the difficulty of the video rather than from the actions taken, subtracting the policy-independent b t b_{t} reduces this variance while leaving the optimal policy unchanged.
In Figure 7 , we ablate this normalization by training on the raw quality r t = q t r_{t}=q_{t} with everything else unchanged, and evaluate both arms every 25 training iterations on the LaSOT validation split.
The raw-reward arm falls below SAM 3 itself on several evaluations in the first 250 iterations and is less stable than the difference-based arm, which stays above SAM 3 throughout.
