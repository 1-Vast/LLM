Abstract
LLM agents accumulate valuable task-specific information through their actions, observations, and environmental feedback during multi-turn task execution.
When effectively used, this information can help agents refine subsequent decisions and improve task performance.
However, the learning value of an interaction depends on its context and subsequent feedback, making it challenging to extract reliable, actionable lessons and revise them as the task unfolds.
In this paper, we introduce ReTTT, which enables experience-driven self-adaptation through test-time training on lessons constructed and continually revised within an episode.
ReTTT lets the agent reflect on interaction feedback, maintain the resulting lessons in an explicit memory, and learn from them by updating an episode-local LoRA adapter while keeping the backbone frozen.
The adapted model then performs both subsequent actions and reflection, coupling policy adaptation with the construction of future learning experience.
We evaluate ReTTT on ALFWorld, ScienceWorld, and SWE-bench Lite using two model families.
ReTTT outperforms trajectory-based test-time training across all six model–benchmark settings, improving task success by up to 12.14 percentage points.
A frozen-reflection comparison further shows a 7.14-point benefit from adapting the reflection policy, while trajectory analysis illustrates how adapted reflection derives more task-specific guidance from failed attempts.
These findings suggest that learning to reflect on its own behavior can boost agents’ performance on long-horizon tasks.

Introduction
Modern large language model (LLM) agents
(
liu2024agentbench
)
tackle multi-turn tasks, such as software development
(
yang2024swe
;
jimenez2024swe
)
, web navigation
(
zhou2024webarena
)
, and scientific research
(
wang2022scienceworld
)
, through an iterative agent loop.
The agent alternates between reasoning, taking actions, and receiving environmental feedback, accumulating a trajectory of plans, intermediate reasoning, actions, and observations in its context
(
yao2022react
)
.
This trajectory records action outcomes and task progress, providing task-specific experience that can inform subsequent decisions and learning.
A substantial body of work investigates how agents can make better use of this interaction history.
Context management methods organize, compress, and retrieve historical information to guide subsequent actions through the input context while keeping model parameters fixed
(
park2023generative
;
packer2023memgpt
;
zhang2026adamem
;
mi2026procmem
)
.
Parametric memory approaches consolidate accumulated experience into learned parameters, allowing information from past trajectories to shape the agent’s policy beyond the immediate context
(
yao2026parammem
;
ren2026scaling
)
.
Recently, agentic test-time training (aTTT) has brought parameter adaptation into ongoing task execution, using signals from agent outputs, environmental observations, or generated progress summaries
(
wang2026no
)
.
It downweights repeated token spans to limit redundant self-training while retaining novel information.
Each update changes the policy that acts next and thereby shapes the interaction history available for subsequent learning.
However, continual parameter updates leave a key question: what should an agent learn from its ongoing interactions?
An interaction trajectory records what happened, but useful supervision must capture what should guide future behavior.
Failure feedback, for example, can turn an unsuccessful action into a lesson about prerequisites for subsequent attempts(Figure
1
a).
New observations can also revise earlier conclusions, requiring the supervision used for later updates to change as the task unfolds.
The challenge is therefore to continually derive reliable, decision-relevant lessons from the trajectory and use them to adapt the policy within the same episode.

To investigate the limitations of agentic test-time training over longer task horizons, we analyze aTTT’s performance and execution errors.
Its improvement over the frozen policy falls from 16.7 percentage points on short tasks to 6.4 on medium tasks and disappears on long tasks (Figure
1
c).
Meanwhile, aTTT’s off-goal error proxy, measured as placements of target objects at non-goal locations per 100 tasks, rises from 5.6 to 17.0 and 38.7 across these groups (Figure
1
d).
These findings suggest that accumulating more interaction data does not necessarily provide better learning supervision, motivating the explicit construction and revision of task-relevant lessons before parameter updates.
To address these challenges, we introduce ReTTT(Figure
1
b), which couples the construction and revision of learning experience with policy adaptation through test-time training.
The same adapted model acts in the environment and constructs the lessons used for later updates within the ongoing episode.
At periodic reflection steps, it interprets interaction feedback to extract task-relevant lessons, including action prerequisites, failure conditions, and guidance for remaining subgoals.
These lessons are checked against interaction evidence and maintained in an explicit memory that is revised as new information arrives.
New and revised lessons, together with selected prior experience, are then used to update an episode-local LoRA adapter through test-time training, while the backbone remains frozen.
We instantiate these lessons as question–answer pairs (ReTTT-QA) and structured summaries (ReTTT-Sum).
We evaluate ReTTT on ALFWorld, ScienceWorld, and SWE-bench Lite using Qwen3.5-9B and Gemma4-12B.
Both ReTTT-QA and ReTTT-Sum outperform aTTT in all six model–benchmark settings (Table
1
).
With Qwen3.5-9B, ReTTT-QA improves success over aTTT by 12.14, 11.67, and 3.33 percentage points on the three benchmarks, respectively.
On ALFWorld, ReTTT-QA’s advantage over aTTT grows from 5.6 percentage points on short tasks to 14.7 on long tasks, grouped by reference action count (Table
2
).
Further analysis examines how adapting the reflection policy contributes to these improvements.
On ALFWorld with Qwen3.5-9B, ReTTT-QA achieves 63.57% success, compared with 56.43% when the reflection policy remains frozen while action-policy adaptation is retained (Section
3.4
).
A trajectory-level example illustrates the difference: with the same interaction history, the adapted reflector derives task-specific search guidance from failed attempts, while the frozen reflector repeats generic advice (Figure
4
).
These observations suggest that adapting reflection helps the agent derive more actionable lessons from its experience, supporting continued progress during long-horizon task execution.
Our contributions are threefold:
•
We identify a mismatch between interaction history and useful learning supervision in agentic test-time training. Quantitative comparisons examine the learning signals used during adaptation and their relationship to reliable long-horizon task execution.
•
We introduce ReTTT, which enables experience-driven self-adaptation through test-time training on lessons distilled and continually revised during task execution, with the adapted model responsible for both actions and subsequent reflection.
•
Experiments across three benchmarks and two model families demonstrate higher task success than aTTT for both ReTTT variants. Frozen-reflection comparisons and trajectory analysis further provide evidence for the benefit of adapting the policy that constructs learning experience.

3.3
Is Parametric Procedural Memory More Effective than Explicit Context Memory?
We first ask whether extracted procedural experience is better retained explicitly in context than internalized into model parameters.
To isolate this factor, we construct Context Memory, which uses the same procedural reflection and memory pipeline as ReTTT but keeps the model frozen and supplies the maintained experience only as textual context.
As shown in Figure
3(a)
, Context Memory improves success from 47.14% to 53.57%, showing that explicit procedural memory is already useful.
Parametric internalization yields substantially larger gains: ReTTT-Sum reaches 60.00% and ReTTT-QA 63.57%, with ReTTT-QA outperforming Context Memory by 10.00 points.
Thus, procedural experience is more effective when internalized into fast weights than when reused only as explicit context.

Related Work
4.1
Memory for Language Agents
Language-agent memory has evolved from explicit storage toward increasingly structured forms of experience reuse.
MemGPT
(
packer2023memgpt
)
manages external memory beyond the immediate context, while Generative Agents
(
park2023generative
)
and Reflexion
(
shinn2023reflexion
)
retain episodic experience and reflection in textual memory.
Voyager
(
wang2023voyager
)
further stores reusable executable skills, shifting memory from recalling past observations toward preserving procedural knowledge.
Recent work makes such experience more adaptive.
AdaMEM
(
zhang2026adamem
)
dynamically constructs test-time strategy memory, and ProcMEM
(
mi2026procmem
)
learns and maintains reusable procedural skills from interaction, both without online parameter updates.
Complementary approaches begin to parameterize experience: ParamMem
(
yao2026parammem
)
encodes cross-sample reflection patterns into parametric memory, while Procedural Memory Distillation
(
liu2026procedural
)
distills accumulated procedural memory into model weights during training.
ReTTT instead targets in-episode procedural internalization: interaction experience is continually reflected, maintained as explicit procedural memory, and written into episode-local fast weights while the same task is still unfolding.
4.2
Test-Time Adaptation for Language Agents
Test-time training adapts model parameters using information available only at inference time.
Early TTT methods
(
sun2020test
)
establish this paradigm for distribution adaptation, while qTTT
(
bansal2026let
)
shows that context-specific gradient updates can improve long-context utilization in LLMs.
These settings primarily adapt to a fixed test input or context.
More recent work extends parameter adaptation to interactive agents.
Agentic TTT (aTTT)
(
wang2026no
)
continually trains on the evolving agent trajectory and reweights repeated tokens to mitigate self-training drift.
ReTTT differs in how reflection, memory, and adaptation are coupled and the currently adapted policy first converts interaction history into screened and dynamically maintained procedural experience.
This experience is then internalized into fast weights shared by both action and reflection generation, so earlier updates influence not only subsequent behavior but also how later experience is interpreted and transformed into future supervision.
5
Conclusion
We introduced ReTTT, which constructs and internalizes maintained procedural
experience for in-episode test-time adaptation, with shared fast weights coupling
action and reflection in a closed loop.
Across three benchmarks and two model families, ReTTT consistently outperforms
trajectory-based agentic TTT, with larger gains on longer-horizon tasks,
highlighting the value of reliable procedural supervision and adaptive reflection.
Future work can extend ReTTT across episodes by selectively consolidating and
transferring reusable procedural experience.

Candidate screening.
Before memory reconciliation, each reflected candidate is passed through a
deterministic screening stage. We first verify that the candidate follows the
expected procedural schema, that the cited evidence refers to valid steps in
the current interaction trajectory, and that the serialized training target
satisfies the prescribed token budget.
Evidence consistency is checked using fixed rules and string-matching
predicates over the cited trajectory observations, environment feedback, and
task outcome. In particular, a candidate is not accepted merely because its
evidence identifiers are valid: procedural claims that contradict the
corresponding trajectory evidence are rejected. For example, claims about
successful progress must be supported by the associated environment feedback
rather than only by an attempted action. Screening therefore performs
rule-based evidence grounding and trajectory-consistency checks, without an
additional LLM judge, learned verifier, or entailment model. Candidates that
fail any screening condition are discarded before entering memory or
contributing to parameter updates.
