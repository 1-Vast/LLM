Abstract
Test-Time Reinforcement Learning (TTRL) enables large language models to adapt directly on unlabeled test problems by constructing pseudo-labels from multiple sampled responses. However, existing TTRL methods typically apply the same expensive sampling and optimization procedure to all test problems, despite substantial differences in their training utility. In this paper, we study whether such problems can be identified before costly test-time training, without access to ground-truth answers. We uncover a stable relationship between internal response representations and problem-level empirical accuracy. Specifically, we define COS as the cosine similarity between a model’s response representation and a correctness-related direction constructed from an independent question-answering dataset. COS consistently correlates with problem solvability across different models, datasets, and experimental settings, and we further provide a theoretical analysis explaining this relationship. Building on this finding, we propose SelectTTRL, a representation-guided data selection method that ranks test problems using COS and applies TTRL only to a selected percentile interval. SelectTTRL leaves the standard TTRL pipeline unchanged for the selected problems, while avoiding unnecessary multi-response sampling and reinforcement learning updates on the remaining ones. Experiments across multiple reasoning benchmarks demonstrate that SelectTTRL substantially reduces test-time computation while maintaining or improving the performance of standard TTRL. Code is available at
https://github.com/heyiliyi/SelectTTRL-public
.

Introduction
Large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated strong capabilities on complex reasoning tasks
(
Qwen et al., 2024
,
Yang et al., 2025
)
, yet further improving their reasoning ability often relies on additional supervision signals
(
Shao et al., 2024
,
Guo et al., 2025
)
. Test-Time Reinforcement Learning (TTRL)
(
Zuo et al., 2025
)
provides a new paradigm for adapting models directly on unlabeled test problems. Specifically, TTRL samples multiple responses for each test problem, derives pseudo-labels through majority voting, and constructs reward signals for reinforcement learning without access to the ground-truth answers. In this way, TTRL enables models to further improve their reasoning ability by exploiting the test data themselves
(
Hosseini et al., 2026
,
Yan et al., 2026
)
.
However, TTRL typically incurs substantial test-time computational overhead
(
Liu et al., 2025
)
.
Specifically, to obtain reliable training signals, each problem requires multiple inference samples followed by reinforcement learning updates. When the test set is large, repeatedly sampling and training on every problem introduces substantial additional cost. More importantly, existing TTRL methods typically allocate similar computation across problems, overlooking differences in their value for test-time training. Not every problem is equally worth optimizing. For problems the model can already solve reliably, additional sampling and updates may provide only marginal gains. In contrast, for difficult problems, repeated sampling may produce highly consistent yet incorrect answers, causing erroneous pseudo-labels to be further reinforced during training
(
Yu et al., 2026
,
Lin et al., 2026
)
. Therefore, uniformly applying the full TTRL procedure to all test problems may waste computation and allocate the limited test-time budget to problems with low training utility. This motivates a natural question:
Can we identify which test problems are worth training on before performing expensive multi-response sampling and reinforcement learning, without access to ground-truth answers?
To answer this question, we investigate whether internal model representations can provide a prior signal of test-problem solvability
(
Zou et al., 2023
,
Azaria and Mitchell, 2023
)
. Specifically, we construct a correctness-related direction from an independent question-answering dataset
(
Rimsky et al., 2024
)
. For each test problem, we then compute the cosine similarity between this direction and the model’s internal response representation. We denote this similarity as
COS
.
We observe a stable relationship between COS and problem-level empirical accuracy. Problems with higher COS generally achieve higher overall accuracy, while those with lower COS are solved less reliably. This relationship holds consistently across different models, datasets, and experimental settings. We further provide a theoretical analysis to explain why this correlation emerges. Our analysis suggests that internal representations contain information related to the relative solvability of test problems. Therefore, COS provides a representation-level signal for estimating and ranking test problems without access to their ground-truth answers.
Building on this finding, we propose
SelectTTRL
, a representation-guided data selection method for efficient test-time reinforcement learning. Before expensive multi-response sampling, SelectTTRL ranks test problems according to COS. It then selects only problems within a prescribed percentile interval for subsequent training. For the selected problems, the standard TTRL pipeline remains unchanged, including multi-response sampling, pseudo-label construction, and policy optimization. In this way, SelectTTRL avoids performing costly sampling and reinforcement learning updates over the entire test set.
Our main contributions are summarized as follows:
•
In this paper, we uncover a stable relationship between internal response representations and problem-level empirical accuracy, and formalize it as
COS
, a representation-level signal for test-problem solvability. Besides, we provide a theoretical analysis explaining why COS reflects the relative solvability of test problems.
•
Building on COS, we propose SelectTTRL. It ranks and selects test problems before the expensive multi-response sampling stage of standard TTRL, and performs subsequent reinforcement learning only on the selected subset. This reduces test-time training cost without modifying the underlying TTRL optimization pipeline.
•
Experiments across multiple reasoning benchmarks show that SelectTTRL substantially reduces test-time computation while maintaining or even improving the performance of standard TTRL.

Related Work
Test-time reinforcement learning.
Test-time reinforcement learning (TTRL) adapts language models to unlabeled test problems by constructing pseudo-supervision from their own sampled responses, typically through majority-voted answers
(
Zuo et al., 2025
)
. Existing work mainly improves TTRL from two perspectives. The first focuses on improving the learning signal within each problem. Entropy-guided sampling and contextual guidance regulate exploration during rollout generation
(
Liu et al., 2025
,
Hosseini et al., 2026
)
, while selective labeling and confidence- or consistency-based consensus aim to mitigate noisy pseudo-supervision
(
Yan et al., 2026
,
Wang et al., 2026b
,
Yu et al., 2026
,
Wang et al., 2026a
)
. These methods improve how sampled responses are generated or converted into training signals, rather than selecting a fixed subset of test problems before adaptation.
A second line of work adaptively controls whether or how a problem contributes to test-time learning. TTRL-Guard adjusts or suspends updates according to consensus dynamics
(
Lin et al., 2026
)
, while DiSCTT routes problems to pseudo-label SFT or RL based on response agreement
(
Moradi and Mudur, 2026
)
. However, such decisions are made only after multiple responses have already been sampled, meaning that much of the test-time computation has already been incurred. Moreover, response agreement does not necessarily indicate that a problem is informative for subsequent optimization. In contrast, we consider
pre-selection
: before multi-response sampling and policy updates, we rank the full set of test problems using representations obtained from an initial response, and apply standard TTRL only to the selected subset. This separates the question of
which problems to optimize on
from modifications to the TTRL learning rule itself.
Data selection for LLM training.
Data selection reduces post-training cost by identifying compact subsets that preserve or improve learning effectiveness
(
Albalak et al., 2024
)
. A broad family of methods scores examples according to properties such as quality, difficulty, uncertainty, and diversity
(
Liu et al., 2024
,
Li et al., 2024
,
Zhao et al., 2025a
)
. Other approaches estimate sample utility through gradients or influence functions. For example, LESS selects instruction-tuning examples according to their gradient influence on target tasks, but requires warm-up training and per-example gradient computation
(
Xia et al., 2024
)
. Related methods similarly exploit gradient alignment or influence estimates to identify useful training subsets
(
Zhang et al., 2025
,
Li et al., 2026
)
.
More recent work explores selection signals that can be obtained without additional trial training. AIR estimates example utility through loss changes induced by retrieval-head masking
(
Liu et al., 2026
)
, while SHIFT combines hidden-state dynamics from a single rollout with diversity-aware selection
(
Wu et al., 2026
)
. These methods reduce the overhead of estimating data utility. However, their downstream optimization still relies on externally available supervision, such as supplied reasoning traces or verifiable rewards. Our setting is different: in TTRL, neither selection nor subsequent learning has access to ground-truth correctness. We therefore study whether an internal representation signal can identify useful test problems
before
pseudo-label construction, under fully unlabeled test-time learning.
Representation engineering.
Representation engineering studies how semantic or behavioral properties are encoded in model activations and how such representations can be read or manipulated
(
Zou et al., 2023
)
. Prior work has shown that hidden states contain predictive signals associated with properties such as truthfulness and correctness
(
Azaria and Mitchell, 2023
)
, while contrastive activation addition constructs semantic directions for controlling model behavior
(
Rimsky et al., 2024
)
. Related activation-based methods intervene on internal representations to improve reasoning or elicit different reasoning behaviors
(
Højer et al., 2025
,
Tang et al., 2025
,
Li et al., 2025
)
. Sparse-autoencoder-based approaches further identify and manipulate interpretable features associated with knowledge use, semantic attributes, and reasoning strategies
(
Zhao et al., 2025b
,
He et al., 2025
,
Fang et al., 2026
)
.
Our use of representations is complementary to these intervention-oriented approaches. Rather than modifying activations during generation, we use a separately calibrated correctness-related direction as a semantic reference for measuring the representation of an initial response. The resulting cosine-similarity signal, COS, is then used to rank unlabeled test problems before TTRL begins. This extends representation-based analysis from predicting or steering model behavior to allocating test-time optimization effort across problems.

How does SelectTTRL affect model performance and training efficiency in test-time reinforcement learning?

5.2
Overall Performance (RQ1)
SelectTTRL performs TTRL on only 35% of the target questions using pretrained LLMs such as Qwen3-8B and Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct. Tables
1
and
2
compare it with full-data TTRL and alternative selection strategies. Overall, SelectTTRL retains most of the gains of full-data TTRL while using substantially fewer questions for adaptation. On MATH-500 and MMLU-Pro STEM, it performs close to Matched-TTRL despite training on only a subset of the target set.
The benefit of selective training is more pronounced on the harder AIME benchmarks. With Qwen3-8B, SelectTTRL outperforms Random and A-PPL in Greedy accuracy on both AIME 2024 and AIME 2025 while remaining competitive with Matched-TTRL. On AIME 2024, it achieves 43.33% Greedy accuracy, compared with 36.67% for Matched-TTRL and 33.33% for both Random and A-PPL. These results suggest that TTRL performance depends not only on the amount of test-time training data, but also on which questions receive the optimization budget.
We attribute this behavior to the quality of COS-based ranking. Questions with intermediate COS values tend to provide a better trade-off between optimization headroom and pseudo-label reliability: very easy questions offer limited additional learning signal, whereas extremely difficult questions are more prone to unreliable consensus rewards. SelectTTRL therefore concentrates TTRL updates on questions that are more likely to provide useful training signals.
The trend is strongest for Qwen3-8B, where COS exhibits a clearer association with question-level correctness. For Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, the ranking signal is somewhat less discriminative, and SelectTTRL performs closer to full-data TTRL. Nevertheless, the method remains competitive across both backbone LLMs, suggesting that its effectiveness does not rely on a single model-specific ranking behavior.
Notably, all results use the correctness direction calibrated from TruthfulQA. Although alternative calibration sources can yield stronger COS–accuracy correlations (Section
5.5
), the fixed TruthfulQA direction is sufficient to support effective question selection across models and benchmarks.

Dependence on the Direction-Extraction Dataset (RQ4)
To evaluate COS sensitivity to the source dataset, we extract directions from PIQA
(
Bisk et al., 2020
)

(

(

Table

six source datasets. PIQA and CommonsenseQA, on which the model achieves
87.65% and 81.08% source accuracy, yield the strongest average Spearman
correlations of 0.6273 and 0.6116, respectively. GPQA, with a lower source
accuracy of 44.95%, yields a weaker average Spearman correlation of 0.5126.
We interpret this pattern as evidence that COS captures correctness-related internal representations. When the model reliably distinguishes correct from incorrect answers on the source task, the contrastive direction better aligns with objective correctness. On harder source tasks, teacher-forcing a correct answer does not guarantee that the model internally recognizes it as correct, weakening the extracted direction and its correlation with target-task accuracy.
These observations connect to the evidence-readout analysis in
Section
4
. When the model achieves higher source-task
accuracy, the activation contrast between correct and incorrect
answers is more likely to reflect differences in internal answer evidence.
The resulting CAA direction can read this evidence in
target tasks more effectively, improving COS ranking of question-level
accuracy. This interpretation motivates choosing a source dataset the model
solves with reasonably high accuracy.
6
Conclusion
In this paper, we investigated how to improve the efficiency of Test-Time Reinforcement Learning by selecting test problems before training-time rollout collection and reinforcement learning. We identified COS, a representation-level signal that exhibited a stable relationship with problem-level empirical accuracy, and provided a theoretical analysis of this relationship. Based on this finding, we proposed SelectTTRL, which selected a subset of test problems for subsequent test-time training according to COS. Experiments across multiple reasoning benchmarks showed that SelectTTRL substantially reduced test-time computation while maintaining or improving the performance of standard TTRL. Overall, our results demonstrated the potential of internal model representations for more selective and efficient test-time adaptation.
