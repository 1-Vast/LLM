losses, but source-loss descent does not directly characterize an
update’s finite-step effect on current adversarial margins. We introduce
ExGrad ( extrapolative gradient recomposition ), which constructs
gradients at clean-input, adversarial-input, and adversarial-weight-perturbed
(AWP) states for each mini-batch. Lightweight virtual steps measure their local
margin responses, after which a globally solvable
three-variable problem selects their coefficients over a bounded affine
domain without nonnegativity constraints. Across different affine radii R R on CIFAR-10, negative clean coefficients rapidly become predominant, and
their magnitude increases with R R . Cross-zero
interventions show that setting this coefficient to zero is insufficient:
mild subtraction further improves PGD-20, while stronger subtraction
trades clean accuracy for additional PGD-20. In CIFAR-10 WRN-28-4 controls,
none of the three tested candidate-removal variants matches full ExGrad,
showing that subtraction requires both adversarial directions in this setting. Projection
diagnostics show that a negative clean coordinate usually coexists with
a positive net clean-axis projection, although both decrease as the
radius grows. Norm-matched controls further indicate that update
magnitude alone does not explain the moderate-radius gains. Experiments
on CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, and ImageNet-100 show radius-dependent
improvements over AWP. On CIFAR-100, ExGrad with R = 0.8 R=0.8 improves all
five reported metrics, including clean and AutoAttack accuracy by 3.84 3.84 and 1.75 1.75 percentage points, respectively. On ImageNet-100, the same
radius improves all four robust metrics over both AWP and PGD-AT. These
results identify explicit clean-gradient subtraction as a
response-revealed update preference whose strength and clean–robust

decision geometry. For an input x x with label y y , define the signed
classification margin m θ ​ ( x , y ) = f y ​ ( x , θ ) − max k ≠ y ⁡ f k ​ ( x , θ ) , m_{\theta}(x,y)=f_{y}(x;\theta)-\max_{k\neq y}f_{k}(x;\theta), where f k ​ ( x , θ ) f_{k}(x;\theta) is the logit of class k k . A robust model must preserve a
sufficient margin not only at the clean input but throughout the allowed input
neighborhood ( Szegedy et al. 2014 ; Goodfellow et al. 2015 ; Madry et al. 2018 ) .
Decision-boundary distance and local loss linearity provide complementary
geometric views of this neighborhood ( Moosavi-Dezfooli et al. 2016 ; Qin et al. 2019 ) .
Adversarial training can therefore be viewed as repeatedly reshaping this
local geometry through parameter updates. Most robust-training procedures obtain an update directly from a predefined
surrogate loss. A gradient 𝐠 = ∇ θ L ​ ( θ ) \mathbf{g}=\nabla_{\theta}L(\theta) is the first-order
sensitivity of its source loss: moving along − 𝐠 -\mathbf{g} locally reduces L L .
This source-loss descent sensitivity, however, is not the same quantity as the
response of a target adversarial margin. For a small step, m θ ′ ​ ( x , y ) − m θ ​ ( x , y ) \displaystyle m_{\theta^{\prime}}(x,y)-m_{\theta}(x,y) ≈ − η ⁡ ⟨ ∇ θ m θ ​ ( x , y ) , 𝐠 ⟩ , \displaystyle\approx-\eta\langle\nabla_{\theta}m_{\theta}(x,y),\mathbf{g}\rangle, θ ′ \displaystyle\theta^{\prime} = θ − η ​ 𝐠 . \displaystyle=\theta-\eta\mathbf{g}. A direction may reduce the loss from which it was derived yet provide limited
margin improvement, or even compress the margin of some adversarial examples.
The locally preferred update is consequently determined by its effect on the
target geometry, not by its source label alone. This mismatch is not resolved by tuning a surrogate-loss coefficient. Such a
coefficient controls how source objectives generate an update, but it does not
report how that update changes the adversarial margin of the current
mini-batch. Gradient norms and pairwise angles are also insufficient for this
purpose: they describe the candidate directions relative to one another,
rather than their effects on the target geometry. This motivates treating a
gradient as a candidate parameter intervention whose consequence should be
measured before it is selected. This distinction becomes actionable when several training states already
provide plausible directions. We consider gradients evaluated on clean inputs,
adversarial inputs, and adversarial inputs under adversarial weight
perturbation (AWP), denoted by 𝐠 clean \mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{clean}} , 𝐠 adv \mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{adv}} , and 𝐠 awp \mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{awp}} .
The first captures nominal fitting, the second input-neighborhood robust
fitting, and the third the correction induced by a perturbed parameter
neighborhood ( Wu et al. 2020 ) . Their effects can complement or cancel
one another and can change with the mini-batch and training stage. Fixed loss
weights, gradient norms, or pairwise angles do not directly measure these
effects on the adversarial margin. These candidates are not three unrelated tasks. They form a nested sequence
of training states: clean input at nominal weights, adversarial input at
nominal weights, and adversarial input at perturbed weights. Each transition
adds a robustness-relevant state change, while the adversarial margin supplies
a common criterion for comparing all three candidate updates. We introduce ExGrad, short for extrapolative gradient recomposition .
For every mini-batch, ExGrad applies a lightweight virtual update along each
candidate gradient from the same nominal parameters, measures the resulting
finite-step adversarial-margin response, restores the parameters, and solves
for a single recomposed update. \mathbf{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}&=1,\qquad\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{2}\leq R.\end{aligned}\right. The unit-sum constraint defines an affine combination, while the radius R R bounds its departure from balanced recomposition. Unlike a nonnegative loss
mixture, this domain lets the measured response suppress, remove, or subtract
a candidate direction. The coefficients are therefore determined from the
measured responses rather than prescribed in advance. Probing every candidate from the same nominal parameters makes the measured
responses directly comparable. The virtual parameters are discarded after
measurement, so only the recomposed direction is committed to the optimizer.
This finite-step construction evaluates the consequence of an update in the
units used by training, without requiring an analytic derivative through the
adversarial-example or weight-perturbation construction. The measured dynamics reveal a persistent negative clean coefficient. A
majority of mini-batches already select β clean < 0 \beta_{\mathrm{clean}}<0 at the
beginning of training, the proportion rapidly approaches one, and some early
mini-batches remain positive. This behavior is stronger than simply assigning
the clean direction a small weight: it crosses the zero-clean boundary into
explicit subtraction within the selected affine representation. A controlled
intervention confirms that setting β clean = 0 \beta_{\mathrm{clean}}=0 does not
reproduce the same robustness profile as a mild negative value. The zero point therefore marks the boundary between clean-gradient removal and
explicit affine subtraction. A positive coefficient retains clean descent, zero
removes it from the affine representation, and a negative coefficient
explicitly subtracts it. In the intervention, the adversarial and AWP
coefficients remain response-adaptive, isolating whether suppression is
sufficient or crossing into subtraction helps. The affine coordinate must nevertheless be distinguished from the complete
update geometry. Because 𝐠 adv \mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{adv}} and 𝐠 awp \mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{awp}} can also project onto 𝐠 clean \mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{clean}} , a
negative β clean \beta_{\mathrm{clean}} does not imply that 𝐠 𝜷 \mathbf{g}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}} has a

Our contributions are: • We separate source-loss descent sensitivity from finite-step
adversarial-margin response and use the latter to select robust-training
updates. • We propose a lightweight, convex, batch-wise coefficient solver over
the bounded affine span of clean, adversarial-input, and AWP gradients. • We identify explicit clean-gradient subtraction, intervene across
its zero boundary, and distinguish its affine coordinate from the net
clean-axis projection. • We use multi-seed cross-dataset evaluation and norm-matched controls

beyond it to permit response-supported subtraction. Related Work Robust objectives and instance regulation. PGD-AT approximates input-space robust optimization ( Madry et al. 2018 ) , while TRADES regulates natural and boundary errors
through an objective-level coefficient ( Zhang et al. 2019 ) .
Robust generalization can require additional data ( Schmidt et al. 2018 ; Carmon et al. 2019 ) , while standard learning may
exploit predictive but non-robust features ( Ilyas et al. 2019 ) .
MART, fast training, consistency regularization, and robust-overfitting studies
modify the objective, attack construction, regularization, or schedule ( Wang et al. 2020 ; Wong et al. 2020 ; Rice et al. 2020 ; Tack et al. 2022 ) ;
geometry-aware, margin-aware, and hard-class-aware methods reweight examples ( Zhang et al. 2021 ; Wang et al. 2021 ; Hou et al. 2023 ) .
Together, these works expose the clean–robust trade-off ( Tsipras et al. 2019 ) , but regulate how source losses generate an
update. ExGrad instead measures how already-constructed candidate updates
change the current adversarial margin. Parameter-neighborhood robustness. AWP constructs a worst-case perturbation in a constrained weight neighborhood
and updates the nominal model using the perturbed-weight adversarial gradient ( Wu et al. 2020 ) . This complements the input neighborhood of PGD-AT
with parameter-neighborhood information. Sharpness-aware minimization provides
a broader parameter-neighborhood perspective ( Foret et al. 2021 ) , and
robust-loss flatness has been linked to robust generalization ( Stutz et al. 2021 ) . Analyses of SAM further emphasize optimization-induced
implicit bias ( Andriushchenko and Flammarion 2022 ) . ExGrad retains the
standard AWP state as a candidate rather than redefining its objective or
prescribing its weight. Gradient recomposition and conflict. Multi-objective optimization and multi-task learning have developed
controls based on Pareto stationarity, rate balancing, conflict projection,
and worst-task improvement ( Sener and Koltun 2018 ; Chen et al. 2018 ; Yu et al. 2020 ; Liu et al. 2021 ) .
Conflict-aware adversarial training similarly studies interference between
clean and adversarial gradients ( Xue et al. 2024 ) . These methods
use source-gradient geometry or joint descent conditions. ExGrad instead
evaluates different input–parameter states against one margin target, permits
negative coefficients, and does not require every source loss to descend. Virtual updates and margin signals. Virtual optimization steps have been used to learn example weights from a
validation objective ( Ren et al. 2018 ; Shu et al. 2019 ) . Task-affinity methods similarly
measure how one task’s step changes another task’s loss ( Fifty et al. 2021 ) . Margin-based attacks and training objectives
characterize robustness through shortest input-space distances to a decision
boundary ( Moosavi-Dezfooli et al. 2016 ; Ding et al. 2020 ) or class-logit margins within
a perturbation set ( Gao et al. 2022 ) .
Without a validation set, ExGrad applies this response logic to restored probes
of the same mini-batch margin, then solves a low-dimensional affine problem.

explicit subtraction, and response adaptation. PGD-20 rises as β clean \beta_{\mathrm{clean}} moves from 0.1 0.1 through zero to − 0.2 -0.2 , while the
fixed mean triplet recovers much, but not all, of the adaptive gain. This
reflects a response-preferred joint composition and a clean–robust
operating-point shift, not evidence that clean fitting is globally harmful;
nonnegative weighting cannot express this cross-zero regime. Coefficient and projection signs describe different geometries: candidate
correlation means that β clean < 0 \beta_{\mathrm{clean}}<0 need not imply p clean < 0 p_{\mathrm{clean}}<0 . Updates at R = 0.8 R=0.8 and 1.0 1.0 usually retain a positive
clean-axis projection; only some late R = 1.2 R=1.2 mini-batches reverse it. ExGrad
therefore establishes coordinate subtraction broadly and complete clean-axis
reversal more selectively. The response objective is attack dependent: larger R R favors PGD and APGD-CE,
while moderate radii transfer better to APGD-T and AutoAttack. The local oracle
also omits momentum trajectories, targeted-attack candidates, and alternative
parameter-neighborhood directions. Multi-attack probes, cheaper response
estimators, and broader candidate states are natural extensions. Conclusion ExGrad selects a bounded affine gradient recomposition from finite-step
adversarial-margin responses. The solver repeatedly selects a negative clean
coefficient; cross-zero controls show that removal is insufficient and mild
subtraction helps when the adversarial-input and AWP directions remain.
Suitable radii improve robustness over AWP on CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, and
ImageNet-100. Increasing R R lowers both β clean \beta_{\mathrm{clean}} and p clean p_{\mathrm{clean}} , suppressing the realized clean-axis component and
shifting the clean–PGD operating point.

not prescribed, update preference. They expose an update-level geometry that
