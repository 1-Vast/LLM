Abstract
LLM-driven evolution enables iterative model development, but two practical goals remain underexplored: finding model designs that transfer across related tasks and sustaining improvement when training is expensive.
We introduce
Population Evolution (PE)
, a collaborative, hierarchical framework that connects ongoing local searches through shared experimental evidence.
PE evaluates code changes across related training instances and shares the results to guide subsequent proposals and promotion to larger training scales.
For expensive targets, PE searches small training subsets and screens candidates through peer and intermediate evaluations before full-target training.
We introduce
RMD-Bench
to evaluate both settings across ranking, watch-time prediction, RL algorithm discovery, and LLM/VLM pretraining.
Compared with standalone evolution at matched source iterations, PE raises mean best local gains from 7.01% to 8.97% in ranking and from 2.84% to 3.85% in watch-time, while improving the best larger-scale outcome in all three joint-discovery families.
In watch-time discovery, PE improves best larger-scale gains with four of five harnesses and all four proposers.
On new recommendation datasets under shared target-side calibration, every evaluated PE design improves over the reference in mean performance.
Under matched total GPU compute, completed LLM discovery runs yield a best relative accuracy gain of 2.48% and 13 successful candidates for PE, versus 0.92% and none for direct evolution.
VLM loss reduction reaches 8.78% versus 5.05% under matched total GPU compute.

Introduction
LLM-driven evolution automates model development by proposing code changes, evaluating them, and using the results to guide further edits
(
Liu et al., 2024
;
Novikov et al., 2025
;
Lange et al., 2025
)
.
Related research agents automate idea generation and experimentation
(
Lu et al., 2024
)
, while neural architecture discovery offers a route toward recursive self-improvement
(
Pepe et al., 2026
)
.
Despite this progress, two goals remain underexplored in LLM-driven neural model discovery: discovering universal model designs across related tasks and sustaining improvement when candidate training is expensive.
Collaborative model discovery within a domain.
Related datasets often share modeling requirements, so a mechanism discovered on one can inform development on another.
Independent single-task searches primarily use their own feedback
(
Novikov et al., 2025
;
Lange et al., 2025
)
, leaving this evidence isolated.
Connecting these searches can support both local improvement and
universal model designs
: a common architecture or training mechanism used across related instances, with parameters trained separately on each.
Sustaining discovery on computationally demanding tasks.
When every candidate requires substantial computation, as in full LLM or VLM training, a fixed budget permits few proposal–evaluation cycles.
Parallel full-target searches broaden exploration, but costly evaluations limit how many revisions each search can make
(
Novikov et al., 2025
)
.
Sustained discovery therefore requires affordable experiments that inform continued development, followed by verification under full-target training.
We introduce
Population Evolution (PE)
, which connects ongoing local searches through a shared ledger of executable interventions and cross-instance evaluation results (Figure
1
).
Each source retains its objective and archive; shared feedback guides local edits, while replay lets other instances reuse its discoveries.
Cross-instance replay tests the same intervention after retraining under each receiving instance’s protocol.
In
joint discovery
, searches on related datasets or training configurations reuse, adapt, and combine mechanisms; replay and larger-scale evaluation establish where the resulting designs remain useful.
In
decomposed discovery
, small subsets of an expensive target’s training data provide affordable search instances, with peer and intermediate evaluations directing candidates toward full-target training.
This hierarchy supports iterative refinement while reserving full-target training for screened candidates.
We introduce the
Recursive Model Discovery Benchmark (RMD-Bench)
, providing executable evaluation protocols for five task families spanning both settings.
Compatible interfaces and fixed references support evaluation of local gains and transfer in joint discovery, and full-target quality and yield in decomposed discovery.
All three joint-discovery families also test transfer to new datasets or disjoint coding tasks.
We start from FuXi-Linear
(
Ye et al., 2026
)
for ranking, EGMN
(
Zhao et al., 2025
)
for watch-time prediction, GRPO
(
Shao et al., 2024
)
for RL algorithm discovery, Gemma 3-style architectures
(
Gemma Team, 2025
)
for LLM pretraining, and Qwen3.5-style architectures
(
Qwen Team, 2026
)
for VLM pretraining.
A complete LLM or VLM discovery run, including five searches and their evaluations, requires thousands of GPU-hours.
At matched source iterations, PE improves the mean per-source best gain in both recommendation families and the best larger-scale outcome in all three joint-discovery families.
Every evaluated PE recommendation design improves over the reference mean on new datasets.
Retraining uses shared target-side calibration without candidate-specific tuning.
For LLM pretraining, completed runs under matched total GPU compute yield a 2.48% best relative accuracy gain and 13 successful candidates for PE, versus 0.92% and none for direct evolution.
For VLM pretraining, best loss reduction rises from 5.05% to 8.78% under matched total GPU compute.
Our contributions are:
•
Population Evolution:
a collaborative and hierarchical framework that uses shared experimental feedback to guide local search, reuses executable interventions across instances, and promotes candidates across training scales.
•
RMD-Bench:
five executable task families with compatible code interfaces, shared reference implementations, and controlled cross-instance and cross-scale evaluation, including transfer to new datasets and tasks in all three Track A families.
•
Model discoveries and transfer:
recommendation designs that improve over shared references on new datasets, better larger-scale outcomes in all three Track A families, and successful full-target LLM/VLM interventions, with watch-time comparisons across harnesses and proposers.

Sharing and evaluating candidates across instances
When a candidate meets its source’s configured improvement criteria, PE publishes its executable intervention, mechanism description, provenance, and score to the shared ledger.
The other source instances are its

2.3
Promoting candidates to larger training scales
Shared results also govern promotion: published candidates must meet the configured improvement criteria on at least two of their four peers in our experiments (Appendix
B.1
).
In
joint discovery (Track A)
, eligible candidates are retrained on a larger-scale instance. Peer and larger-scale results return to the ledger, supporting local improvement and identifying designs useful across related instances (Figure
1
(b)).
In
decomposed discovery (Track B)
, source searches run on affordable training subsets. Eligible candidates are tested on two intermediate instances; those passing both advance to full-target training (Figure
1
(c)). Peer and intermediate results inform further proposals, while full-target scores and pass/fail outcomes are withheld from proposal context.
This is intended to prevent a few full-target successes from prematurely steering all source searches toward similar designs.
A candidate
graduates
when it passes the final stage: larger-scale evaluation in Track A or full-target evaluation in Track B.
Local searches continue while these evaluations run asynchronously.
3
RMD-Bench: Tasks and Evaluation
RMD-Bench defines five task families with objectives, reference implementations, editable components, and executable training/evaluation protocols.
Compatible interfaces support retraining across related instances. The 34 core instances vary data, training seed, or scale; Track A also evaluates transfer beyond discovery instances.
The benchmark fixes evaluation conditions within each instance; each discovery system chooses its search, sharing, and selection policies.

3.1
Joint discovery (Track A)
Track A tests local improvement and discovery of universal model designs useful across related tasks.
Discovery instances.
Ranking, watch-time prediction, and RL algorithm discovery each have five source instances and one larger-scale instance (18 instances in total; Figure
1
(b)).
Within each recommendation family, the five sources use different datasets; watch-time includes CIKM16 and WeChat. Both families use KuaiRand-27K for larger-scale evaluation.
Ranking edits sequence representations and temporal interactions in FuXi-Linear
(
Ye et al., 2026
)
, using the mean of NDCG@10, HR@10, and MRR as its score.
Watch-time discovery edits EGMN’s distributional head and point-prediction rule
(
Zhao et al., 2025
)
, using MAE in seconds.
RL discovery edits the GRPO loss
(
Shao et al., 2024
)
and rollout filtering for Qwen2.5-Coder policies
(
Hui et al., 2024
)
.
The five RL sources train 1.5B policies on the same task with different seeds; the larger-scale instance trains a 3B policy and evaluates pass@1 on a 423-problem LiveCodeBench partition
(
Jain et al., 2024
)
.
Appendices
A.1
–
A.3
specify the three protocols.
Transfer beyond discovery instances.
Track A evaluates designs beyond the discovery instances: KuaiVideo for ranking, VK-LSVD for watch-time prediction, and 632 LiveCodeBench tasks disjoint from the larger-scale selection partition for RL.
The two recommendation datasets are excluded from source search, peer evaluation, and promotion. KuaiVideo also changes the objective from next-item ranking to candidate-conditioned click-through rate (CTR) prediction.
Recommendation retraining uses a shared configuration calibrated and scored on each target’s released test split, without candidate-specific redesign or tuning. RL evaluation uses held-out coding tasks.
These evaluations supplement the 34 core instances; Appendix
F
specifies selection, calibration, and reporting.
3.2
Decomposed discovery (Track B)
Track B evaluates whether discovery on small training subsets produces effective model and training designs for an expensive full target within a compute budget.
Training hierarchy.
LLM and VLM pretraining each have five source instances, two intermediate instances, and one full-target instance (16 instances in total; Figure
1
(c)).
The hierarchy tests transfer across training subsets and training scales before committing to expensive full-target evaluation.
The sources include three disjoint 5% subsets, D5-A/B/C. D2-A and D2-B each contain 2% of the target corpus and are nested in D5-A and D5-B, respectively.
Disjoint peers test whether gains persist beyond the source subset; the nested 2%/5% instances test transfer across data volumes and numbers of training steps.
Two approximately 10% intermediate instances test different forms of transfer: D10 clean uses data disjoint from every source, while D10+C combines D5-C with a fresh 5% subset.
Requiring success on both is intended to prioritize designs whose gains persist under full-target training.
Full-target evaluation uses 100% of the training corpus. The same executable intervention is evaluated at each tier under its prescribed training configuration.
Pretraining tasks.
LLM discovery starts from a 185M-parameter Gemma 3-style decoder
(
Gemma Team, 2025
)
and can modify its architecture, optimizer, and training mechanisms.
Training uses the FineWeb-Edu 100B core set
(
Lozhkov et al., 2024
;
Karpathy, 2025
)
.
The score is mean accuracy over the seven zero-shot tasks used in Mamba-2
(
Dao & Gu, 2024
)
: ARC-Challenge, ARC-Easy, HellaSwag, LAMBADA OpenAI, OpenBookQA, PIQA, and WinoGrande.
VLM discovery edits a Qwen3.5-style backbone
(
Qwen Team, 2026
)
, projector, optimizer, and schedule, retaining a frozen SigLIP encoder
(
Zhai et al., 2023
)
.
The starting VLM has approximately 646M trainable parameters; full-target training uses 2.38M raw FineVisionMax-format examples
(
Wiedmann et al., 2025
)
. The score is token-weighted validation cross-entropy.
Appendices
A.4
and
A.5
give the full specifications.

Experiments
Experiments test local discovery and transfer in Track A, and full-target quality and completed-run yield in Track B. Section

4.1
Experimental setup and comparisons
Shared protocols.
The main experiments use the AdaEvolve-based search harness
(
Cemri et al., 2026
)
with GPT-5.5 at high reasoning effort as the proposer (Appendix
B
).
Methods share starting models, reference configurations, editable interfaces, instructions, and training/evaluation protocols (Appendix
A.6
). Gains use fixed instance references (Equation
2
).
Section
4.4
varies the harness and proposer in watch-time discovery.
Track A: independent and collaborative discovery.
Standalone evolution (S) runs independent source searches using the same harness and parent-selection rules as PE, without shared context. Both methods run five 100-iteration searches. Table
2
A–C compares locally proposed candidates with and without shared feedback; peer-replayed candidates are reported separately (Section
5.1
).
Local recommendation results average the best gain from each dataset; RL uses the best result across five searches of the same task.
At larger scale, S retrains each source’s top candidates by source performance, while PE retrains promoted candidates. S’s per-source quota is rounded up to give it at least as many larger-scale evaluations as PE (Table
2
D).
Neither method searches locally at larger scale.
Transfer evaluations use shared target protocols without candidate-specific redesign or tuning (Appendix
F.1
).
Track B: hierarchical discovery under a compute budget.
PE screens candidates from five subset searches at intermediate scale before full training; direct evolution (Direct) runs five full-target searches. Completed runs use matched total GPU compute within each family. PE proposals use only source and intermediate feedback; Direct uses full-target feedback (Appendix
B.5
).
4.2
Joint discovery improves local quality and generalization
We examine local gains, transfer within the discovery hierarchy, and transfer to new datasets or tasks.
Shared feedback improves mean best local recommendation gains.
At the matched source allocation, PE raises mean best local gains from 7.01% to 8.97% in ranking and 2.84% to 3.85% in watch-time, exceeding S on 3/5 and 4/5 datasets, respectively (Table
2
).
PE’s mean gains over calibrated starting models are non-trivial in magnitude, comparable to published improvements over the strongest reported baselines on public datasets: 0.71–12.15% in NDCG@10 for FuXi-Linear
(
Ye et al., 2026
)
and 1.72–5.04% in MAE for EGMN
(
Zhao et al., 2025
)
.
The best RL source gain rises from 23.53% to 32.35% (Table
2
C).
Selected designs improve larger-scale outcomes.
After larger-scale retraining without local search, PE yields more successful evaluations than S and raises best gains from 4.70% to 6.19% in ranking, 1.77% to 2.17% in watch-time, and 1.95% to 6.64% in RL (Table
2
D).
These larger-scale instances participate in discovery; we next evaluate transfer to new datasets and tasks.

7
Conclusion
Population Evolution connects searches through shared evidence and hierarchical promotion; RMD-Bench supplies executable evaluation protocols.
Shared feedback improves mean best local recommendation gains; peer replay enables reuse. PE improves best larger-scale outcomes, and its recommendation designs transfer to new datasets.
Under matched total GPU compute, hierarchical discovery improves full-target LLM/VLM quality and completed-run yield.
Watch-time best larger-scale gains improve with four of five harnesses and all four proposers.

Publishing a candidate.
After a candidate meets its source instance’s publication margin (Appendix

The record identifies the source instance and iteration, reports its score and reference baseline, and tracks the candidate’s evaluation results and current status.
The record keeps executable code distinct from the mechanism description: peer evaluation executes the code, while proposal context summarizes the mechanism and its measured outcomes.
Evaluating the intervention on peers.
For each peer, the coordinator restores that instance’s reference implementation, applies the published intervention, and runs the peer’s prescribed training and evaluation.
Model parameters are trained separately under the receiving instance’s protocol.

Each completed evaluation adds the receiving instance, score, gain, and pass decision to the candidate’s record.
Valid weak or near-neutral results remain available even when they do not meet a promotion margin.

Appendix F
Transfer to New Datasets and Coding Tasks
F.1
Candidate selection and evaluation protocol
This appendix specifies the three transfer evaluations in Section
3.1
, testing the designs returned by each complete discovery procedure beyond its discovery instances.
Watch-time heads transfer to VK-LSVD; ranking designs transfer to KuaiVideo with a change from full-catalog next-item prediction to candidate-conditioned click prediction; RL modifications are evaluated on a task partition disjoint from the 423-task larger-scale selection set.
The two recommendation datasets were excluded from source search, peer evaluation, and larger-scale promotion.
Candidate selection and evaluation.
Each procedure supplies candidates through its own selection rule. Each discovered intervention is evaluated without candidate-specific redesign or hyperparameter tuning, using a shared target protocol.
For watch-time, PE contributes designs that passed larger-scale evaluation, and S contributes the best source candidate from each of five datasets.
For ranking, PE likewise contributes designs that passed larger-scale evaluation, and S contributes the top four source candidates from each of five datasets.
RL compares two successful PE candidates and the best S candidate from each of five source searches.
Recommendation weights are trained from scratch on each new dataset; RL policies start from the same pretrained model and follow a fixed training protocol before evaluation on held-out coding tasks.
Target calibration and reporting.
Each recommendation target uses one baseline-calibrated configuration shared by the reference and every candidate, including the training duration.
Baseline hyperparameter and training-duration selection use the released test split, which is also used for final reporting; no separate validation split is used for these selection decisions.
The recommendation results therefore measure transfer to new datasets under shared target-side calibration, rather than an independent test evaluation after calibration.
Candidate-specific HPO and evolutionary adaptation on these targets are disabled.
The RL reference uses the fixed, unmodified training configuration without target-side HPO; the 632 coding tasks are disjoint from the larger-scale selection partition.
Recommendation references and candidates are trained with three seeds; RL references and candidates undergo three independent stochastic reruns with the same nominal training seed.
We report means and sample standard deviations over these three runs. These repetitions assess candidate performance rather than variability across complete discovery runs.
AUC, gAUC, and pass@1 are expressed as percentages; MAE is in seconds. Means and standard deviations use two decimal places, except log loss, which uses four. All comparisons use the original precision.

Appendix G
Limitations and Future Directions
RMD-Bench covers five task families, with compatible intervention interfaces within each family.
The transfer results apply to the architectures, training protocols, and evaluation settings studied here; extending PE to less compatible tasks may require additional interface design.
Track A evaluates design generalization, whereas Track B targets improvement on a specified expensive workload.
The source-level comparison tests shared experimental feedback; larger-scale and full-target results evaluate the complete discovery procedures.
Given the cost of full runs, further independent searches would help characterize variability in discovered designs and final performance (Appendix
B.5
).
Future work can also examine alternative exchange and promotion policies and extend the benchmark to additional task families.
