Antonin Berthon 1 1 footnotemark: 1 Nicolás Astorga Mihaela van der Schaar Affiliation: University of Cambridge Affiliation: Cambridge, UK Email: {ak2680,armb3}@cam.ac.uk Abstract Despite their extensive pretraining knowledge and strong reasoning capabilities, large language models (LLMs) struggle to adaptively gather information in experimental design tasks. We study how to amortize sequential Bayesian experimental design (BED) into an LLM by explicitly training the reasoning that guides action selection using information-gain rewards. Our approach specifies the experimental generative model independently of the LLM’s own beliefs, allowing us to optimize expected information gain under a model of our choosing. We investigate the design choices for amortization, including alternative generative-model constructions, reward estimation, hypothesis-space specification, multi-step objectives, and computational costs relative to training- and inference-time BED baselines. On Twenty Questions, the resulting policies improve information acquisition and task performance, outperform inference- and training-time BED baselines at substantially lower computational cost, and generalize to unseen categories. We further demonstrate positive transfer from Twenty Questions training to Medi-Q, a real-world clinical dialogue benchmark. These results suggest that information gathering can be learned as a generalizable reasoning skill in LLMs. 1 Introduction Many real-world problems are not fully specified upfront, yet humans are remarkably effective at gathering the information needed to solve them ( Li et al., 2024 ; Huang et al., 2024 ) . Consider a clinician diagnosing a patient. They draw on their medical knowledge to ask about relevant symptoms, think about possible diagnoses, and identify tests that can distinguish between them. As new evidence is acquired, they refine their hypotheses to decide what to investigate next. Effective information gathering thus requires both the prior knowledge to inform decision-making and the ability to continuously adapt those decisions in light of new evidence. Given their extensive pretraining knowledge and strong reasoning capabilities, large language models (LLMs) are increasingly being explored as agents in adaptive decision-making ( Zhou et al., 2025a ; Ashokkumar et al., 2026 ) . Despite this potential, LLMs have been shown to perform poorly in multi-turn settings ( Krishnamurthy et al., 2024 ; Zhou et al., 2025b ; Li et al., 2026 ; Laban et al., 2026 ) . In particular, Gupta et al. (2025) demonstrate that LLMs are insensitive to experimental feedback, suggesting that their performance relies more on their (potentially inaccurate) prior knowledge than on their ability to adapt their reasoning to new evidence. In this work, we investigate how to improve LLMs’ ability to adaptively gather information . Rather than using an LLM to sample informative actions ( Kobalczyk et al., 2025 ; Choudhury et al., 2025 ; Hartmann et al., 2026 ) , we hypothesize that learning generalizable information gathering strategies requires explicitly training the reasoning process that guides action selection. We formalize this problem through sequential Bayesian experimental design (BED) Rainforth et al. (2023) , which uses a generative model of the experiment to select actions that reduce uncertainty about an unknown quantity of interest. We investigate how to amortize this information-theoretic objective into an LLM reasoning policy using reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR). Crucially, training-time amortization allows us to specify the experimental generative model independently of the LLM’s own beliefs. This means that we can evaluate actions based on their expected information gain under a model of our choosing. We additionally investigate whether optimizing information gain over multiple interactions encourages non-myopic information-gathering strategies. We empirically show that amortizing BED into a reasoning LLM develops strategies that generalize to previously unseen domains and tasks. Specifically, we train reasoning LLMs on Twenty Questions and demonstrate improved information acquisition across both seen and unseen categories. We show that models trained in this environment exhibit positive transfer to the real-world clinical dialogue benchmark Medi-Q ( Li et al., 2024 ) . Our results show that decoupling the experimental generative model from the LLM’s beliefs improves information acquisition. We further find that rewarding expected information-gain rewards under our specified generative model improves learning relative to realized rewards, and that optimizing information acquisition over future interactions can improve sequential information gathering in non-myopic environments. To gain information about θ \theta , we can select an experimental design x x , which results in an observation y y . For example, in a diagnostic setting, θ \theta may represent a patient’s underlying condition that we want to identify, x x a diagnostic test, and y y the resulting test outcome. Because experiments may be subject to constraints such as cost or time, the chosen design x x should be as informative as possible. BED provides a framework for quantifying how informative a potential design x x is using a generative model of the experiment, p ⁡ ( θ , y ∣ x ) = p ⁡ ( θ ) ​ p ​ ( y ∣ θ , x ) p(\theta,y\mid x)=p(\theta)p(y\mid\theta,x) . Here, the prior p ⁡ ( θ ) p(\theta) represents our initial beliefs about θ \theta and the likelihood p ⁡ ( y ∣ θ , x ) p(y\mid\theta,x) describes the probability of observing y y given θ \theta and x x . After selecting x x and observing y y , these beliefs are updated through the posterior p ⁡ ( θ ∣ y , x ) p(\theta\mid y,x) . The informativeness of a potential design x x is then quantified by its expected information gain (EIG): EIG ⁡ ( x ) \displaystyle\mathrm{EIG}(x) = 𝔼 p ⁡ ( θ ) ​ p ​ ( y ∣ θ , x ) ​ [ log ⁡ p ⁡ ( θ ∣ y , x ) − log ⁡ p ⁡ ( θ ) ] \displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{p(\theta)p(y\mid\theta,x)}\left[\log p(\theta\mid y,x)-\log p(\theta)\right] (1) = 𝔼 p ⁡ ( θ ) ​ p ​ ( y ∣ θ , x ) ​ [ log ⁡ p ⁡ ( y ∣ θ , x ) − log ⁡ p ⁡ ( y ∣ x ) ] , \displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{p(\theta)p(y\mid\theta,x)}\left[\log p(y\mid\theta,x)-\log p(y\mid x)\right], (2) which measures how much information design x x will provide about θ \theta , in expectation over possible θ \theta and observations y y . Using Bayes’ rule, the EIG can be seen as either the expected reduction in uncertainty about θ \theta after observing y y (Eq. 1 ) or the expected reduction in uncertainty about y y from knowing θ \theta (Eq. 2 ). The optimal one-step design is defined as x ⋆ = arg ⁡ max x ∈ 𝒳 ⁡ EIG ⁡ ( x ) x^{\star}=\arg\max_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\mathrm{EIG}(x) , where 𝒳 \mathcal{X} denotes the space of designs. This formulation extends naturally to the sequential setting where the design at step t t is chosen based on the history of previous experiments, h t − 1 = ( x 1 , y 1 , … , x t − 1 , y t − 1 ) h_{t-1}=(x_{1},y_{1},\ldots,x_{t-1},y_{t-1}) by maximizing the EIG under the updated belief p ⁡ ( θ ∣ h t − 1 ) p(\theta\mid h_{t-1}) . The experimental design at step t t can then be selected as x t ⋆ = arg ⁡ max x ∈ 𝒳 ⁡ EIG ⁡ ( x , h t − 1 ) x_{t}^{\star}=\arg\max_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\mathrm{EIG}(x;h_{t-1}) . Challenges of Design Selection. Selecting designs according to the EIG in adaptive settings presents two major challenges. First, optimizing the EIG requires a costly estimation of the posterior p ( θ ∣ x 1 : t , y 1 : t ) p(\theta\mid x_{1:t},y_{1:t}) at each step, which depends on the observed interaction history and therefore cannot be computed in advance. Moreover, even given this posterior, calculating the EIG is doubly intractable: it involves an outer expectation over possible latent states and observations, and an inner marginalization over latent states to compute the posterior predictive distribution p ⁡ ( y t ∣ x t , h t − 1 ) p(y_{t}\mid x_{t},h_{t-1}) . Repeating this estimation and optimization procedure after every observation can be prohibitively expensive for settings that require decisions to be made in real time. Second, one-step EIG optimization considers only the immediate next design. This can be suboptimal in non-myopic decision states, where acquiring more information in the future requires first selecting a design with limited immediate information gain. 2.2 Amortizing Sequential Experimental Design To address the computational cost of optimizing the EIG at inference time, Deep Adaptive Design (DAD, Foster et al. (2021) ) amortizes sequential experimental design into a design policy π ϕ ​ ( x t ∣ h t − 1 ) \pi_{\phi}(x_{t}\mid h_{t-1}) , which maps the history h t − 1 h_{t-1} to the next design x t x_{t} . This shifts the computational cost to training, allowing the learned policy to directly select the next design at inference time without EIG optimization. Under this design-policy perspective, the standard sequential BED procedure described above corresponds to an implicit policy π S \pi_{S} , which selects each design by updating the posterior and optimizing the one-step EIG ( Foster et al., 2021 ) . Amortizing this policy directly would therefore require repeatedly simulating this expensive sequential BED procedure to generate training targets. Instead, DAD observes that the total EIG of a sequential experiment is equal to the sum of its conditional EIGs across steps. Summing these conditional information gains gives the information gained between the initial prior and the posterior after the complete trajectory. BED-based information gathering with LLMs has been studied through inference-time design selection and training-time policy optimization. Inference-time methods estimate EIG using LLM-induced generative models and select among candidate questions ( Kobalczyk et al., 2025 ; Choudhury et al., 2025 ) ; CA-BED extends this approach to non-myopic selection through conversation-tree planning ( Arnould et al., 2026 ) . ASIG ( Hartmann et al., 2026 ) instead amortizes design selection into an LLM policy, while retaining a generative model derived from the policy LLM’s predictive distributions. Our approach explicitly optimizes the reasoning preceding design selection, constructs the training generative model independently of the policy LLM, and uses expected information rewards over multiple steps to allow for non-myopic information gathering. We discuss related works in detail in Appendix A . 4 Method: Amortizing BED in Reasoning LLMs Our goal is to amortize sequential BED into a reasoning LLM. One approach to using an LLM as a policy is to directly sample a design given an interaction history, π ϕ ​ ( x t ∣ h t − 1 ) \pi_{\phi}(x_{t}\mid h_{t-1}) , and optimize its expected information gain ( Hartmann et al., 2026 ) . Instead, our aim is to train an LLM that can reason through limited information : deliberating over which hypotheses remain plausible, how possible observations distinguish them, and which further experiments those observations may enable. We therefore parameterize the policy as π ϕ ​ ( z t , x t ∣ h t − 1 ) = π ϕ ​ ( z t ∣ h t − 1 ) ⋅ π ϕ ​ ( x t ∣ h t − 1 , z t ) . \pi_{\phi}(z_{t},x_{t}\mid h_{t-1})=\pi_{\phi}(z_{t}\mid h_{t-1})\cdot\pi_{\phi}(x_{t}\mid h_{t-1},z_{t}). (5) and train its joint output ( z t , x t ) (z_{t},x_{t}) with an information gain objective. At a high level, our training procedure consists of three steps: 1. Generate a design through reasoning. Given an interaction history h t − 1 h_{t-1} , the policy generates a reasoning trace z t z_{t} followed by a design x t x_{t} . 2. Evaluate its information value. A generative model, specified independently of the policy, provides the hypothesis and outcome distributions used to compute the information gain of the design x t x_{t} . 3. Optimize the reasoning policy. We use information-gain rewards accumulated over K K -step rollouts to train the reasoning policy with Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) ( Shao et al., 2024 ) . This procedure requires two main design choices: how to construct the generative model used to evaluate information gain (Sec. 4.1 ), and how to turn this information signal into a training reward (Sec. 4.2 ). 4.1 How should the generative model p ⁡ ( θ , y , x ) p(\theta,y;x) be constructed? Estimating the EIG requires specifying a generative model p ⁡ ( θ , y , x ) p(\theta,y;x) of the experiment. In our amortized setting, this choice determines both the quality of the training signal and the computational cost of obtaining it. We build on the contrastive formulation in Sec. 2.2 , which requires specifying two components: a prior p ⁡ ( θ ) p(\theta) over contrastive hypotheses and a likelihood p ⁡ ( y ∣ θ , x ) p(y\mid\theta,x) for evaluating proposed designs. We describe how we construct these components below. Decoupling the generative model from the policy. One option is to use the policy LLM itself to estimate both components, as in the approaches discussed in Sec. 3 . Training-time amortization enables an important flexibility: the generative model can instead be specified independently of the policy being trained, without requiring it for design selection at inference time. This allows us to define the experimental distribution used for training upfront, while keeping it fixed as the policy is updated (see Appendix D.6 ). This is particularly useful when the initial policy provides a poor approximation of that distribution. For example, the training prior can place mass on hypotheses that the policy itself would be unlikely to generate, while a separate likelihood model can provide more reliable estimates of p ⁡ ( y ∣ θ , x ) p(y\mid\theta,x) than the policy’s own predictive distribution. The prior p ⁡ ( θ ) p(\theta) . The contrastive hypotheses can be sampled from the prior for each trajectory, as in sPCE, or fixed across training. We define our prior as a uniform distribution over a finite contrastive set Θ C = { θ ℓ } ℓ = 0 L \Theta_{C}=\{\theta_{\ell}\}_{\ell=0}^{L} , with p C ​ ( θ ℓ ) = 1 / ( L + 1 ) p_{C}(\theta_{\ell})=1/(L+1) . This prior is fixed across all training trajectories and defines the hypothesis distribution with respect to which information gain is computed. The size of the set introduces a trade-off between coverage and computational cost, while its composition and prior weights determine which hypotheses the policy is trained to distinguish. The likelihood p ⁡ ( y ∣ θ , x ) p(y\mid\theta,x) . In an open-ended natural-language design space, the possible designs x x cannot generally be enumerated in advance, making it impractical to precompute their likelihoods. We therefore use a separate, larger LLM as a semantic likelihood evaluator p oracle ​ ( y ∣ θ , x ) p_{\mathrm{oracle}}(y\mid\theta,x) , which can be queried during training. Following Blau et al. (2022) , we assume that each observation is conditionally independent of the interaction history given the hypothesis and current design, allowing us to accumulate likelihoods as C t , ℓ C_{t,\ell} (see Sec. 2.2 ). The observation y y may be stochastic even when θ \theta is known. For example, the answer to “Is it cute?” is ambiguous for a fixed θ \theta ( Choudhury et al., 2025 ) . We therefore retain the full likelihood distribution over the finite outcome space 𝒴 \mathcal{Y} . Each possible outcome y y induces a corresponding updated likelihood, C t , ℓ ( y ) = C t − 1 , ℓ ​ p oracle ​ ( y ∣ θ ℓ , x t ) C_{t,\ell}^{(y)}=C_{t-1,\ell}p_{\mathrm{oracle}}(y\mid\theta_{\ell},x_{t}) , which allows us to marginalize over possible outcomes when computing the reward (discussed in Sec. 4.2 ). This design also allows for likelihood evaluations p oracle ​ ( y ∣ θ ℓ , x t ) p_{\mathrm{oracle}}(y\mid\theta_{\ell},x_{t}) to be cached and reused across training. 4.2 How should the reward be assigned? Having specified the generative model in Sec. 4.1 , we now consider how information gain over the training hypothesis space should be converted into a reward and how these rewards should be aggregated into training returns. In this case, realized and expected rewards can differ beyond variance reduction, as we discuss further in Appendix D.5 . Constructing the Return. The reward in Eq. 7 measures only the immediate information value of a design. However, a design may also enable more informative future interactions. Following the sequential formulation of Blau et al. (2022) , the undiscounted sum of per-step information rewards recovers multi-step EIG in expectation when rollouts follow the specified generative model. We therefore evaluate each proposed design through a rollout of the current policy. We limit the horizon to K K steps for tractability and introduce γ \gamma to discount future information: R t , K , γ = ∑ k = 0 K − 1 γ k ​ r t + k EIG . R_{t,K,\gamma}=\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\gamma^{k}r_{t+k}^{\mathrm{EIG}}. (8) Setting K = 1 K=1 recovers the myopic objective, while K > 1 K>1 with γ > 0 \gamma>0 rewards the initial decision for information acquired at subsequent steps. In practice, our TQ implementation prunes low-weight hypotheses and advances rollouts using the oracle’s most likely answer rather than sampling an outcome. Our approach makes two key design choices: decoupling the experimental generative model from the policy to avoid relying on potentially poor predictive distributions, and constructing expected information-gain rewards that can be accumulated across interactions. We find that our method improves information acquisition across unseen hypothesis spaces, outperforming other methods, and that the learned behavior transfers to Medi-Q, a real-world clinical dialogue dataset. Our results suggest that adaptive information gathering can be learned as a transferable reasoning capability in LLMs rather than reconstructed through explicit search at every interaction. Limitations. We study relatively short rollout horizons. Extending the approach to longer horizons while maintaining a reliable training signal is an important direction. We also find that performance is sensitive to the coverage of the contrastive hypothesis set when it is misspecified relative to the true hypothesis space. Further understanding how the size, composition, and prior weighting of the contrastive set affect learning and transfer is therefore an important avenue for future study. In Forty-second International Conference on Machine Learning , External Links: Link Cited by: §1 . Appendix A Extended Related Works Inference-time BED. Methods for task disambiguation and adaptive questioning use LLM-generated hypotheses and response predictions to select questions by estimated EIG ( Kobalczyk et al., 2025 ; Choudhury et al., 2025 ) ; related agents maintain explicit Bayesian beliefs to guide exploration ( Grand et al., 2026 ) . These approaches require repeated belief maintenance and utility evaluation at deployment. While one-step EIG selection is myopic, uncertainty-aware planning and conversation-tree search incorporate simulated future interactions ( Hu et al., 2024 ; Arnould et al., 2026 ) . Thus, non-myopic information gathering is not unique to trained policies; our aim is to amortize this behavior into a reasoning LLM rather than perform explicit design search at inference time. Training-time BED. Information-driven post-training uses EIG-ranked question preferences ( Mazzaccara et al., 2024 ) or dense rewards based on changes in the model’s confidence in a known target ( Auzina et al., 2026 ; Deng et al., 2026 ) . These approaches establish informative questioning as a learnable behavior, but differ in their information estimators and optimization procedures. ASIG ( Hartmann et al., 2026 ) amortizes BED into an LLM policy using a generative model derived from the policy’s predictive distributions; its evaluated configuration combines one-step EIG with a multi-step task-outcome reward. We instead explicitly optimize the reasoning preceding design selection and specify the training generative model independently of the policy, using a fixed contrastive prior and a separate semantic likelihood evaluator. This construction enables expected information rewards over the finite hypothesis and outcome spaces, which we accumulate into information-only multi-step returns. Amortized sequential experimental design. Policy-based BED learns adaptive designs across an experiment, shifting optimization to training and accounting for the information available from future observations ( Foster et al., 2021 ; Blau et al., 2022 ; Ivanova et al., 2021 ) . This includes both tractable-likelihood and implicit-simulator settings. Semi-amortized approaches additionally refine a pretrained design policy using observations from the ongoing experiment ( Hedman et al., 2025 ) . We build on the fully amortized formulation, studying how its policy, generative model, and information objective should be instantiated for reasoning LLMs with natural-language actions. Task-oriented exploration and interaction learning. Complementary approaches train curious or collaborative agents using task-oriented rewards, including objectives that assess the consequences of an action through future interactions ( Tajwar et al., 2025 ; Wu et al., 2025 ) . Successful interaction traces also provide supervision for learning clarifying questions ( Andukuri et al., 2024 ) . These works show that strategic information gathering can be learned without an explicit BED objective, and that learned behavior can transfer across tasks ( Tajwar et al., 2025 ) . Our focus is instead on the construction of information-only training returns and their effect on acquisition, reasoning, and transfer. External models for information gathering. Prior work combines language models with parametric Bayesian preference models or learned predictive models for adaptive elicitation ( Handa et al., 2024 ; Wang et al., 2025 ) . Retriever-coupled approaches similarly derive question-training signals from an external candidate distribution or retrieval objective ( Chi et al., 2024 ) . These provide precedents for separating language generation from information-value estimation. Our construction uses a specified finite hypothesis space and a separate semantic likelihood evaluator during training, while deployment requires neither that hypothesis space nor retrieval-based question selection. Estimating information value. Variational and contrastive objectives make Bayesian design optimization tractable ( Foster et al., 2019 ; Foster et al., 2020 ) , while nested Monte Carlo analysis characterizes challenges in estimating information utilities ( Rainforth et al., 2018 ) ; Rainforth et al. (2024) survey these approaches. Appendix B Extended Experimental Results B.1 Contrastive set ablations A larger contrastive set improves performance. B.3 Non-myopic information acquisition We evaluate multi-step rewards in three complementary ways: a controlled environment where context must be acquired before it becomes useful, prediction of target attributes during open-ended questioning, and information acquisition in Twenty Questions. The Cube-NM experiment isolates the ability to learn an initially uninformative action; the other evaluations examine whether a longer reward horizon also benefits natural-language policies. B.3.1 Cube-NM: acquiring context before information A controlled non-myopic environment. We adapt Cube-NM ( Schütz et al., 2026 ; Rückstieß et al., 2013 ) as a small mechanism check. Its 32 diseases and three equally likely, disease-independent contexts define 96 disease–context pairs. Each context activates eight tests; a separate context test reveals which block is active but provides zero immediate disease information. A useful policy must therefore trade immediate information for better subsequent acquisition. We use a natural-language test-selection interface without reasoning traces to isolate this routing decision from open-ended question generation and medical knowledge. Training and evaluation. The 25 equal-cost tests comprise three context-dependent blocks of eight tests and one context test. The context is independent of the disease, and outcomes are deterministic given both. The textual interface returns positive, negative, or inconclusive outcomes and the diseases invalidated by the observation; the context test instead lists the informative tests. Test identifiers are randomly permuted once, identically across the matched runs, and the outcome matrix and context-test identity are not initially disclosed. The policy selects one identifier without a reasoning trace. We train Qwen3-0.6B with GRPO/LoRA for 250 updates at learning rate 10 − 5 10^{-5} , sampling 16 states and 16 rollouts per state from a rolling on-policy bank covering turns 0–4. Temperature is 2, the entropy coefficient is 0.02, and γ = 0.9 \gamma=0.9 . Evaluation uses greedy actions over all 96 disease–context pairs and an exact Bayesian MAP decoder. Total EIG is log ⁡ 32 \log 32 minus the mean disease-posterior entropy. These are matched single-training-seed results, not estimates of variability across training runs. The two-step reward learns the initially uninformative context test. Figure 6 shows that the two-step policy learns to select the context test with almost unit probability, while the one-step policy favors an immediately informative disease test. This incurs an initial cost: after one test, Bayesian MAP solve rate is 3.1 % 3.1\% for K = 2 K=2 versus 5.2 % 5.2\% for K = 1 K=1 . The two-step policy overtakes after three tests ( 27.1 % 27.1\% versus 21.9 % 21.9\% ) and reaches 75.0 % 75.0\% versus 30.2 % 30.2\% after five. Thus the multi-step reward can learn a design for its downstream benefit, even when its immediate disease-information reward is zero. These matched single-seed results support the intended mechanism, not global optimality or an additional claim about reasoning transfer. B.3.4 Myopic versus non-myopic question selection in Twenty Questions Many BED approaches optimize a myopic objective because evaluating future designs is computationally demanding. In an idealized, noiseless Twenty Questions game with a uniform prior and unrestricted binary partitions, balanced questions motivate a greedy strategy. However, the availability of such queries depends on the structure of the query and hypothesis spaces ( Nowak, 2011 ) . An open-ended language interface does not ensure that a policy can propose a balanced, semantically meaningful question for every remaining hypothesis set. Questions can therefore differ both in immediate EIG and in how readily subsequent questions distinguish the surviving hypotheses. This is a possible source of non-myopic behavior, not a claim that natural-language tasks are intrinsically non-myopic. Simulation protocol. Appendix D Additional Discussion of the Design Policy The previous section identified two challenges that motivate amortizing BED: inhibitive inference time and myopic design selection. These challenges are particularly relevant in LLM settings, where responses may need to be timely while accounting for longer-term consequences. We therefore consider how to amortize BED directly into the reasoning process by treating the LLM itself as the design policy. This requires us to consider the LLM setting. What makes learning a LLM design policy unique? Compared to conventional design policies, LLM design policies differ significantly in the design space. Conventional design policies typically map a state h t − 1 h_{t-1} directly to a design x t x_{t} from a predefined design space 𝒳 \mathcal{X} ; for instance, Blau et al. (2022) study designs in a two-dimensional space. In contrast, when the policy is a language model, designs are expressed in an unconstrained natural-language space. Moreover, a reasoning LLM policy can generate an intermediate reasoning process that precedes its final design choice. Making this intermediate reasoning an explicit part of the policy output enables an important capability: the policy can be rewarded not only for the final design but also for the reasoning z t z_{t} that produced it. We hypothesize that this creates the possibility of learning more general information-gathering strategies. We therefore parameterize the policy as π ϕ ​ ( z t , x t , h t − 1 ) = π ϕ ​ ( z t , h t − 1 ) ⋅ π ϕ ​ ( x t , h t − 1 , z t ) , \pi_{\phi}(z_{t},x_{t};h_{t-1})=\pi_{\phi}(z_{t};h_{t-1})\cdot\pi_{\phi}(x_{t};h_{t-1},z_{t}), (9) where z t z_{t} denotes the model’s reasoning trace 2 2 2 Following Choudhury et al. (2025) , we use “ \,;\, ” to denote general dependence and reserve “ ∣ \mid ” for probabilistic conditioning under a specified joint distribution. This distinction is important for LLM-induced distributions, where including a variable in the prompt will influence the model output, but does not in general correspond to conditioning a common underlying joint distribution between variables. . In other words, the model first reasons over the interaction history and then selects a design conditioned on both the history and this reasoning. Employing this LLM design policy requires addressing two design questions (DQs): DQ1: How should the joint distribution p ⁡ ( y , θ , x ) p(y,\theta;x) be constructed? (Sec. D.1 ) Computing the (total) EIG requires specifying the joint distribution over hypotheses and experimental outcomes p ⁡ ( y , θ , x ) p(y,\theta;x) . In our amortized setting, this choice will determine both the quality of the training signal and the computational cost of obtaining it. DQ2: How can we provide a non-myopic objective? (Sec. D.2 ) A non-myopic objective requires assigning value to a current design according to information that may only be acquired in future experiments. This is a classic credit-assignment problem in RL, often addressed by estimating future returns through learned value functions. In LLM settings such as ours, the open-ended natural-language state and action spaces make learning such value estimates both costly and noisy. D.1 Constructing the Experimental Generative Model We require a generative model of the experiment to define the distribution under which information gain is evaluated. Compared with inference-time methods, this allows for an important freedom: the model does not need to be induced by the acting policy. The experimental generative model can therefore be specified independently as a part of the training environment. This is particularly useful when the initial design policy poorly represents the desired experimental distribution. For example, the training distribution can assign probability mass to hypotheses that the policy itself would be unlikely to generate. Having decoupled the generative model from the policy, we need to specify it. The experimental generative model admits two factorizations, p ⁡ ( θ , y ∣ x ) = p ⁡ ( θ ) ⋅ p ⁡ ( y ∣ θ , x ) = p ⁡ ( y ∣ x ) ⋅ p ⁡ ( θ ∣ y , x ) . p(\theta,y\mid x)=p(\theta)\cdot p(y\mid\theta,x)=p(y\mid x)\cdot p(\theta\mid y,x). (10) If the components are estimated separately rather than derived from a common joint, the two constructions are not necessarily equivalent. The appropriate factorization therefore depends on which quantities can be obtained reliably and efficiently, as discussed in great detail in BED LLM ( Choudhury et al., 2025 ) . We adopt the factorization p ⁡ ( θ ) ​ p ​ ( y ∣ θ , x ) p(\theta)p(y\mid\theta,x) . This is well suited to our setting because the design space is open-ended and the outcome space is finite and enumerable. For any proposed design x x and hypothesis θ \theta , the training environment can therefore evaluate the complete distribution over possible outcomes p ⁡ ( y ∣ θ , x ) p(y\mid\theta,x) . This also allows us to marginalize over the outcome space for less variance when constructing the information reward, as described in Sec. D.2 . The prior p ⁡ ( θ ) p(\theta) . Following Blau et al. (2022) , we represent the hypothesis distribution using a finite contrastive set { θ ℓ } ℓ = 0 L \{\theta_{\ell}\}_{\ell=0}^{L} drawn from the desired prior p ⁡ ( θ ) p(\theta) . The contrastive set can be specified once before policy optimization. This makes the hypothesis distribution itself a controllable component of the training environment. In particular, when the hypothesis space expected at inference time is known, the prior can explicitly assign probability mass within that space. In our experiments, we use a uniform prior over this contrastive set. We specifically consider hypotheses from Choudhury et al. (2025) , although in general the set can be constructed from any available prior knowledge or generative model. The likelihood p ⁡ ( y ∣ θ , x ) p(y\mid\theta,x) . For open-ended natural-language designs, the space of possible designs x x cannot generally be enumerated in advance, making it impractical to specify a likelihood beforehand. We therefore use a separate LLM as a semantic likelihood evaluator p oracle ​ ( y , θ , x ) p_{\mathrm{oracle}}(y;\theta,x) which can be queried during training. As in Choudhury et al. (2025) , we retain the full stochastic likelihood to account for cases where the outcome is not deterministic even when θ \theta is known. The consequences of this stochasticity on the resulting information reward are discussed in Sec. D.2 . D.2 Non-Myopic Information Returns With the generative model specified in Sec. D.1 , we now have the contrastive hypotheses and corresponding likelihoods p o ​ r ​ a ​ c ​ l ​ e ​ ( y , θ , x ) p_{oracle}(y;\theta,x) required to evaluate the sequential contrastive reward in Eq. 4 . In Blau et al. (2022) , this reward is evaluated for a realized outcome. In our setting, the outcome space 𝒴 \mathcal{Y} is enumerable, and the likelihood evaluator provides the full distribution over 𝒴 \mathcal{Y} for each contrastive hypothesis. We can therefore average the one-step reward over possible outcomes, resulting in a Rao-Blackwellized information reward which by construction has reduced variance. To construct a non-myopic objective, Blau et al. (2022) use an actor-critic method to estimate the expected cumulative future information reward associated with each design. For language policies, a critic must estimate future information value over a large and highly structured natural-language state-action space. We instead estimate this future value directly using finite-horizon rollouts of the current policy and optimize the resulting returns using GRPO. Starting from a history h t − 1 h_{t-1} , the policy generates a reasoning trace and design, ( z t , x t ) ∼ π ϕ ( ⋅ ∣ h t − 1 ) (z_{t},x_{t})\sim\pi_{\phi}(\cdot\mid h_{t-1}) , after which the policy and environment are rolled forward for up to K − 1 K-1 additional experiments. We assign the initial decision the return R t , K , γ = ∑ k = 0 K − 1 γ k ​ r t + k . R_{t,K,\gamma}=\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\gamma^{k}r_{t+k}. (11) K K determines the number of experimental steps included in the return and γ \gamma controls the relative weight placed on information obtained further into the future. For each training history, GRPO samples a group of initial decisions. Each decision is evaluated by its corresponding K K -step information return, and the relative returns within the group are used to update the policy. D.3 Fixed Contrastive Sets as Finite Bayesian Models Standard sPCE constructs a finite contrastive approximation by sampling hypotheses from the prior for each trajectory.
