    <title>VeriCUA: Learning Proactive Verification for Reliable GUI Agent Rewards</title>
    <updated>2026-10-19T14:17:33Z</updated>
    <link href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.26469v1" rel="alternate" type="text/html"/>
    <link href="https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.26469v1" rel="related" type="application/pdf" title="pdf"/>
    <summary>Reinforcement learning for computer-use agents increasingly relies on proactive reward agents that interact with the post-execution GUI environment to verify what recorded screenshots cannot reveal. The reliability of such verification hinges on two coupled decisions: what to check and when to stop. Existing methods leave these decisions to prompted foundation models. However, neither scaling up the model nor providing few-shot examples reliably improves verification: prompted Judges ranging from 4B to GPT-5.4 all plateau at 68%-70% macro-average agreement. This is because the value of a check depends on the environment rather than the task alone, and becomes apparent only through execution. Learning from execution is itself constrained, as the Actor's terminal state cannot be faithfully replicated during training, allowing only one verification attempt per state. We introduce VeriCUA, a proactive reward agent that learns its verification strategy from these single attempts. A trainable Judge decides what to check and when to stop, while a frozen Probe executes the checks in the live environment. Online reinforcement learning optimizes the Judge for both verdict correctness and efficient Probe use, with returns normalized across terminal states. Trained only on OSWorld and evaluated without further training on MobileWorld and WebArena-Verified, VeriCUA achieves the highest reward agreement across all three benchmarks and lies on the agreement-cost Pareto frontier among proactive verifiers. It surpasses few-shot GPT-5.4 by 5.36 points with 47.6%-59.8% fewer Probe tokens, and even a 1B VeriCUA exceeds every prompted Judge. As the reward for Actor RL, it raises task success by 3.21 points overall and 5.40 points on held-out multi-application tasks. These results identify the verification strategy, not verifier scale, as the key to reliable GUI rewards.</summary>

    <title>Learning Waypoints and Passage Events for Decentralized Multi-Quadrotor Trajectory Planning</title>
    <updated>2026-10-18T05:18:51Z</updated>
    <link href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.25024v1" rel="alternate" type="text/html"/>
    <link href="https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.25024v1" rel="related" type="application/pdf" title="pdf"/>
    <summary>Multi-quadrotor passage planning requires coordinated spatial routes and passage times under vehicle dynamics and actuation constraints. Optimizing these decisions jointly couples temporal allocation, nonlinear motion, and inter-vehicle interactions, creating a computational challenge for local trajectory generation. We propose a learning-optimization framework that predicts spatial waypoints and ordered passage events for independently solved quadrotor planners. A common physical time grid aligns the vehicles, with intermediate events fixed by the networks and terminal progress optimized locally to permit different completion metrics. Differentiable sequential convex programming incorporates rigid-body dynamics and rotor-thrust bounds, allowing collision and completion gradients to train both networks through the generated trajectories. In a two-quadrotor evaluation across 24 layouts, the adapted policy passes physical feasibility and sampled separation checks in all five held-out layouts. Their mean completion metric decreases by 1.52% relative to the inherited policy. The median forward time is 21.01 ms on the held-out exchange layout with matching numerical trajectory references. These results demonstrate that learned spatial and event parameters can coordinate independent rigid-body planners while preserving local vehicle constraints in the evaluated scenarios.</summary>

    <title>MLExpert: Towards a Generalist Agentic Model for Autonomous Machine Learning Engineering</title>
    <updated>2026-10-15T17:56:32Z</updated>

    <link href="https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.22344v1" rel="related" type="application/pdf" title="pdf"/>
    <summary>Large language models (LLMs) are expanding from assisting scientific research and engineering to autonomously developing and improving AI systems, advancing AI for AI (AI4AI) as a pathway toward recursive self-improvement (RSI). Workflow-driven machine learning engineering (MLE) systems provide a practical path toward this pursuit through complex orchestration that manages long-horizon experimentation, while recent MLE systems adopt model-directed interaction, allowing models to autonomously organize experiments and adapt optimization strategies based on execution feedback. However, feedback from each experiment reflects several preceding decisions, and a long MLE run contains many such costly experiments. Training must therefore select useful supervision from long interaction trajectories and learn from intermediate outcomes. We propose a post-training method for autonomous long-horizon MLE optimization and apply it to train MLExpert, a 35B Mixture-of-Experts agentic model. Hierarchical agentic SFT transfers general tool-use and coding capabilities to MLE through outcome-guided supervision. Windowed Interaction RL (WIRL) treats the multi-turn decisions between successive experiment results as an execution window and assigns it a shared outcome-based reward. Asynchronous collection and policy correction support policy updates before the trajectory ends. On MLE-Bench Lite, MLExpert improves the average medal rate of the base model from 37.9% to 72.7%, achieving performance comparable to larger frontier models such as Kimi-K3 and GLM-5.3. Further experiments show that MLExpert improves performance on NatureBench Lite, agent scaffold optimization, and algorithm optimization, suggesting that the learned optimization capability generalizes to other tasks.</summary>

    <title>When Pairwise Preferences Cycle: A Reliability-Anchored Preference Walk for Video GRPO</title>
    <updated>2026-10-14T12:30:39Z</updated>
    <link href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20089v1" rel="alternate" type="text/html"/>
    <link href="https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.20089v1" rel="related" type="application/pdf" title="pdf"/>
    <summary>Pairwise preference feedback has become a widely adopted supervision signal for reinforcement learning in video generation models, as pairwise comparisons provide a more reliable alternative than pointwise scalar ratings for evaluating the quality of generated videos. Specifically, we define a preference graph for each GRPO group under a pairwise evaluator, where nodes represent sampled rollouts and edges encode pairwise preferences. Auditing 6,976 on-policy comparison graphs, we find that cyclic preferences are prevalent and persist throughout training. Naive pairwise reward aggregation based on average win rates discards the underlying comparison structures and treats each rollout independently, which may introduce unreliable advantage estimates for certain rollouts and consequently affect reinforcement learning optimization. Therefore, we propose the \emph{Reliability-Anchored Preference Walk} (\ours{}), a graph-aware advantage aggregation method that strengthens the global preference signal from win rates with reliable relational evidence from cyclic preference graphs through a reliability-guided preference walk. Under matched evaluator, comparison protocol, group size, prompt schedule, and query budget, \ours{} requires no additional evaluator queries beyond the observed comparisons within each rollout group and improves over Pairwise-GRPO across controlled comparisons, transfer settings, and blind human evaluations.</summary>

    <title>How the Classical Baseline Shapes Machine-Learning Speed Claims in Topology Optimization</title>
    <updated>2026-10-17T22:13:50Z</updated>
    <link href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.16051v2" rel="alternate" type="text/html"/>
    <link href="https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.16051v2" rel="related" type="application/pdf" title="pdf"/>
    <summary>Topology optimization places material in a design domain to optimize a structure's performance, such as its stiffness or heat conduction. Machine-learning methods often report large speed-ups over classical, non-learned optimization, but a slow classical code or a lower-quality learned design can exaggerate them. We re-ran released code for eight methods on one workstation and studied two in depth: Optimize Any Topology (OAT), a diffusion model that generates designs, and a graph-based neural optimizer. Replacing the classical code with an efficient implementation of the same algorithm cut these two methods' speed-ups by up to 20-fold. We then timed the classical code until its design was at least as stiff as the learned one; for OAT, we picked this point after the runs, which gives the classical code its best case. Published timings imply a 134x speed-up for OAT on a 256 x 256 grid; our matched single-design comparisons gave 1.5x to 3.5x. At matched stiffness, the graph-based optimizer was slower than the classical code in all six two-dimensional problems. Of the six other methods, two kept their advantage, one shrank, one reversed, and two were not comparable. In an exploratory audit, language models judged that 8 of 28 speed comparisons in randomly sampled, openly accessible articles lacked evidence of equal design quality; our own review counted 11, so the count depends on who judges. We provide a comparison procedure and a minimum report: classical code, hardware, design quality, what each timing includes, and data-generation and training costs.</summary>

    <title>DeltaNet-PC: Test-Time Memory Without Backpropagation Through Time</title>
    <updated>2026-10-10T21:17:00Z</updated>
    <link href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.15185v1" rel="alternate" type="text/html"/>
    <link href="https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.15185v1" rel="related" type="application/pdf" title="pdf"/>
    <summary>Test-time memorization of sequential information is a foundational capability for any intelligent system that needs to rapidly store and retrieve data, as demonstrated by the attention mechanism in large language models. However, Transformers and linear attention variants are trained via backpropagation through time (BPTT), which has drawbacks such as its enormous memory requirements. Predictive coding (PC), an algorithm that optimizes a prediction-error energy function with local updates, offers a compelling solution but still lacks test-time memory for sequential data. We develop a solution to this problem by treating the per-step memory update $ΔS_t$ as a latent variable, as opposed to a slow weight to be learned. By taking the log probability of a standard PC-based probabilistic model, we derive a PC-native solution for verbatim sequence memory, and relate our update rule to common choices in the linear attention literature (e.g., DeltaNet). We present an extensive set of empirical tests to probe our system's properties, including its scaling capacity, memory footprint, and ability to infer a context from evidence (e.g., bank (river) vs. bank (money)). We show how DeltaNet-PC can be used for storing single sequences of data, multiple sequences of data, and key-value associative memory pairs with perfect recall accuracy.</summary>

    <summary>Conventional control approaches to optimization problems under uncertainty entail a practical compromise: deterministic controls offer robustness at the expense of pathwise accuracy while stochastic controls can adapt to individual realizations but suffer from the curse of dimensionality. To achieve both robustness and adaptivity, a neural operator-based approach is proposed in this paper for distributed elliptic optimal control problems with high-dimensional random inputs. A data-driven training algorithm is first established to learn the mapping from coefficient realizations directly to their optimal pathwise controls.     <summary>Physical layer (PHY) information hiding refers to the concealment of secrets within PHY waveforms without compromising the integrity of the primary communication link, thereby enabling critical applications such as covert communication, PHY steganography, and watermarking. However, existing Wi-Fi information hiding approaches suffer from two limitations: restricted steganographic capacity and insufficient robustness against environmental variations, both of which severely constrain practical deployment. The hardware prototype study validates the feasibility of the proposed Wi-Fi information hiding scheme.</summary>

    <title>ROAM: Reinforcement Learning Optimized Adaptive Moving Target Defense for Smart Grid</title>
    <updated>2026-10-08T21:19:39Z</updated>
    <link href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.12730v1" rel="alternate" type="text/html"/>
    <link href="https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.12730v1" rel="related" type="application/pdf" title="pdf"/>
    <summary>Smart grids rely on distributed phasor measurement units (PMUs) and wide-area communication networks whose data-acquisition pipelines are vulnerable to stealthy false data injection (FDI) attacks that evade conventional bad-data detection (BDD). Moving target defense (MTD) counters this exposure by continuously remapping sensor identities, but existing schemes apply static or pre-scheduled randomization and are each constrained by a single failure mode: seed-based methods are fast yet fragile to key compromise, while machine learning (ML) based prediction methods are resilient yet incur prohibitive latency. Neither adapts its randomization to live threat evidence. This paper presents ROAM, a deep reinforcement learning (DRL) driven MTD framework in which a deep Q-network (DQN) agent continuously re-optimizes the sensor-to-ID mapping at the aggregation layer. At each decision step, the agent selects the deception coverage, flags the specific sensors to remap, and sets the split of the remapped sensors between seed-based and ML-prediction-based recovery, without modifying PMU hardware and while preserving IEEE C37.118 compatibility. On held-out BDD-compliant FDI scenarios across the IEEE 14-, 57-, and 118- bus systems, ROAM keeps the percentage of stealthy attacks below 3% while sustaining detection rates above 93%. Against an adaptive soft actor-critic (SAC) attacker that observes the defender's configuration, ROAM keeps stealthy attacks below 2.6% with detection above 92%. Hardware-in-the-loop testing on an OPAL-RT platform confirms real-time feasibility, with sub-18ms average shuffle execution and an average reshuffle blind window of 2.6% of the 3-s decision cycle.</summary>

    <title>Beyond QAOA: A Review of AI and Quantum Computing for Adaptive Combinatorial Optimization</title>
    <updated>2026-10-09T12:10:30Z</updated>
    <link href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.11759v2" rel="alternate" type="text/html"/>
    <link href="https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.11759v2" rel="related" type="application/pdf" title="pdf"/>
    <summary>Near-term quantum approaches to combinatorial optimization are limited by qubit counts, circuit fidelity, sampling cost, and the difficulty of encoding constraints, while machine learning is increasingly used to configure and control quantum optimization workflows. We call such workflows adaptive: decisions conventionally fixed in advance, from formulation and penalties to shot budgets, backends, and whether to invoke a quantum processor at all, are made by learned policies that respond to the instance, the progress of the solve, or the hardware. This review examines three paradigms, AI for quantum optimization, quantum for AI-driven optimization, and AI-quantum co-optimization, and organizes the literature by the decision being learned rather than by application. A structured search identified a 119-paper corpus, of which 67 papers form an evidence core coded in detail. The evidence is considerably stronger for AI-assisted quantum optimization than for the reverse direction: learning already reduces quantum evaluations, improves initialization, supports decomposition and penalty control, and mitigates noise, whereas evidence that quantum computation improves learned optimizers remains largely confined to small-scale simulation. Experimental controls are thin: of 52 studies in the three paradigms, 22 include no classical baseline, the quantum contribution is fully isolated in 10 of 24 where an ablation applies, and the median largest experiment uses 16 qubits. We introduce an M0-M5 evidence hierarchy, from simulation to practical quantum advantage, and find no broadly convincing result at the highest level. We argue that scaling is increasingly a systems problem: the question is not only whether a problem fits on a quantum processor, but how classical and quantum resources should be allocated across the optimization process.</summary>

    <title>Machine Learning Optimization for Enhanced OS Fingerprinting</title>
    <updated>2026-10-08T02:58:09Z</updated>
    <link href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.11133v1" rel="alternate" type="text/html"/>
    <link href="https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.11133v1" rel="related" type="application/pdf" title="pdf"/>
    <summary>Operating System (OS) Fingerprinting is a technique that can be used to identify a network's operating systems by evaluating network traffic in the form of TCP/IP packets. This research will explore the effectiveness of passively identifying operating systems on the CIC-IDS2017 dataset, a collection of over 47 gigabytes of pcap files with their corresponding operating systems. This research also proposes a new command line interface, OsirisML, which uses nPrint to preprocess the data into tabular data and XGBoost to apply ML to the data to generate, retrain, and test ML models. When packets are split randomly between training and testing, OsirisML models reach an accuracy of 97.66% on a down-sampled subset of the Friday capture and 84.69% on the entire capture. On the entire Monday capture, which contains no attacks, OsirisML reaches an accuracy of 73.83% and an F-1 score of 79.38%.</summary>
