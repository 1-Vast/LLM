Abstract Processing a long sequence does not guarantee that distant information remains usable. As context grows, a fixed-size memory must retain many associations, connect information across positions, and preserve relevant structure without being overwhelmed by everything it observes. These are distinct requirements: a memory may store more information without representing the relationships needed for retrieval, and it may transport those relationships without preserving the ones that matter. Thus, the challenge is not simply extending context length, but constructing a memory that remains usable as context grows. We study this problem in large-chunk test-time training (TTT) and identify two basic failures: single-step writes accumulate interference, limiting capacity , while self-associative writes cannot form cross-position bindings . We introduce Double-Take (DT) , which repairs these failures with multi-step unpinned updates and cross-position mixing, and adds bidirectional change-sensitive writes for selective retention . Capacity alone leaves end-to-end recall at the 0.10 0.10 chance floor; adding binding raises it to 0.92 0.92 . Trained only on 32 ​ kb 32\,\mathrm{kb} genomic contexts, DT reaches 0.927 0.927 AUROC at 131 ​ kb 131\,\mathrm{kb} and 0.856 0.856 near 1 ​ Mb 1\,\mathrm{Mb} , while transferring zero-shot from human to mouse. Together, these results identify capacity, binding, and selectivity as three requirements for usable long-range memory. 1 Introduction Long-context modeling is increasingly measured by how many tokens a model can process, but a large context window does not guarantee that the information inside it remains usable. Long-context language models can lose access to relevant information depending on where it appears in the sequence ( Liu et al., 2024 ) , and controlled evaluations show that performance often degrades sharply as context length and retrieval complexity increase ( Hsieh et al., 2024 ) . Similar limitations appear in efficient sequence models, where associative recall accounts for a substantial part of the gap with attention-based models ( Arora et al., 2024a ) . Together, these results point to a distinction that context length alone does not capture: processing distant information and remembering it are different problems . This distinction becomes more important as models scale to sequences that cannot remain explicitly addressable. Full attention preserves direct token-to-token interaction but incurs quadratic cost ( Vaswani et al., 2017 ) ; efficient architectures instead summarize or compress history through structured sequence operators or recurrent states ( Gu and Dao, 2023 ; Nguyen et al., 2023 ; Schiff et al., 2024 ) . Once information is compressed, three questions become unavoidable. How many distinct associations can survive as new information arrives? Can the memory preserve a relationship between events that occurred at different positions? And, when the context becomes very large, which of those relationships should remain prominent? Genomics makes all three questions concrete: individual nucleotides can matter, their regulatory partners may lie tens of kilobases to megabases away, and only a small fraction of the intervening sequence may be relevant to that interaction ( Rao et al., 2014 ; Dixon et al., 2012 ; Zhou and Troyanskaya, 2015 ) . The difficulty is therefore not merely reaching farther, but preserving useful structure while doing so. Test-time training (TTT) ( Sun et al., 2024 ) offers a natural way to revisit this problem because it replaces a fixed recurrent state with an adaptive fast-weight memory that is updated as the sequence is processed. Large-chunk TTT (LaCT) extends this idea efficiently to long sequences ( Zhang et al., 2025 ) . Yet examining what happens when a large chunk is written into that memory exposes the same three questions in a particularly simple form. A single update superposes many associations, so new writes interfere with those already stored. Even if that interference is reduced, same-position key–value writes contain no explicit relationship between different positions. And even if both problems are resolved, transporting every association equally gives the memory no mechanism for distinguishing structure that matters from content that merely occurred. These are three different limitations: capacity , binding , and selectivity . This observation leads directly to Double-Take (DT) . Multi-step unpinned updates allow competing associations to be resolved rather than collapsed into a single write, increasing capacity . Local cross-position mixing exposes relationships before they enter memory, enabling binding . Finally, bidirectional change-sensitive writes expose differences between opposing contexts, providing a mechanism for selective retention . DT therefore follows from a simple view of long-range memory: additional context becomes useful only when a model can retain enough information, relate information across positions, and preserve the relationships that matter . Building on this mechanism we present the following contributions: • We identify interference-limited capacity and the inability of self-associative writes to form cross-position bindings as two limitations of large-chunk test-time memory. • We repair capacity with multi-step unpinned updates, enable cross-position binding through local mixing, and introduce bidirectional memory for selective retention. • We show that DT remains informative more than 30 × 30\times beyond its training context, directly recruits information outside the local receptive field through its test-time memory, and transfers zero-shot from human to mouse. 2 Background 2.1 Fast-Weight Memory and Test-Time Training Efficient long-context models cannot keep every past token explicitly available, so they compress the growing sequence into a smaller state that is carried forward. This makes long sequences practical, but recall does not automatically grow with context. Associative recall remains a major source of error in efficient sequence models ( Arora et al., 2023 ) , state size trades directly against recall ability ( Arora et al., 2024b ) , and fixed-size latent states face fundamental limitations on copying and retrieval ( Jelassi et al., 2024 ) . Related work has therefore explored compressed, external, and persistent memories as ways to preserve information beyond a standard recurrent state ( Rae et al., 2019 ; Wu et al., 2022 ) . Fast-weight memory takes a different approach: rather than storing the past only in activations, it writes temporary information into parameters that change as the sequence is processed ( Ba et al., 2016 ) . Linear attention can itself be interpreted as such a fast-weight memory, where key-value associations are written into a finite state ( Schlag et al., 2021 ) . Later delta-rule methods showed that changing the write rule can improve how these associations are corrected and retrieved ( Schlag et al., 2021 ; Yang et al., 2024 ; Yang et al., 2025 ) . Test-time training (TTT) extends this idea by making the hidden state a small learnable model whose parameters are updated directly from the incoming sequence ( Sun et al., 2024 ) . Given keys K K and values V V , a memory write takes the form W ← W − η ​ ∇ W ℒ ​ ( f W ​ ( K ) , V ) , W\leftarrow W-\eta\nabla_{W}\mathcal{L}\!\left(f_{W}(K),V\right), (1) and a later query Q Q reads from the updated memory through f W ​ ( Q ) f_{W}(Q) . Large-Chunk Test-Time Training (LaCT) makes this process practical at long sequence lengths by updating the fast-weight memory over large blocks of tokens ( Zhang et al., 2025 ) . This greatly improves efficiency, but it also makes each update responsible for many associations at once. That immediately raises three questions. Can many associations be written without interfering with one another? Can information from different positions be connected inside the memory? And, as more context arrives, which of those relationships should remain important? We refer to these as capacity , binding , and selectivity . Prior fast-weight and gated-memory work already shows that both the form of the write and the control of memory updates matter for long-range retrieval ( Schlag et al., 2021 ; Yang et al., 2025 ; Behrouz et al., 2026 ) ; our focus is on understanding how these three requirements arise specifically when large chunks are written into test-time memory. 2.2 Long-Context Genomic Modeling Genomics makes these requirements especially concrete. Regulatory information can depend on individual nucleotides while interactions span tens to hundreds of kilobases through chromatin contacts and domain organization ( Dixon et al., 2012 ; Rao et al., 2014 ) . Akita showed that DNA sequence alone can predict three-dimensional genome folding ( Fudenberg et al., 2020 ) , while Enformer demonstrated that integrating long-range sequence improves regulatory prediction ( Avsec et al., 2021 ) . More recent models extend this scale further: Borzoi models regulatory activity over large genomic windows ( Linder et al., 2025 ) , while HyenaDNA and Caduceus bring long-context modeling to nucleotide resolution ( Nguyen et al., 2023 ; Schiff et al., 2024 ) . These models make distant sequence increasingly accessible; the remaining question is whether a memory can preserve enough of that information, connect the relevant positions, and retain the structure that matters. This is the question we take up next. We examine the large-chunk fast-weight write itself and ask what prevents capacity , binding , and selectivity from being achieved. 3 Double-Take Double-Take (DT) is a fast-weight memory that processes the sequence in chunks while carrying a persistent memory state across them. The key is that this refinement remains partial . The incoming state already contains information accumulated from earlier chunks. Under the stock rule, each update is followed by row-wise renormalization, and repeated updates progressively rotate the state toward the current write, erasing what earlier chunks stored. DT therefore limits the number of inner updates and carries the resulting state M m = M m ( S ) M_{m}=M_{m}^{(S)} directly to the next chunk. The carried state is diluted, and not replaced. Multi-step partial updates thus increase capacity while preserving one evolving memory across chunks. Yet capacity alone cannot change the structure of the write: k i k_{i} is still associated only with v i v_{i} from the same position. A relationship between different positions must therefore enter the memory in a different way. 3.2 Local Mixing Creates Cross-Position Bindings Increasing capacity lets more associations survive, but it does not change which associations the memory can represent. In the standard write, k i k_{i} is paired only with v i v_{i} , so the memory contains terms of the form v i ​ k i ⊤ v_{i}k_{i}^{\top} . A relationship between two different positions is therefore absent from the write itself. No number of additional inner updates can recover a term that was never presented to the memory. Thus, rather than asking the memory to infer a missing relationship after the write, DT makes that relationship part of what is written. Importantly, the width- 3 3 convolution is not the source of long-range reach. It only determines which nearby positions become bound together. Once that relationship enters the persistent fast-weight state, it is carried across subsequent chunks and remains available to distant queries. Local mixing therefore provides the binding ; the persistent memory provides the transport . The memory can now store more associations and carry relationships across distance. But making a relationship available does not make it important: the memory still treats ordinary content and meaningful structural change alike. 3.3 Change-Point Writes Add Selectivity Binding allows relationships between positions to enter memory, but it does not determine which of them should be emphasized. As more context is written, DT therefore looks for a simple signal of change: whether the context on one side of a position differs from the context on the other. Similar surroundings should contribute little, while a strong difference should remain visible. To expose this difference, DT views each position from both directions. The forward stream summarizes the w = 512 w=512 positions preceding i i , giving μ i L \mu_{i}^{L} , while the same causal operation on the reversed sequence gives the corresponding right-context summary μ i R \mu_{i}^{R} . After realigning the reverse stream, DT writes their difference, y i = μ i L − μ i R . y_{i}=\mu_{i}^{L}-\mu_{i}^{R}. This directly produces the desired behavior: when the two sides are similar, their summaries largely cancel; when they differ, the contrast remains. A transition from representation a a to b b , for example, produces approximately a − b a-b , whereas a locally similar region produces a value near zero. The write therefore emphasizes change rather than treating every position equally. The same distinction is preserved when the memory is read. If o i L o_{i}^{L} and o i R o_{i}^{R} are the aligned forward and reverse reads, DT retains both their shared component, ( o i L + o i R ) / 2 (o_{i}^{L}+o_{i}^{R})/2 , and their directional difference, o i L − o i R o_{i}^{L}-o_{i}^{R} . The forward and reversed sequences are processed together by stacking them along the batch dimension, so the two views do not require a second sequential traversal. The resulting memory remains causal within each directional pass. During masked-language pretraining, masking is applied before the query, key, and value streams are constructed, preventing a masked position from writing its own ground-truth nucleotide into memory. 4 Experiments We evaluate DT at two levels. First, controlled associative-recall experiments isolate the fast-weight memory itself, allowing the capacity and binding mechanisms to be tested independently of genomic pretraining. We then evaluate the complete model on long-context genomic tasks, where the encoder is frozen and only lightweight downstream probes are trained. This second stage tests whether the repaired memory remains useful as context grows beyond the training length, whether its write becomes selective, and whether the learned representation transfers outside the human training distribution. Our encoder contains 87 87 M parameters in twelve identical blocks, each combining a depthwise convolution, sliding-window attention, DT fast-weight memory, and a position-wise MLP. The embedding width is 768 768 , the memory-head dimension is 32 32 , and memory updates operate on chunks of 2048 2048 tokens. We pretrain at single-nucleotide resolution on GRCh38 using a bidirectional masked-language-modeling objective with 32 ​ kb 32\,\mathrm{kb} sequences; chromosomes 8 and 9 are excluded from pretraining and reserved for downstream human evaluation. The sliding-window attention path covers 128 128 positions on either side per layer, giving an attention-only receptive field of approximately ± 1.5 ​ kb \pm 1.5\,\mathrm{kb} across the twelve-block stack. Information used from substantially beyond this range must therefore pass through the fast-weight memory path. Unless stated otherwise, matched comparisons use the same architecture, examples, splits, and downstream protocol; public long-context models are treated as external reference points rather than causal controls. We therefore use distal masking to isolate the memory path directly. Every prediction target is separated from the nearest visible token by at least 3,072 3{,}072 bp, well beyond the approximately ± 1.5 ​ kb \pm 1.5\,\mathrm{kb} receptive field of the local convolution and sliding-window attention path. Predictive information from visible sequence beyond this range must therefore be mediated by the persistent fast-weight state. With the adapting bidirectional memory enabled, DT reaches a distal validation loss of 1.292 1.292 . Freezing the same fast-weight state increases the loss to 15.30 15.30 , showing that the test-time write carries predictive information unavailable to the local path. Replacing the two directed memory passes with a single global-state control reaches 1.326 1.326 ; the bidirectional formulation performs better in all six paired validation evaluations. Because the evaluation pipeline is optimized around the adapting state, we treat the frozen-state comparison as a mechanistic control rather than interpreting the absolute magnitude of the loss gap. 4.4 DT Selectively Preserves Genomic Change Having established that distant information is both useful and directly recruited through the fast-weight state, we next ask what information that memory preferentially preserves. We begin with eQTL variant-effect prediction , where the representation must capture how a sequence change alters regulatory state. We organize the comparison from task-specific prediction to increasingly general long-context encoders. Task-specific expert and CNN models provide supervised references specialized for the prediction task. HyenaDNA introduces long-range convolutional sequence mixing, while Caduceus-PH and Caduceus-PS add bidirectional state-space processing over long genomic context. We then evaluate DT with the encoder frozen and train only a lightweight logistic adapter, asking whether the pretrained memory already exposes the information required for variant-effect prediction. Because the published baselines use their own fine-tuning protocols, we additionally use a protocol-matched frozen HyenaDNA comparison on whole blood. Finally, to separate sensitivity to sequence change from generic long-range decodability, we pair eQTL prediction with enhancer-target-gene assignment , where the representation must retain the identity of a particular distant element rather than the effect of a local change. We identify capacity , binding , and selectivity as three requirements for usable long-range test-time memory, and DT addresses them through multi-step updates, cross-position mixing, and bidirectional change-point writes. Controlled experiments isolate each mechanism, while genomic results show that the resulting memory remains useful far beyond its 32 32 kb training context and transfers across species at megabase scale. Our study is limited to genomic sequence and does not incorporate transcriptomic, epigenomic, or proteomic supervision; comparison with some recent TTT methods is also constrained by the lack of reproducible implementations. Extending these memory principles across modalities, architectures, and domains is an important next step. Appendix C Extended Experimental Details This section provides the experiment-specific protocols underlying Sec. 4.2 . Shared architecture, pretraining, memory configuration, and downstream-readout details are given above; here we focus on how each experiment is constructed, what is held fixed, and what each comparison measures. We follow the same progression as the main paper: controlled capacity and binding, long-context extrapolation, selective transport, and finally cross-species transfer.
