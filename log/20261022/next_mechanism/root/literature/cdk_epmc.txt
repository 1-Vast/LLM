[article-title] ESR1 mutations and CDK4/6 inhibitor choice shape clonal selection and adaptive cell states during acquired resistance

[title] Background

[p] In estrogen receptor-positive (ER +) breast cancer, CDK4/6 inhibitors (CDK4/6is) combined with endocrine therapy (ET) are standard first-line treatment for metastatic disease. However, most patients eventually develop resistance. Activating ESR1 mutations are a prevalent mechanism of acquired resistance to ET and are enriched after ET plus a CDK4/6 inhibitor (CDK4/6i), but their role in the clonal evolution and adaptive mechanisms of acquired resistance to CDK4/6 inhibition, independent of ET, is unknown. In addition, whether different CDK4/6is impose distinct selective pressures and divergent resistance states remains elusive.

[title] Methods

[p] To investigate the clonal dynamics, cell states and cellular plasticity during acquired CDK4/6i resistance in mutant versus wild-type (WT) ESR1, we performed high-complexity DNA barcoding (ClonTracer library) with longitudinal sampling and multi-omic profiling in an isogeneic MCF7 model expressing WT ER or Y537S mutant ER. We also evaluated the clonality of the ESR1 mutations in clinical samples with CDK4/6i resistance.

[title] Results

[p] We showed that ESR1 mutations are enriched in clinical tumors with acquired resistance to CDK4/6is, and in paired biopsies expanded to near clonality after treatment. We demonstrated progressive clonal selection with both divergent and partially convergent evolutionary trajectories. The ESR1 mutation substantially reshapes clonal and epigenetic evolution during palbociclib resistance but had a weaker impact under abemaciclib selection. Overall, clonal evolution and cell states in palbociclib and abemaciclib resistance were distinct. Single-cell RNA-seq revealed transcriptional heterogeneity highlighting cellular plasticity during passaging of cells and selection. Finally, in vivo barcoding of mammary xenograft, local recurrences, and distant metastases demonstrated site-specific clonal outgrowth in mutant ER metastases, and partial overlap between metastatic and CDK4/6i-resistant subclones, supporting the dual role of specific populations in therapeutic resistance and metastatic colonization.

[title] Conclusions

[p] High-resolution lineage tracing and multi-omic studies demonstrate that CDK4/6i resistance is shaped by clonal selection and adaptive remodeling of cell states, with the ESR1 mutation status and the specific inhibitor acting as key determinants of evolutionary trajectories. These findings suggest that both variables should be considered when designing sequential and combination treatment strategies to overcome CDK4/6i resistance.

[title] Keywords

[title] Background

[p] Endocrine therapy (ET) in combination with a CDK4/6 inhibitor (CDK4/6i) is the cornerstone treatment in estrogen receptor positive (ER +) breast cancer [1]. These therapies converge on the cyclin D1/CDK4/6/Rb axis, blocking the G1-S cell cycle transition and inducing cell cycle arrest, which accounts, at least in part, for their synergistic effects [2–4]. Despite the substantial benefits from this treatment combination, nearly all patients with metastatic disease will ultimately develop resistance. Genetic alterations that are direct drivers of acquired resistance to CDK4/6is, such as RB1 mutations, occur in only a small proportion of patients [5, 6]. Other recurrent alterations that indirectly drive resistance were also reported, including mutations in FAT1 [7], PIK3CA [8] and FGFR1 amplifications [9]. In addition, epigenetic remodeling with consequent upregulation of resistance-associated signaling pathways [10] is likely to represent another mechanism of CDK4/6i resistance.

[p] The ESR1 ligand binding domain (LBD) mutations, which confer constitutive activity and resistance to ET [11, 12], were not shown to be associated with primary (intrinsic) resistance to CDK4/6i-ET combinations [13–15]. However, these mutations, particularly the Y537S ESR1 mutation, that exhibits the most robust ligand independent and antagonistic resistance phenotypes among the ESR1 LBD mutations [16], were enriched following the acquisition of resistance to the combination of the CDK4/6i palbociclib and ET [6]. Moreover, clinical trials showed that in patients treated with ET plus a CDK4/6i, who had evidence of an emerging ESR1 mutation, better targeting of the ESR1 mutations by switching from an aromatase inhibitor to a selective estrogen receptor degrader (SERD) (fulvestrant or camizestrant) prolonged treatment efficacy [17, 18]. These clinical observations suggest that the ESR1 mutations have a role in acquired resistance to CDK4/6i-ET combinations. However, their mechanistic role in resistance to the CDK4/6i component isolated from ET remains unresolved. It is unknown whether these mutations directly alter clonal selection, reshape chromatin landscapes, or influence the adaptive cellular states that permit survival under CDK4/6 inhibition. Furthermore, whether different CDK4/6is impose distinct selective pressures giving rise to divergent resistant states has not been investigated. This question is particularly relevant since multiple CDK4/6is are approved for clinical use, and prior studies have shown that abemaciclib has a broader target profile, with activity against CDKs beyond CDK4 and CDK6 [19].

[p] In this study we investigated the impact of the Y537S ESR1 mutation and specific CDK4/6is on clonal evolution, genetic diversity and cell state plasticity, and how they shape the fitness landscape of acquired resistance.

[title] Methods

[title] Clinical sample tissue collection, whole exome sequencing and RNA sequencing

[p] Tissues were collected from patients treated at the Dana- Farber Cancer Institute as described in detail previously and the current cohort is an expansion of the previously published cohort [20]. Metastatic core biopsies were obtained from patients, and samples were immediately snapfrozen in optimal cutting temperature (OCT) and stored in − 80 °C. Archival formalin-fixed, paraffin embedded (FFPE) blocks of primary tumor samples were also obtained. A blood sample was obtained during treatment, and whole blood was stored at − 80 °C until DNA extraction from peripheral blood mononuclear cells (for germline DNA) was performed.

[p] Tissue samples were collected from patients with ER + metastatic breast cancer who were treated with a CDK4/6i, with or without ET, and provided metastatic tumor biopsies (N = 112 biopsies, N = 89 patients). Tumor biopsies were classified as CDK4/6i-sensitive if obtained prior to CDK4/6i treatment from patients who subsequently experienced disease progression after > 6 months of therapy (N = 28 pre-treatment biopsies), acquired resistant if obtained from patients who progressed following > 6 months treatment with a CDK4/6i (N = 53 post-treatment biopsies), and intrinsically resistant if obtained from patients who progressed within six months of treatment initiation (N = 31 pre-treatment biopsies) (details in Additional file 1: Table S1) [21]. This cohort included 23 pre-treatment sensitive tumors matched with post-treatment acquired resistant tumors from the same patient.

[p] Tumor samples from this cohort were subjected to whole exome sequencing (WES) and RNA sequencing (RNA-seq). Clinical annotations were generated from the electronic medical record under the supervision of a board-certified medical oncologist and a cancer registrar (Additional file 1: Table S1). All samples included in this study were voluntarily donated by patients who provided informed consent under an institutional review board (IRB)-approved protocol (Dana-Farber/Harvard Cancer Center no. 05–246), which includes permission for sample acquisition, clinical data abstraction, sample analysis and data sharing. Analysis of biospecimens at the Broad Institute was performed under Broad Institute protocol number 15-370B.

[p] The intrinsic resistant and sensitive cohorts were limited to biopsies that were obtained within 1 year prior to starting treatment with a CDK4/6i. Intrinsic resistance was defined as tumors that developed resistance within 6 months of treatment with a CDK4/6i in the metastatic setting. Sensitive tumors were from patients that had disease stability or responsive disease for at least 6 months in the metastatic setting. Samples with acquired resistance included biopsies that were acquired within 30 days prior to progression and up to 1 year after disease progression. These were samples that were obtained from patients that had disease stability or responsive disease for at least 6 months in the metastatic setting and later developed progressive disease.

[p] These categories were defined based on the 6th and 7th International consensus guidelines for the management of advanced breast cancer (ABC7 guidelines) [21]. The cohort included 23 matched pairs consisting of a pre-treatment, CDK4/6i-sensitive tumor biopsy and a post-treatment, acquired resistant biopsy from the same patient.

[p] Transcriptional analyses were performed only on samples from patients with acquired resistance who had a biopsy within 30 days of disease progression while on treatment (excluding patients who underwent a biopsy after the CDK4/6i was discontinued). Samples with non-pathogenic ESR1 mutation were excluded from the transcriptional analysis.

[p] RNA-seq reads were mapped to the human genome (hg19) with STAR aligner [22] with default parameters. Transcriptome quality was assessed using RNA-SeQC 2 [23] and gene expression quantification was conducted using RSEM [24]. Samples with < 8,000 unique genes were removed from subsequent analyses. Gene expression was measured using upper quartile normalized log2(TPM + 1) values of expressed genes and corrected for tissue type (Frozen/OCT vs. FFPE) with receptor status as a covariate using ComBat [25] in the sva package. Single sample gene set enrichment analysis was performed using the Hallmark gene sets [26, 27].

[p] WES were captured using Illumina technology and the sequencing data processing and analysis was performed using the Picard and Terra pipelines at the Broad Institute. The Picard pipeline (http://picard.sourceforge.net) was used to produce a BAM file with aligned reads. This includes alignment to the GRCh37 human reference sequence using the BWA aligner [28] and estimation and recalibration of base quality score with the Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK) [29]. A custom-made cancer genomics analysis pipeline was used to identify somatic alterations using the Terra platform (https://app.terra.bio/). The CGA WES Characterization pipeline developed at the Broad Institute was used to call, filter and annotate somatic mutations and copy number variation (CNV). GATK CNV was used for the generation of accurate relative copy number profiles from the WES data and reference/alternate read counts at heterozygous single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) sites present in both the normal and tumor samples. After accurate proportional coverage profiles were generated for a sample, Allelic CapSeg tool [30] was used to generate a segmented allelic copy ratio profile. Allelic copy number profile and mutational call data were modeled jointly by ABSOLUTE [31] to produce purity for the samples, a discrete copy number profile, and compute cancer cell fractions (CCF). Tumor samples which had a purity of 8% or more were used for downstream analysis. The clonal structure and clonal evolution of ESR1 mutations was inferred with PhylogicNDT [32].

[p] Additional validation RNA-seq analyses were performed in a smaller sample set of ER + and HER2-negative metastatic breast cancer biopsies with acquired resistance to palbociclib [33]. RNA-seq data for these patient samples are available in dbGaP (study accession phs001285.v2.p1).

[title] Cell lines

[p] MCF7 cells engineered to express a doxycycline (DOX)-inducible-hemagglutinin (HA)-tagged Y537S mutant ER [13] were grown in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 10 µg/ml penicillin–streptomycin-glutamine (PSG) and 500 µg/ml of geneticin (full medium), either without DOX (ER-WT MCF7 cells) or with 1 µg/ml DOX to induce the expression of the Y537S ER mutation (ER-mutant MCF7 cells). This model was extensively characterized in reference 13.

[p] DOX-untreated engineered MCF7 cells were transduced with the ClonTracer library [34], a high-complexity 30 bp-long semi-random DNA barcode library which contains ~ 73 million unique barcodes (Addgene #69830). The packaging of the library into lentiviruses and the infection of the cells were performed following the manufacturer’s instructions. Specifically, 20 million DOX-untreated MCF7 cells were infected with the ClonTracer library. To enhance virus infection, cells were maintained in full medium supplemented with 8 µg/ml of polybrene for 48 h. After transduction, the selection of the barcoded cells was performed by maintaining them in full medium supplemented with 2 µg/ml of puromycin for 96 h. In order to ensure that each cell was infected with one barcode, the multiplicity of infection (MOI) was set at 0.1. The selected barcoded cells (parental cells) were expanded for 3 passages in full medium supplemented with 1 µg/ml of puromycin and the complexity of the library was assessed by barcode sequencing. Next, the parental cells were further expanded and divided to be maintained in full medium supplemented with 1 µg/ml of puromycin, with or without DOX.

[p] To generate three evolutionary replicates of ER-WT and ER-mutant palbociclib-resistant and abemaciclib-resistant cells, DOX-treated and untreated MCF7 cells were cultured in triplicates over time and exposed to escalating doses of the drugs (50 nM, 100 nM, 200 nM, 350 nM, 500 nM, 750 nM and 1 µM) every three passages. Cells were defined as resistant when their ability to proliferate in the presence of palbociclib or abemaciclib 1 µM was restored. Palbociclib-resistant and abemaciclib-resistant cells were regularly maintained in full medium supplemented with 1 µg/ml of puromycin, with or without DOX, and with palbociclib or abemaciclib 1 µM, respectively. In parallel, as controls, ER-WT and ER-mutant MCF7 cells were cultured in vehicle (0.01% dimethyl sulfoxide, DMSO). To ensure equal barcode representation across replicates and minimize stochastic barcode losses over time, 30 million cells were plated at every passage. To assess the barcoding library distribution over time, resistant models were sequenced at three time points: an early time point (after 3 passages in full medium with palbociclib or abemaciclib at 50 nM), an intermediate time point (after 3 passages in full medium with palbociclib or abemaciclib at 200 nM), and at the time of resistance to palbociclib or abemaciclib. Control cells were sequenced at the same timepoints.

[p] WT-ER was overexpressed in WT-ER PalboR MCF7 cells by lentiviral transduction using a pLVX-ESR1_HA_blast plasmid (an ESR1 overexpression construct carrying a C-terminal 3 × HA tag). This plasmid was custom made by GenScript using the pLVX-IRES-puro vector. The pLVX-ESR1_HA_blast plasmid was packaged into viral particles in HEK293T cells using the pMD2.G (Addgene #12259) and psPAX (Addgene #12260) lentiviral packaging vectors. Transduced cells were selected and maintained in full medium supplemented with 5 µg/ml blasticidin for 10 days. After selection, the WT-ER palbociclib-resistant MCF7 cells overexpressing WT-ER (ER-OE PalboR MCF7 cells) were maintained in full medium supplemented with 1 µg/ml puromycin, 5 µg/ml blasticidin, and 1 µM palbociclib.

[p] Cells were authenticated by short tandem repeat profiling (Bio-Synthesis, USA) and regularly tested for mycoplasma contamination using the MycoAlert® Mycoplasma Detection Kit (Lonza) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

[title] Single cell clone isolation

[p] Single cell clones were generated by serial limiting dilutions of either ER-WT or ER-mutant vehicle-treated and palbociclib-resistant MCF7 cells, seeding 1 cell per well in 96-well plates.

[p] To validate that the single cell clones originated from a single cell, single cell subclones were generated by serial limiting dilutions of either ER-WT or ER-mutant palbociclib-resistant clonal cells, seeding 1 cell per well in 96-well plates.

[p] ER-WT and ER-mutant vehicle-control clones were regularly cultured in full medium supplemented with 0.01% DMSO, 1 µg/ml of puromycin, with or without DOX. ER-WT and ER-mutant palbociclib-resistant clones and subclones were regularly cultured in full medium supplemented with palbociclib 1 µM, 1 µg/ml of puromycin, with or without DOX.

[title] Droplet digital PCR (ddPCR) for validation of single cell clones

[p] DNA was extracted from vehicle-control and palbociclib-resistant clones using the Blood & Cell Culture DNA Midi Kit (Qiagen) and quantified by Nanodrop (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The allelic fraction of the barcodes enriched in a clone was assessed using the ddPCR™ Copy Number Variation Assay (Biorad) and the QX200 ddPCR system (Biorad). Data were analyzed with the QuantaSoft software (Biorad). The number of copies of the ASL gene/clone was assessed with a pre-designed assay comprising both a specific ASL HEX probe and ASL-specific primers (dHsaCP2506678, Biorad) and used as a reference. The number of copies of the top enriched barcodes in each clone was checked using the primers and the FAM probes reported in Additional file 2: Table S2 and determined based on the barcode/ASL ratio.

[title] Xenograft studies

[p] Ovariectomized NSG (Jackson, RRID:IMSR JAX:005557) mice that were 5 weeks old were placed on 2000 ppm doxycycline diet and then implanted with 5 million MCF7 cells stably expressing the DOX inducible Y537S ESR1 mutation and the ClonTracer library. Specifically, cells were resuspended in 50% matrigel and injected in the right mammary fat pad. Weekly body weight and tumor caliper measurements were taken for the remainder of the study.

[p] Eight days after cell injection, surgery was performed in 3 mice (#816, #818, #823) to remove the mammary fat pad tumor (average tumor size: 92 mm3) and assess the complexity of the library of the injected cell population. Survival surgery was performed in the remaining 10 mice 28 days after cell injection (average tumor size: 350 mm3). Primary tumors were collected from all the mice and snap frozen in liquid nitrogen. After the removal of the early mammary tumor, local tumor re-growth was monitored by caliper assessments of mammary fat pads. Metastatic tumor growth was monitored with MRI imaging every 3 weeks. Mice were euthanized if they showed significant weight loss due to tumor burden, if tumors exceeded 2 cm in size, or if MRI scans revealed substantial tumor burden. Recurrent tumors grown in the mammary fat pad and distant metastases were collected and snap frozen in liquid nitrogen.

[p] All mice were maintained and all procedures were conducted in accordance with local guidelines and therapeutic interventions approved by the Animal Care and Use Committees of Dana-Farber Cancer Institute, protocol 04–111. Mice were housed up to 5 mice/cage. The room temperature was kept at 20–26 °C and humidity 30–70%. The animals were under a 12 h:12 h light–dark cycle and had ad libitum access to water and irradiated Laboratory Rodent Diet. Animals had at least 3 days of acclimation prior to study initiation. At the experimental endpoint, mice were euthanized by carbon dioxide inhalation using a gradual fill method, followed by a secondary physical method (cervical dislocation) to ensure death, in accordance with institutional animal care guidelines and the American Veterinary Medical Association (AVMA) Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals.

[title] Drugs

[p] Palbociclib (cat. #S1579) and abemaciclib (cat. #S7158) for in vitro studies were purchased from Selleckchem.

[title] Growth assays

[p] Cells were seeded in triplicate in 96-well plates (5,000 cells/well), and 24 h later (day 0) treated with palbociclib, abemaciclib, or vehicle (0.01% DMSO). At specific timepoints cells were stained with 0.4 µg/ml hoechst for nuclear staining and 4 µg/ml propidium iodide to label dead cells. The number of live and dead cells following drug treatments was counted using the Celigo image Cytometer (Nexcelom). For the IC50 studies, cells were counted at day 5 and the number of live cells was normalized against the number of cells treated with the respective drug’s vehicle. IC50 values were next calculated in Graphpad Prism using the log(inhibitor) vs. response – Variable slope (four parameters) function. For the GR metrics, cells were counted at day 0 and at day 6. GR50 and GRmax values were next calculated using the R metrics package (v1.36) (https://bioconductor.org/packages/GRmetrics/). For the growth studies, the number of live cells at different timepoints was normalized to the number of live cells counted at day 0.

[title] Functional analysis of ER-WT and ER-mutant impact on acquired CDK4/6i resistance

[p] To assess the impact of the Y537S ESR1 mutation in the acquired resistance to CDK4/6is, two million ER-mutant and ER-WT palbociclib-resistant MCF7 cells that were maintained in full medium with and without DOX, respectively, and with palbociclib 1 µM, were seeded in 10 cm dishes and cultured both with and without DOX for 4 weeks. At each passage, cells were counted and two million cells were replated. Cell numbers were normalized to the re-plated cell number and plotted.

[p] To determine whether restoring the expression of WT-ER in ER-WT palbociclib-resistant cells is sufficient to resensitize the cells to CDK4/6is, ER-WT palbociclib-resistant MCF7 cells (no-DOX) were transduced to overexpress WT-ER (as above in the cell line section). Two million ER-WT palbociclib-resistant MCF7 cells, with and without ER-WT overexpression, were kept in their respective medium supplemented with palbociclib 1 µM. At each passage, cells were counted and two million cells were plated back. Cell numbers were normalized to the re-plated cell number and plotted.

[title] Western blots

[p] Whole cell protein extraction was performed scraping the cells with RIPA buffer (Boston BioProducts) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Sigma Aldrich). Cell pellets were incubated 30 min at 4 °C while rotating, sonicated for 1 min and centrifuged for 15 min at 4 °C at 14,000 g. Supernatants were assessed by BCA assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific) to measure the protein concentration of the extracts, following the manufacturer’s instructions. Samples were subjected to SDS-PAGE by using NuPAGE 4 to 12% Bis–Tris gels (Life Technologies) and blotted on Trans-Blot Turbo Midi Nitrocellulose membranes (Bio-Rad Laboratories). Primary antibodies: Rb (4H1) (CST #9309, 1:1,000), ER (D6R2W) (CST #13258, 1:1,000) and GAPDH (D16H11) (Brand CST #5174, 1:1,000). Secondary antibodies: anti-rabbit (Invitrogen #31458, 1:2,500) and anti-mouse (Invitrogen #SA1-100, 1:2,500).

[title] Barcodes sequencing

[p] Genomic DNA was extracted from 10 million cells or frozen pulverized tumors using Blood & Cell Culture DNA Kit (Qiagen). Library preparation was performed using 2 µg of genomic DNA, Titanium Taq DNA Polymerase (Takara Bio) and primers following the PCR conditions reported in Additional file 2: Table S2.

[p] The PCR product (~ 183 bp) was purified using the QIAquick PCR purification kit (Qiagen) following the manufacturer’s protocol, quantified by Qubit dsDNA high-sensitivity assay (ThermoFisher Scientific) and sequenced at the Molecular Biology Core Facility of Dana-Farber Cancer Institute by Illumina NS500 Single-End 75 bp sequencing, using the Illumina custom primer GCGACCACCGAGATCTACACACTGACTGCAGTCTGAGTCTGACAG.

[title] Barcode sequencing analyses

[p] The barcode composition analysis was performed using the ClonTracer algorithm as previously described [34] and as reported in Addgene (Addgene #69830, https://www.addgene.org/pooled-library/clontracer/).

[title] Barcode-level analyses

[p] Low-frequency barcodes, defined as barcodes with fractions less than a chosen threshold fraction across all samples for models included in an analysis, were removed prior to the generation of the barcode fraction heatmaps (threshold = 0.01), the hierarchical clustering of all the bulk samples (threshold = 0.001), and the study of the dynamics of cell clusters within each condition (threshold = 0.001). These low-frequency barcodes were retained for barcode-level entropy calculations.

[p] For barcode fraction heatmaps, Euclidean distances between barcodes (samples) were calculated on rows (columns) of the (barcodes x samples) natural-log transformed barcode fraction matrix and hierarchically clustered with complete linkage. A pseudocount of 1e-12 was added to all barcode fractions prior to log transforming for distance calculations.

[p] Barcode distributions were compared across pairs of samples using the log2 unit Jensen-Shannon (JS) divergence using the philentropy (v0.9.0) R package [35]. Matrices of JS divergences among samples were hierarchically clustered using complete linkage. The JS divergences between samples and their respective parental control were compared across three timepoints to assess the progression of clonal selection using two-sided paired t-tests adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Holm procedure with pairing by evolutionary replicate.

[p] Clonal selection among the barcodes at the endpoint for each sample was compared by calculating the normalized, natural-log unit Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence of the sample barcode fraction distribution against a reference discrete uniform distribution over the same number of barcodes and normalizing by the total number of barcodes using the philentropy (v0.9.0) R package [35].

[p] Normalized KL divergences at the barcode level were compared between models using two-sided, unpaired, unequal variances t-tests using rstatix (v0.7.2) (https://doi.org/10.32614/CRAN.package.rstatix) and adjusting for multiple testing with the Holm procedure [36].

[title] Cellular population-level analyses

[p] Cellular populations per model were chosen by taking the (barcode x sample) barcode fraction matrix across one parental model and all 3 timepoints (Early, Middle, End) for all 3 evolutionary replicates of a model (10 samples total) and fitting a Poisson GLM per row and extracting the Poisson Pearson residual (PPR) matrix. Euclidean distances between barcodes were calculated on rows of the PPR matrix and hierarchically clustered with complete linkage. The resulting dendrogram was cut to get 8 cellular populations per model.

[p] Clonal selection among the 8 cellular populations at the End timepoint for each sample per condition was evaluated by calculating the normalized, natural-log KL divergence of the cellular population fraction distribution (rescaled to 1) against a reference discrete uniform distribution over 8 elements using the philentropy (v0.9.0) R package [35].

[p] Normalized KL divergences at the cellular population level were compared between models using unpaired, t-tests with unequal variances, using rstatix (v0.7.2) (https://doi.org/10.32614/CRAN.package.rstatix), and were adjusted for multiple testing with the Holm procedure where appropriate [36].

[p] All barcoding analyses were done in R (v4.3.2, v4.4.1) using tidyverse (v2.0.0) (https://doi.org/10.32614/CRAN.package.tidyverse) packages and readxl (v1.4.5) (https://doi.org/10.32614/CRAN.package.readxl). Plots were created in R, using ggplot2 (v3.5.2) (https://doi.org/10.32614/CRAN.package.ggplot2), RColorBrewer (v1.1–3) (https://doi.org/10.32614/CRAN.package.RColorBrewer), pheatmap (v1.0.12) (https://doi.org/10.32614/CRAN.package.pheatmap), and ggpubr (v0.6.0) (https://doi.org/10.32614/CRAN.package.ggpubr), or in GraphPad Prism (version 10.0.0).

[title] WES of cell models

[p] Genomic DNA was extracted from 10 million cells or frozen pulverized tumors using Blood & Cell Culture DNA Kit (Qiagen). Quality controls, library preparation and WES were performed at Novogene Corporation Inc. Sequencing libraries were generated using Agilent SureSelectXT2 Homo Sapiens All Exon V6 Kit (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA) based on the manufacturer’s recommendations. WES was conducted by Illumina Paired-End 150 bp sequencing.

[title] Cell line WES analysis

[title] Mutation calling

[p] Mutation calling was performed as described here: https://assets-eu.researchsquare.com/files/rs-4270758/v1/784d15e4-765a-4b1e-a02b-7c93680b707c.pdf?c=1716564499. Briefly, somatic single nucleotide variants were called using MuTect [37], followed by filters to remove any mutations due to OxoG artifacts. A BLAT realignment filter was used downstream to remove any mutations having potentially mismapped reads. In our analysis we used the parental cell line as a control sample. For details, refer to code in GitHub (link provided in the Data availability section).

[title] CNV

[p] We used the GATK-CNV (https://github.com/gatk-workflows/gatk4-somatic-cnvs) segmentation algorithm to assign copy-number segments along the exomes. To obtain gene-level copy ratio calls, a read-count normalization was performed by calculating separately for vehicle-control cells, drug-resistant cells and parental cells, the proportion of all reads that mapped to a gene of interest. The samples had systematic differences in coverage; for instance, the samples treated with abemaciclib had greater average depth than most samples treated with palbociclib. To mitigate sample-specific variance differences while preserving within-sample copy-number patterns, we applied per-sample z-score normalization prior to visualization. The normalized proportion of reads that mapped to a gene computed for the vehicle-control and drug-resistant cells was then divided by that one computed for the parental cells, to further normalize the gene-level read count. Code implementation can be found in GitHub (link provided in the Data availability section).

[title] Bulk RNA-seq of cell models

[p] Total RNA was extracted from cells seeded in 6-well plates using TRIzol (Life Technologies) and RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen). Quality controls, library preparation and RNA-seq were performed at the Molecular Biology Core Facility of Dana-Farber Cancer Institute. RNA-seq libraries were prepared using KAPA mRNA HyperPrep Kit (Roche). The RNA was sequenced by Illumina Novaseq6000 Paired-End 150 bp sequencing. RNA extraction and sequencing were performed at least in technical duplicates.

[title] RNA-seq analysis

[p] Sequenced reads were aligned to the hg19 reference genome assembly and samples were analyzed using the pipeline VIPER [38]. Alignment to the hg19 human genome was done using STAR v2.7.0f followed by transcript assembly using cufflinks (v2.2.1) [39] and RseQC (v2.6.2) [40]. Batch effect adjustments were made using ComBat [25]. Differential expression analysis was done using DEseq2 (v1.18.1) [41].

[title] Single cell-RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq)

[p] To perform scRNA-seq analyses we used single-cell suspensions from parental cells and ER-WT and ER-mutant vehicle-control, palbociclib-resistant and abemaciclib-resistant cells. Specifically, for vehicle-control, palbociclib-resistant and abemaciclib-resistant conditions, the scRNA-seq was performed on the evolutionary replicates #3. ScRNA-seq analyses were also performed on the ER-WT palbociclib-resistant clone #3_15.

[p] ScRNA-seq libraries were prepared at the Translational Immunogenic Lab at Dana-Farber Cancer Institute, according to 10 × Genomics Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 3’ v3.1 protocol and using cell stock concentrations with a likelihood of around 8,000 transcriptomes recovering. The quality of the libraries was checked by Bioanalyzer using the High Sensitivity DNA kit (Agilent). The RNA was sequenced by Illumina HiSeq or Novaseq6000 Paired-End 150 bp sequencing at Novogene Corporation Inc.

[title] ScRNA-seq analysis

[p] Samples were aligned with the nf-core/scrnaseq (v2.7.1) [42] pipeline from the collection of nf-core workflows [43] using the Bioconda [44] and Biocontainers [45] environments and Nextflow (v23.04.1). Alignment was done using the default Salmon Alevin [46, 47] pseudoaligner to the hg38 reference genome and GENCODE GTF, using pre-built reference assemblies and annotations from refgenie (v0.12.1) [48, 49] with custom options ‘–profile singularity’, ‘–protocol 10X3’, and ‘–skip-emptydrops’.

[p] Salmon Alevin outputs were imported into R using fishpond (v2.10.0) [50] in mode ‘loadFry(outputFormat = "snRNA")’ and empty droplets and dead cells were filtered out by exploratory data analysis of QC metrics and manually choosing thresholds to keep cells with percent mitochondrial reads ≤ 25%, minimum number of genes with nonzero reads per cell ≥ 100, and minimum total number reads per cell ≥ 10,000. DropletUtils (v1.24.0) emptyDrops function was used to predict empty droplets, keeping cells with an FDR ≤ 0.01 [51, 52]. Count matrices were converted using SeuratObject (v5.0.2) (https://doi.org/10.32614/CRAN.package.SeuratObject) into Seurat (v5.2.1) [53] objects.

[p] Dimensionality reduction was done by Poisson generalized linear model principal components analysis (GLM-PCA) [54, 55] on the gene count matrix as input and considering the top 2,500 genes with the highest deviance across all cells using scry R package (v1.12.0) (https://doi.org/10.18129/B9.bioc.scry) as input to fastglmpca [56] (v0.1–103) (https://doi.org/10.32614/CRAN.package.fastglmpca) with 50 GLM-PCs chosen. A UMAP dimensionality reduction was calculated on the first 50 GLM-PCA principal components (GLM-PCs) as input using the uwot R package (v0.2.3) (https://doi.org/10.32614/CRAN.package.uwot).

[p] Cell–cell distances within each sample were calculated as Euclidean distances on the first 50 GLM-PCs on all pairwise comparisons of 1,000 sampled random cells per sample.

[p] Cell cycle phases were predicted using Seurat’s Cell Cycle Scoring function on expression values from Seurat’s default NormalizeData (normalization.method = 'LogNormalize') normalization.

[p] Clustering was done using kmeans clustering on the first 50 GLM-PCs. A combination of exploratory plots of cell embeddings, silhouette scores, expression of marker gene sets, and clustering tree diagrams examining size, stability, and separation of clusters were used to choose the number of biologically meaningful clusters per sample using the cluster (v2.1.8) (https://doi.org/10.32614/CRAN.package.cluster) and clustree (v0.5.1) R packages [57]. In downstream analyses split by cell cycle phase, clusters were subsequently subdivided into cells of a particular predicted cell cycle phase.

[p] Gene set testing was performed by calculating the quasi-Poisson GLM Wald statistic for each gene for comparing expression rates either between pairs of samples or between clusters within a sample, where each gene for each sample has its own estimated overdispersion term using Matrix (v1.7–3) (https://doi.org/10.32614/CRAN.package.Matrix) and MatrixGenerics (v1.16.0) (https://doi.org/10.18129/B9.bioc.MatrixGenerics). Genes were ranked by these Wald statistics prior to pre-ranked correlation-adjusted mean rank gene set testing using cameraPR [58] from limma (v3.60.6) [59] on the Hallmark gene collection with msigdbr [60] (v7.5.1) (https://doi.org/10.32614/CRAN.package.msigdbr). For gene set testing between two models, genes were ranked by the Wald statistic comparing the difference of expression rates between those two models. For gene set testing of one cluster compared to multiple others (marker gene set setting), we rank genes based on all of the Wald statistics comparing that fixed cluster to the others. For a gene, if all such Wald statistics are positive, we take their minimum as the ranking-statistic for that gene, if all negative, we take their maximum as the ranking-statistic for that gene, and if they are mixed, we take zero as the ranking-statistic for that gene. This prioritizes marker genes that have expression rates that are larger or smaller than all other clusters as distinctive markers and places genes that are mixed among the other clusters in the middle of the ranked gene list. For within-sample across-cluster testing, we omitted any cluster where there were fewer than 50 genes, and for across-cluster comparisons within a cell cycle phase, we compared clusters of cells for a single predicted cycle phase (S, G1, G2M) at a time. Multiple testing of gene sets was accounted for by controlling the false discovery rate (FDR) within families of tests using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure [61].

[p] Copy number inference was run on the gene count matrix with the R package inferCNV (v1.20.0) (inferCNV of the Trinity CTAT Project. https://github.com/broadinstitute/inferCNV), considering the parental cells as the reference population and using the following options: infercnv::run(cutoff = 0.1, cluster_by_groups = TRUE, denoise = TRUE, HMM = FALSE) and infercnv::plot_cnv(cluster_by_groups = TRUE, x.center = 1, x.range = "auto", hclust_method = 'ward.D', color_safe_pal = FALSE, png_res = 300, dynamic_resize = 0).

[p] All scRNA-seq analyses were performed in R (v4.3.2, v4.4.1) using tidyverse (v2.0.0) (https://doi.org/10.32614/CRAN.package.tidyverse) packages. Plots were created using ggplot2 (v3.5.2) (https://doi.org/10.32614/CRAN.package.ggplot2) and RColorBrewer (v1.1–3) (https://doi.org/10.32614/CRAN.package.RColorBrewer).

[title] ATAC-sequencing (ATAC-seq)

[p] Nuclei extraction from vehicle-control, palbociclib-resistant and abemaciclib-resistant MCF7 cells, either ER-WT or ER-mutant, and transposition reactions were performed as previously described [62].

[p] Briefly, cells were collected and washed. Cells pellets were resuspended and incubated for 3 min in 50 µL of cold ATAC-seq resuspension buffer (RSB; 10 mmol/L Tris–HCl pH 7.4, 10 mmol/L NaCl, and 3 mmol/L MgCl2 in water) containing 0.1% NP40, 0.1% Tween-20, and 0.01% digitonin. After the incubation, cells were resuspended in 1 mL of cold RSB containing 0.1% Tween-20 but no NP40 or digitonin and centrifuged. Nuclei were then resuspended in 50 μL of transposition mix (25 μL 2 × TD buffer, 2.5 μL transposase (100 nmol/L final), 16.5 μL PBS, 0.5 μL 1% digitonin, 0.5 μL 10% Tween-20, and 5 μL water). Transposition reactions were incubated at 37 °C for 30 min with 1,000 rpm mixing. Reactions were cleaned up with Qiagen MinElute Kit following the manufacturer’s instruction.

[p] Library preparation was performed as previously described [63] using 5 uM Customized Nextera PCR primers, Invitrogen 10X SYBR Green I, NEBNext High-Fidelity 2X PCR Master Mix (New England Labs).

[p] The amplified libraries were purified using Qiagen PCR Cleanup Kit according to the manufacturer’s protocol and sequenced at Novogene.

[title] ATAC-seq analysis

[p] Analysis was done using COBRA [64] and CHIPS [65]. Sequence tags were aligned to the hg19 reference genome using the Burrows-Wheeler Aligner (BWA) [28]; only uniquely mapped, non-redundant reads were retained. These reads were used to generate binding sites with Model-Based Analysis of ChIP-Seq 2 (MACS v2.1.1.20160309) [66], with an FDR threshold of 0.01. DESeq2 [41] was used to identify differentially accessible regions between drug-resistant vs vehicle-treated cells, ER-mutant vs ER-WT cells, and abemaciclib-resistant vs palbociclib-resistant cells. Gained or lost peaks were defined with the threshold of log2 fold change of > 1 or < – 1, respectively, and adjusted p-value < 0.05. Binding and Expression Target Analysis (BETA basic) (v1.0.7) [67] was used to integrate differentially accessible chromatin sites with differential gene expression data to infer direct target genes.

[title] The Y537S ESR1 mutation shapes the clonal evolution of CDK4/6i-resistant cells

[p] To study the clonal evolution during acquired resistance to CDK4/6is, we employed the ClonTracer DNA barcoding system [34] with three primary objectives: (i) track clonal dynamics during the acquisition of resistance to CDK4/6i, (ii) assess the impact of the ESR1 Y537S mutation on these dynamics, and (iii) compare the clonal dynamics in acquired resistance to palbociclib versus abemaciclib, two distinct CDK4/6is. MCF7 cells with stable expression of a doxycycline (DOX) inducible hemagglutinin (HA)-tagged Y537S ESR1 construct [13] were transduced with the ClonTracer barcoding library at a MOI of 0.1 to minimize multiple barcode integrations per cell (Fig. 1E). Following confirmation of the even distribution and high diversity of the barcoded population (average of unique barcodes = 303,756, 95% CI = 300,796 to 306,715) (Additional file 3: Fig. S1A) (Additional file 4: Table S3), cells were expanded and divided into DOX-treated and untreated flasks, and further subdivided into treatment groups with vehicle-control, escalating doses of palbociclib or escalating doses of abemaciclib. Each condition included three evolutionary replicates plated at a 100-fold barcode representation to limit stochastic losses of barcodes during passaging and to ensure comparable starting barcode representation across replicates. Each replicate constitutes an independent realization of an evolutionary trajectory initiated from the same ancestral, barcoded population and subjected to identical selective pressures.

[p] At baseline, expression of the Y537S ESR1 mutation had a limited impact on sensitivity to either palbociclib or abemaciclib. As expected, exposure to escalating doses of CDK4/6is led to the acquisition of resistance (Additional file 5: Table S4) (Additional file 3: Fig. S1B-H). ESR1 Y537S mRNA measurements confirmed expression of ER mutant transcript in all DOX-treated cells (Fig. 1F). Total ESR1 mRNA levels were increased in palbociclib and abemaciclib-resistant cells expressing the ESR1 Y537S mutation compared to the vehicle-treated counterparts. In contrast, ER-WT resistant cells had lower total ESR1 mRNA levels compared to their vehicle-control (Fig. 1G) (Additional file 6: Table S5). At the protein level, cells without DOX treatment did not express mutant ER, as expected. In these cells, WT-ER expression decreased after acquisition of CDK4/6i resistance. In parental cells, mutant ER expression was confirmed after 72 h of DOX exposure. However, prolonged culture in vehicle led to loss of mutant ER expression, consistent with prior observations that the Y537S ESR1 mutation does not provide a fitness advantage in full medium conditions. In contrast, Y537S ESR1 expressing cells treated with palbociclib or abemaciclib retained mutant ER expression (Fig. 1H). These differences in ER expression in distinct conditions provide initial evidence supporting a role of the Y537S ESR1 mutation in the development of CDK4/6i resistance.

[p] Long-term treatment of cells with vehicle or increasing doses of palbociclib and abemaciclib led to gradual selection of specific cellular subpopulations, as shown by barcode sequencing at the three time points (Fig. 2A-B and Additional file 3: Fig. S2A-B), consistent with recent studies showing that adaptive resistance to target therapies occurs along a continuum [68, 69]. [p] To begin to decipher differences in the barcode selection in WT and mutant ER cells with palbociclib and abemaciclib resistance, we performed unsupervised hierarchical clustering of barcode fractions, restricting the analysis to barcodes with a fraction ≥ 0.01 in at least one sample. In all conditions, the three evolutionary replicates had the strongest level of similarity (Fig. 2C-D). The impact of the expression of the Y537S mutation on the barcode selection differed between palbociclib and abemaciclib resistance. In the palbociclib- and vehicle-treated cells, the primary clustering split grouped the cells by ESR1 mutational status, followed by treatment type (vehicle-control versus palbociclib) (Fig. 2C). Conversely, in the abemaciclib- and vehicle-treated cells, treatment was the dominant driver of clustering, followed by ESR1 mutational status (Fig. 2D). Consistent with these patterns, the normalized Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence, which quantifies the extent to which the barcode distribution in each condition differs from that of the parental population and thus reflects changes in clonal population structure, was higher in palbociclib-resistant WT-ER than in palbociclib-resistant mutant-ER cells (adjusted p-value < 0.01, t-test with each comparison adjusted for multiple testing using the Holm procedure) (Fig. 2E). This finding indicates that the ESR1 mutation is associated with altered clonal dynamics during the evolution of palbociclib resistance, with reduced dependence on marked enrichment of a limited number of subclones. In contrast, the normalized KL divergence was similar between WT-ER and mutant-ER abemaciclib-resistant cells (adjusted p-value > 0.05, t-test with each comparison adjusted for multiple testing using the Holm procedure), suggesting a more limited effect of the mutation on changes in clonal population structure during the acquisition of abemaciclib resistance (Fig. 2F), consistent with the broader target profile of abemaciclib [19]. In both drug-resistant conditions, the normalized KL divergence was higher compared to the vehicle-control treated cells, consistent with greater shifts in barcode composition and reduced clonal diversity after the acquisition of CDK4/6i resistance. Collectively, these results provide evidence of clonal selection during the acquisition of CDK4/6i resistance, however, they do not rule out the contributions of cell plasticity and adaptive transitions during resistance. These results also support a more prominent role for ESR1 Y537S in the remodeling of the clonal population structure during palbociclib resistance than abemaciclib resistance.

[p] Although enriched barcodes aggregated the evolutionary replicates, the replicates did not fully overlap. In this experimental design, divergence in adaptive trajectories across evolutionary replicates is the expected null outcome. In the palbociclib-resistant models, only 15.4% of the enriched barcodes in ER-WT cells and 55.6% of enriched barcodes in ER-mutant cells were shared across all the three replicates (ER-WT: average sum of the fractions of the shared barcodes was 0.79; ER-mutant: average sum of the fractions of the shared barcodes was 0.81) (Additional file 3: Fig. S2C-D). Similarly, in the abemaciclib-resistant models, 22.7% of the barcodes enriched in ER-WT cells and 44.4% of the barcodes enriched in ER-mutant cells were shared across all the three evolutionary replicates (ER-WT: average sum of the fractions of the shared barcodes was 0.83; ER-mutant: average sum of the fractions of the shared barcodes was 0.43) (Additional file 3: Fig. S2E-F).

[p] To quantify the differences in clonal population structure across all cell models, we calculated pairwise Jensen-Shannon (JS) divergence between sample barcode distributions, with lower values indicating compositional similarity and higher values indicating greater divergence. JS divergence was generally low within the evolutionary replicates for ER-WT palbociclib-resistant cells (range: 0.07–0.13) and mutant palbociclib-resistant cells (range: 0.2–0.41), whereas it was higher for the ER-WT abemaciclib-resistant (range, 0.2–0.52) and mutant abemaciclib-resistant (range: 0.11–0.42) samples (Fig. 2G-H). These findings indicate that the degree of clonal population restructuring differed between the palbociclib- and abemaciclib-resistant models, consistent with distinct evolutionary responses to these two CDK4/6is.

[p] We also calculated the JS divergence between the samples over three time points (early, middle and end), and their respective parental control, which revealed a gradual increase in JS divergence from baseline over the course of resistance acquisition (two-sided paired t-tests, with each set of timepoint comparisons adjusted for multiple testing within a mutational status and treatment using the Holm procedure) (Additional file 3: Fig. S2G-H). [title] Inferred cellular populations reveal ESR1 mutant and CDK4/6i specific dependent dynamics

[p] In a barcoded cell population, it is unlikely that every unique barcode represents a truly distinct cellular state. Moreover, despite using a low MOI, a fraction of cells is likely to be infected with more than one barcode. To address these limitations, we inferred cellular populations by grouping barcodes with similar temporal trajectories, tracking changes in barcode fractions over time. Using this approach, we identified 8 cellular populations in each model. These populations were reproducible across the three evolutionary replicates within each model but showed substantial variability and limited overlap across models (Additional file 3: Fig. S3A-F). Comparing population dynamics across models, ESR1 Y537S expression was associated with earlier enrichment in vehicle-treated and palbociclib or abemaciclib-resistant cell populations (Fig. 3D-G and Additional file 3: Fig. S3G-J). [p] We next examined the scRNA-seq data of each cell model and observed structured transcriptional heterogeneity. These patterns were independent of the cell cycle states, evidence for biological meaningful transcriptional heterogeneity (Fig. 6E-J and Additional file 3: Fig. S7D-E). Pathway analysis comparing each cluster to the remaining cells within each model indicated that these transcriptional states were biologically distinct. As an example, in the ER-mutant palbociclib-resistant cells one cluster was enriched for TNF-alpha signaling, a second was enriched in MYC targets and cell proliferation pathways, and a third was enriched in ER response pathways. Similarly, the cell line derived from a single palbociclib-resistant cell harboring an RB1 deletion (Fig. 6H) consisted of three transcriptionally defined cell states; cluster 1 had higher expression of genes related to ER, TNF-alpha and TGF-beta, cluster 2 exhibited higher expression of genes involved in EMT, and cluster 3 was enriched in genes of the mTOR signaling and cell proliferation pathways. Importantly, these differences remained evident even after accounting for the cell cycle phase (Additional file 3: Fig. S8). In aggregate, our data suggests that transcriptional heterogeneity and plasticity may enable cells to adapt to treatment or other stressors. We showed that even in a population derived from a single cell harboring a defined genetic mechanism of resistance there is transcriptional heterogeneity, consistent with the coexistence of genetic and epigenetic mechanisms of resistance.

[p] Because WT-ER resistant cells showed reduced ER expression, we next tested whether loss of ER is required for resistance. First, we induced the expression of the ESR1 mutant trans-gene in the palbociclib-resistant ER-WT cells treating them with DOX. [title] Subpopulations with a shared fitness for resistance and metastases

[p] We previously demonstrated that activating ESR1 mutations drive metastases in vivo [13]. To place these findings within the same clonal-dynamics framework used for our CDK4/6 inhibitor resistance studies, we profiled barcode selection in mammary gland xenografts, local recurrences, and distant metastases. We also asked whether the cellular populations that expand during distant metastases in vivo overlap with those selected during the development of CDK4/6i resistance in vitro, which would suggest shared fitness advantages across these evolutionary contexts. MCF7 cells stably expressing the DOX inducible Y537S ESR1 mutation and the ClonTracer library were injected into the mammary fat pads of ovariectomized mice and mice were treated with DOX. Tumors from three mice were harvested at 1 week to assess the barcode complexity, while tumors from the remaining ten mice were resected after 4 weeks (salvage surgery) (Fig. 7F). Mice were then monitored for local and distant recurrences. Barcode sequencing of the 1-week tumors confirmed an even distribution of the barcodes with a barcode complexity of more than 80,000 unique barcodes (average of unique barcodes = 81,493, 95% CI = 75,949 to 87,937) (Additional file 4: Table S3). We confirmed that the tumors contained barcodes that were found to be enriched in the CDK4/6i-resistant cells (Additional file 3: Fig. S9A-D), indicating that the clones capable of expanding under therapeutic pressure were present within the initial population. By 4 weeks, the mammary xenografts showed substantial clonal selection, with ~ 30,000 unique barcodes remaining (average of unique barcodes = 30,413, 95% CI = 26,651 to 34,176) (Additional file 4: Table S3).

[p] We next analyzed local recurrences and distant metastases. The growth rates and clonal selection patterns of the mammary gland recurrent tumors were highly variable (Fig. 7G-I). For example, a slow growing tumor that emerged after day 105 (#821) exhibited strong clonal selection with high normalized KL divergence (#821_1: normalized KL = 0.78, #821_2: normalized KL = 0.79) and was dominated by four barcodes contributing to the tumor, while a fast-growing tumor (#816) had a lower normalized KL divergence (#816_1: normalized KL = 0.44, #816_2: normalized KL = 0.51). The clonal diversity was variable across distant metastatic lesions and were dependent on the site of the metastasis (Fig. 7J-K). Liver metastases exhibited strong enrichment of 1–2 dominant barcodes, consistent with high normalized KL divergence compared to primary-early (DM = −0.84, 95% UMCI = [−0.87,−0.81], t(9.22) = −62.54, padj < 1e-06) and compared to primary-late (DM = −0.74, 95% UMCI = [−0.77,−0.71], t(14.67) = −47.78, padj < 1e-06) samples (DM: difference in means, 95% UMCI: 95% unadjusted marginal confidence interval, padj: adjusted p-value, two-sided unequal-variances t-test with each comparison adjusted for multiple testing with the Holm procedure). In contrast, spleen and lung metastases retained more diverse barcode representation and lower normalized KL divergence values. Spleen metastases had KL divergences closer to primary-early (DM = −0.29, 95% UMCI = [−0.72,0.14], t(2.00) = −2.87, padj = 3.1e-01) and primary-late samples (DM = −0.19, 95% UMCI = [−0.62,0.24], t(2.03) = −1.90, padj = 3.1e-01).

[p] To determine whether the clonal populations selected in vivo were related to those enriched during drug resistance, we compared barcodes enriched in the CDK4/6i-resistant cell lines with those detected in recurrent and metastatic tumors. We observed significant overlap between barcodes enriched in palbociclib-resistant ER-WT cells and those found in the local recurrent tumor and spleen metastases from mouse #817, as well as lung metastases from mouse #816. We also observed overlap between barcodes enriched in ER-mutant palbociclib-resistant cells and those present in spleen metastases from mouse #817 and lung metastases from mouse #816 (Fig. 7L and Additional file 3: Fig. S9E). Together, these data extend our clonal-dynamics framework from in vitro resistance to in vivo progression, showing that tumor evolution is shaped by both context-specific selection and shared clonal fitness programs. In this model, genomic resistance-associated changes may contribute to the fitness of specific clones, while the metastatic niche further constrains which barcoded populations ultimately expand, thereby linking genetic evolution, clonal selection, and organ-specific outgrowth.

[title] Discussion

[p] Multiple mechanisms of resistance to CDK4/6is in ER + breast cancer have been identified [83, 84]. These include activation of upstream effectors, downstream bypass mechanisms and alterations in the CDK4/6-cyclin D1-Rb axis. Here, we combined high-complexity barcoding with longitudinal sampling and multi-omic profiling to resolve how clonal selection and cell plasticity unfolds during CDK4/6i resistance, how this process is shaped by an ESR1 mutation, and whether different CDK4/6is drive distinct evolutionary outcomes. Three key insights emerge from our study: (i) ESR1 mutations are enriched in clinical samples of acquired CDK4/6i resistance and can expand to near clonality, (ii) ESR1 Y537S measurably reshapes clonal and epigenetic evolution under palbociclib but has a weaker impact under abemaciclib, and (iii) palbociclib and abemaciclib impose distinct selective pressures leading to divergent resistant states with limited clonal overlap and asymmetric cross-resistance.

[p] The ClonTracer profiling demonstrated marked selection of barcodes and a decrease in clonal diversity under drug pressure. However, evolutionary replicate overlap of enriched barcodes was incomplete, indicating that multiple evolutionary routes to resistance can co-exist with potentially multiple collateral sensitivities. At the same time, replicate clustering and within-condition similarity suggested convergence that can arise from biological constraints influenced by the drug and genetic background. Importantly, even within dominant resistant clones, single-cell transcriptomic analyses reveal substantial transcriptional heterogeneity, including coexisting cell-cycle, estrogen-responsive, EMT-like, and stress-associated programs. This heterogeneity persists even in populations derived from a single resistant cell, highlighting cell plasticity and the divergence in CDK4/6i resistance. Additionally, increasing evidence suggests that therapeutic resistance does not arise through a binary transition from drug sensitivity to resistance but rather emerges along a continuum of progressively adaptive cellular states shaped by selective pressure [68]. Our findings strongly support this model in the context of CDK4/6i resistance. Longitudinal sequencing of the high-complexity DNA barcoding demonstrated that resistance is driven by progressive enrichment barcodes. Together, these observations align with a model in which genetic predisposition and transcriptional plasticity cooperate to enable incremental gains in fitness along a resistance landscape, challenging binary sensitive/resistant classifications and underscoring the dynamic, multifactorial nature of acquired CDK4/6i resistance.

[p] The role of the Y537S ESR1 mutation in ET resistance is well established [11]. There is also evidence that ESR1 mutations have a role in resistance to the combination of ET and palbociclib [6, 17, 18]. However, it is unknown if this effect is due to ET resistance only or whether the ESR1 mutations also contribute to resistance to CDK4/6is. Our study indicates that the expression of the Y537S ESR1 mutation, which is the activating ESR1 mutation with the most robust phenotypes [13, 16], influences cell adaptation, selection pressures and clonal evolution under treatment, but it is not required to maintain resistance once resistance is established. Specifically, Y537S ESR1 mutation is associated with sustained mutant ER expression and broader clonal diversity in palbociclib-resistant models, suggesting reduced selection pressure for subclones harboring other drivers of resistance. In contrast, its impact on abemaciclib resistance was less pronounced, with similar clonal diversity between ER-WT and ER-mutant models. These two drugs also have distinct clinical properties, including their side effect profiles [85], the demonstration of single agent activity with abemaciclib [86], and the ability of abemaciclib to reduce disease recurrence in the adjuvant setting [87]. Both our findings and these clinical observations likely reflect differences in the inhibitory profiles of the two drugs. Palbociclib is a relatively selective CDK4/6 inhibitor, whereas abemaciclib exhibits greater affinity for CDK4, and displays broader activity against additional CDKs, including CDK1, CDK2 and CDK9 inhibition [19]. Notably, because CDK9 signaling does not directly converge with the ER axis, its inhibition by abemaciclib may explain the diminished influence of mutant ER on the acquisition of resistance to abemaciclib compared with palbociclib resistance. Furthermore, the barcode selection differed between palbociclib and abemaciclib irrespective of the ER mutant status, highlighting distinct resistance mechanisms. This effect is further supported by our observation that abemaciclib-resistant cells exhibit cross-resistance to palbociclib, whereas palbociclib-resistant cells remain sensitive to abemaciclib, albeit with higher IC50 values. These findings align with a previous pre-clinical study [88] and the postMONARCH clinical trial, which demonstrated clinical benefit from abemaciclib treatment after progression on palbociclib treatment suggesting non-overlapping resistance mechanisms between these inhibitors [89].

[p] Our integrated ATAC-seq and RNA-seq profiling revealed therapy-induced shifts in chromatin accessibility and gene expression programs, underscoring an interplay between genetic and epigenetic mechanisms of resistance. Principal component analysis of ATAC-seq data separated drug-resistant cells from vehicle-treated controls, indicating widespread chromatin remodeling during resistance. In the palbociclib-resistant condition, Y537S ER-mutant cells formed a distinct epigenetic cluster apart from ER-WT clones, whereas abemaciclib-resistant cells showed a more consolidated chromatin state regardless of ESR1 mutational status. This finding mirrors the clonal barcode patterns and suggests that the ESR1 mutation drives unique chromatin reprogramming under palbociclib pressure that is not observed with abemaciclib. In addition, the changes in chromatin state further underscore the cell plasticity during resistance and the importance of targeting multiple pathways after CDK4/6i resistance.

[p] The complexity of clonal evolution was further evident when we extended our barcode analysis to an in vivo setting. Using barcoded MCF7 cells implanted as mammary fat pad xenografts, we examined how clones evolved during tumor growth, regression, and metastasis in the presence of an active Y537S mutant ER. Even in the absence of drug treatment, we found substantial clonal selection during tumor progression and spread. The dependence of clonal dominance on metastatic site, as demonstrated by the strong clonal selection in the liver versus greater diversity in lung and spleen, suggests organ-specific constraints on colonization. Overlap between barcodes enriched in palbociclib-resistant states in vitro and those enriched in select recurrent and metastatic lesions suggest that some subpopulations have shared fitness advantages relevant to both therapeutic resistance and metastatic competence.

[p] Our study has a few limitations. We used an engineered inducible ESR1 Y537S system enabling controlled lineage tracing, but this system cannot capture all biologically relevant selective pressures. Specifically, interactions between cancer cells and the tumor microenvironment cells are not accounted for. Future studies should extend lineage tracing to in vivo drug treatment in immune competent models. Since the barcodes in the ClonTracer library are not expressed we could not link all the enriched subpopulations with their specific transcriptional profiles. In addition, given the relatively small number of evolutionary replicates, we cannot definitively rule out the possibility that the observed convergence is influenced, at least in part, by sampling bias.

[title] Conclusions

[p] Our study shows that in ER + breast cancer the evolution of resistance to CDK4/6is is jointly shaped by clonal selection and adaptive state remodeling, supporting the need for combination treatments after the acquisition of resistance. The Y537S ESR1 mutation and the specific CDK4/6is are both major determinants of the evolutionary resistance trajectories. The ESR1 Y537S mutation promotes distinct palbociclib-associated chromatin and transcriptional programs during acquisition of resistance but becomes dispensable for resistance maintenance once alternative sustaining pathways are established. Palbociclib and abemaciclib drive distinct evolutionary outcomes with limited clonal overlap and impose different selective landscapes. These findings underscore the need to consider ER mutational status and inhibitor-specific evolutionary pressures when designing sequential approaches and combination strategies.
