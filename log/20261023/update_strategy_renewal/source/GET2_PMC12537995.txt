CD16A Shedding Regulates Innate Cell Engager‐Induced Serial Killing by Natural Killer Cells
Corresponding author.

ABSTRACT
Natural killer (NK) cells can protect from tumor‐transformed cells using a fine‐tuned machinery of activating and inhibiting receptors. An important activating receptor is Fc gamma receptor IIIa (FcγRIIIA or CD16A), which can trigger antibody‐dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) when recognizing antibody‐opsonized target cells. One strategy to boost ADCC responses may be achieved by inhibiting activation‐induced shedding of CD16A from the NK cell surface. However, previous preclinical studies have shown contrasting results regarding the effectiveness and limitations of this approach. Here, microchip‐based live cell‐imaging was used to assess the consequences of CD16A shedding inhibition on the dynamics of NK cell cytotoxicity. The bispecific innate cell engager acimtamig (AFM13) was superior to IgG1 monoclonal antibodies in ADCC and in increasing the fraction of cytotoxic NK cells and serial killers. Under conditions where CD16A shedding was inhibited, acimtamig still triggered ADCC; however, the ability to promote serial killing was reduced and associated with impaired NK cell detachment from target cells. These results demonstrate that CD16A shedding represents an intrinsic feature of NK cell biology that is critical to sustain the antitumoral cytotoxicity of NK cells. This has implications for CD16A engineering of NK cell products and their combination with CD16A‐directed NK cell engagers.
Keywords:  acimtamig, CD16 shedding, innate cell engager, microchip, NK cells, serial killing
NK cells kill tumor targets via antibody‐dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC), mediated by the receptor CD16A. We show that the bispecific innate cell engager acimtamig (AFM13) enhances ADCC and boosts NK cell serial killing. Live‐cell imaging revealed that CD16A shedding is essential for efficient NK cell detachment and sustained killing.

Introduction
Natural killer (NK) cells respond to malignantly transformed and virus‐infected cells by direct cytotoxicity and release of pro‐inflammatory cytokines. NK cells can be divided into two main subsets based on their expression of CD56 and the IgG receptor FcγRIIIA (CD16A). CD56 bright  CD16A −  NK cells show strong cytokine production, while CD56 dim  CD16A +  NK cells instead show strong cytotoxic activity and represent approximately 90% of circulating NK cells in blood and spleen [ 1 ,  2 ]. NK cells discriminate between healthy and diseased cells through a balance between inhibitory and activating signals, mediated by cell surface receptors. Important activating receptors include CD16A, NKG2D, DNAM‐1, and natural cytotoxicity receptors [ 3 ]. NK cell cytotoxicity is dependent on immune synapse formation—a tight intercellular junction between the NK cell and target cell—where the NK cell assembles its cytotoxic machinery, culminating in delivery of granules containing perforin and granzyme, or the presentation of death receptors [ 4 ,  5 ].
Upon cellular activation, the CD16A receptor can be cleaved from the cell surface of neutrophils and NK cells by the metalloprotease (MMP), a disintegrin and metalloprotease‐17 (ADAM‐17) or membrane type 6 matrix metalloproteinase [ 6 ,  7 ,  8 ]. CD16A shedding can also be induced by other stimuli, for example, NKG2D [ 9 ] or cytokines [ 10 ,  11 ,  12 ]. Therefore, a strategy to enhance antibody‐dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) by NK cells is to use molecular inhibitors or protein constructs that prevent shedding of CD16A [ 13 ,  14 ]. While this approach aims to maximize CD16A availability for ADCC [ 15 ,  16 ], and has shown increased cytokine production by NK cells [ 6 ,  17 ], studies have shown that inhibition of CD16A shedding can lead to impaired detachment from target cells, reducing the capacity for an individual NK cell to form serial contacts with target cells [ 17 ] and even induce NK cell death [ 9 ]. Altogether, the data have remained ambiguous regarding the overall consequences and dynamic changes associated with CD16A shedding inhibition.
Bispecific NK cell engagers have emerged as a successful strategy for increased immune cell activation and tumor cell killing [ 18 ,  19 ,  20 ,  21 ]. Engagers with specificity for the activating receptor CD16A can selectively trigger ADCC by NK cells or antibody‐dependent cellular phagocytosis (ADCP) by macrophages. The CD30/CD16A‐specific innate cell engager (ICE) acimtamig (AFM13) has shown efficacy in patients with CD30 +  lymphomas as a monotherapy [ 22 ], in combination with the checkpoint inhibitor, pembrolizumab [ 23 ], and in combination with adoptive NK cell therapy [ 24 ].
Here, live cell imaging with single‐cell resolution was used to assess how stimulation with ICE molecules affects the dynamics of NK cell cytotoxicity and the impact of CD16A shedding in these processes. We show that high‐affinity CD16A engagement by acimtamig can increase both the fraction of cytotoxic NK cells and the number of target cells killed by individual NK cells (serial killing). The elevated fraction of cytotoxic NK cells induced by ICE molecules is largely conserved under conditions when CD16A shedding is inhibited. However, analysis of the functional dynamics of NK cells in combination with acimtamig reveals that inhibition of CD16A shedding impairs NK cell detachment from target cells, resulting in cell cluster formation, reduced NK cell motility, and decreased NK cell viability. Together, these factors lead to reduced NK cell serial killing. Although shedding inhibition might be effective in the short term, it can impact sequential targeting of tumor cells and decrease the viability of individual NK cells and, therefore, limit the overall antitumoral activity.
Results
Acimtamig Induces Potent NK Cell Responses Against CD30‐Expressing Target Cells
The CD30‐targeting ICE acimtamig has previously been shown to efficiently induce cytotoxic activity of NK cells toward CD30‐expressing target cells via ADCC [ 24 ,  25 ,  26 ]. Here, those results are corroborated and show that, compared with CD30 mAb or an antibody containing S239D and I332E modifications to increase CD16 binding (here called CD30 Enh‐mAb) [ 26 ,  27 ,  28 ], acimtamig induced significantly greater NK cell degranulation and IFNγ productionagainst the CD30 high  and CD30 low  expressing cell lines Karpas‐299 and Nalm‐6, respectively (Figure  1A,B ; Figure  S1A–C ).

To investigate the concentration dependence and kinetics of bulk NK cell‐induced ADCC, the Incucyte live‐cell analysis system was used (Figure  1C–G ). NK cell cytotoxicity against Karpas‐299 and Nalm‐6 in the presence of acimtamig, CD30 mAb, or CD30 Enh‐mAb was induced in a dose‐dependent manner (Figure  1D,E ; Figure  S1D–E ), where acimtamig induced a cytotoxic response at significantly lower concentrations compared with IgG antibodies for both target cells (Figure  S1F,G ). Stimulation with an anti‐CD16 mAb targeting the same anti‐CD16A domain as acimtamig and with a silenced Fc part, making it unable to induce ADCC, showed no NK cell activation. Kinetics analyses showed that a significant part of the acimtamig‐induced killing was happening within the first hour for Karpas‐299, while the kinetics of Nalm‐6 killing were slower (Figure  1F,G ; Figure  S1H,I ).
In summary, in the presence of acimtamig, NK cell cytotoxicity against CD30 high ‐ and CD30 low ‐expressing tumor cells was induced in a dose‐ and time‐dependent manner, with superior potency when compared with wild‐type and Fc‐enhanced IgG molecules.
Acimtamig Increases the Fraction of Cytotoxic NK Cells and Boosts Serial Killing
To assess the cytotoxic potential of individual NK cells, microchip‐based live cell screening was performed (Figure  2A ) [ 29 ]. Fluorescently labelled primary human NK cells and target cells were seeded into microwells of the chip, aiming for on average one NK cell and four target cells in each well. Once settled, the number of cells and their viability status in each well was assessed every hour by microscopy. Acimtamig stimulation triggered a significantly higher fraction of cytotoxic NK cells both toward Karpas‐299 and Nalm‐6 target cells compared with untreated controls (Figure  2B ; Figure  S2 ). Next, acimtamig was compared with CD30 mAb and CD30 Enh‐mAb. Against CD30 high  Karpas‐299 target cells, both acimtamig and CD30 Enh‐mAb induced a significantly higher fraction of cytotoxic NK cells compared with CD30 mAb and control (Figure  2C ). However, against CD30 low  Nalm‐6 target cells, acimtamig triggered NK cell cytotoxicity significantly above CD30 Enh‐mAb (Figure  2D ).

Our microchip‐based live cell imaging system is particularly suitable to assess the NK cell serial killing capacity, by selectively analyzing microwells containing a single NK cell with multiple (here set to ≥3) target cells (Figure  2E ). Against Karpas‐299, both acimtamig and CD30 Enh‐mAb showed elevated fractions of NK cells performing multiple kills, while only acimtamig had that effect in Nalm‐6 cells (Figure  2F–H ). When depicting the fraction of cytotoxic NK cells that killed at least three target cells (serial killers), this became even more evident (Figure  2G–I ).
These data demonstrate that high‐affinity engagement of CD16A by acimtamig increases the fraction of cytotoxic NK cells and boosts the fraction of serial killers against CD30‐expressing target cells.
Acimtamig‐Induced Serial Killing Is Decreased by Inhibition of CD16A Shedding
The effect of CD16A shedding on serial engagement of target cells by NK cells was investigated using our microchip technology (Figure  3 ). CD16A shedding was inhibited by exposing human primary NK cells to Batimastat, a broad‐spectrum MMP inhibitor, while interacting with CD30 intermediate  target cells (Figure  S1A ), HL‐60, or CD30 low  target cells Nalm‐6 (Figure  3A–F , G–K , respectively). In agreement with previous studies [ 25 ,  30 ], the flow cytometry experiment confirmed that Batimastat inhibited CD16 shedding on NK cells cocultured with Nalm‐6 target cells in the presence of acimtamig (Figure  S3A ). HL‐60 target cells were introduced since Batimastat treatment was toxic to Karpas‐299 target cells. Similar toxicity was not observed in the other two target cell lines or in the NK cells (Figure  S3B,C ). With HL‐60 target cells, acimtamig treatment increased the fraction of time spent in conjugates, and inhibition of CD16A shedding increased that fraction further (Figure  3B ). This appeared to be due to impaired detachment between NK and target cells since NK cells commonly remained in conjugation with killed target cells, resulting in the formation of large clusters of cells (Figure  3A ). Similarly, when NK cells were incubated with the adherent epidermal growth factor (EGFR)‐expressing cell line DK‐MG together with the EGFR/CD16 bispecific ICE AFM24 and Batimastat, large clusters of target cells were formed around the NK cells (Figure  S3D ), and a significant increase in the fraction of NK cells involved in long‐lasting contacts was observed (Figure  S3E ). For the CD30 low  target Nalm‐6, the fraction of time in conjugation was not significantly elevated (Figure  3G ).

NK cells efficiently killed HL‐60 target cells, and the number of cytotoxic cells increased with acimtamig irrespective of Batimastat co‐treatment (Figure  3C ). Conversely, the fraction of cytotoxic NK cells responding to Nalm‐6 target cells upon co‐treatment of acimtamig and Batimastat showed a decreasing trend (Figure  3H ). As suggested in previous reports [ 9 ,  17 ], we here show that inhibition of CD16A shedding affected the cytotoxic potential of individual NK cells (Figure  3D,I ). Acimtamig‐induced NK cell serial killing was reduced in the presence of Batimastat (Figure  3D,E , I,J ). Furthermore, in line with previous reports [ 9 ], it was observed that the fraction of NK cells that died after performing killing was elevated when treated with Batimastat (Figure  3F,K ).
These results show that inhibition of CD16A shedding does not reduce the fraction of acimtamig‐triggered cytotoxic NK cells but leads to impaired NK cell detachment, which impedes serial killing and possibly lowers NK cell viability.
Expression of a Noncleavable CD16A Reduces Acimtamig‐Induced NK Serial Killing
Next, a Batimastat‐independent system using variants of the NK92 NK cell line overexpressing either noncleavable high‐affinity CD16A (NK92 HA‐NC) or cleavable high‐affinity CD16A (NK92 HA) [ 31 ,  32 ] was used. Consistent with these CD16A variants, NK92 HA showed CD16A downregulation in response to susceptible HL‐60 target cells, both in the absence and presence of acimtamig, suggesting that cleavable CD16A is effectively downregulated also by the ADCC‐independent activation of the NK cells. In contrast, little downregulation was observed for noncleavable NK92 HA‐NC (Figure  4A,B ). The residual decrease of CD16A in the presence of acimtamig in noncleavable NK92 HA‐NC may be suggestive of an alternative mechanism for downregulation, for example, internalization [ 33 ].

A similar analysis of CD16A expression after interaction with Karpas‐299 and Nalm‐6 target cells was also performed (Figure  S4A ). For NK92 HA, CD16A was downregulated in an acimtamig‐dependent fashion for Karpas‐299, but only to a small extent for Nalm‐6, both with and without acimtamig. CD16A downregulation in NK92 HA‐NC did occur for Karpas‐299 treated with acimtamig, supporting the presence of an additional mechanism for downregulation besides shedding.
All three NK92 cell lines were also evaluated in a standard 4 h degranulation (CD107a) assay (Figure  S4B ). NK92 HA and HA‐NC showed similar degranulation trends for Karpas‐299, HL‐60, and Nalm‐6, with increased degranulation in the presence of acimtamig to varying extent. Due to the possible internalization process in Karpas‐299, we chose Nalm‐6 and HL‐60 for microchip‐based live cell imaging.
The dynamics and cytotoxicity of NK92 HA and NK92 HA‐NC cells were assessed in microwell chip experiments using NK92 cells co‐incubated with HL‐60 (Figure  4C–H ) or Nalm‐6 cells (Figure  S5 ) with or without acimtamig. Both NK92 cell line variants spent a large fraction (>80%) of time in conjugation with HL‐60 target cells (Figure  4D ) and showed high (>90%) cytotoxicity (Figure  4E ). For Nalm‐6, the fractions of times in conjugates were lower (<50%) in all conditions (Figure  S5A ), while the fraction of cytotoxic cells varied between 45%–80%, with a significant increase when introducing acimtamig to NK92 HA (Figure  S5B ). A majority of NK92 cells killed multiple HL‐60 targets independent of condition, with a shift in distribution between cleavable and noncleavable CD16A in the presence of acimtamig (Figure  4F ). Consistently, serial killing was reduced for NK92 HA‐NC cells in combination with acimtamig (Figure  4G ). For Nalm‐6, serial killing increased moderately with the addition of acimtamig (Figure  S5C,D ). NK viability showed minor differences between conditions for HL‐60 (Figure  4H ) but decreased for Nalm‐6 when acimtamig was introduced (Figure  S5E ), in particular, for NK92 NC‐HA. Thus, in our system expression of cleavable CD16A can increase the effector's ability to perform serial killing.
Discussion
Serial killing is an important characteristic of NK cell cytotoxicity, and in vitro experiments have shown that a low fraction of effective NK cells can be responsible for a majority of the target cell killing [ 34 ,  35 ]. Serial killing requires an NK cell to repeatedly locate, recognize, and kill new target cells. The factors that are essential for serial killing have not been completely elucidated, but limiting factors could be NK cell motility, maintenance of activating signaling, repeated mobilization of the cytotoxic machinery, and effective detachment from previous target cells. Often, the majority of the NK cells in a population will not perform multiple kills and may not become activated by a certain tumor target cell [ 34 ,  35 ]. Antibody‐based NK cell engagers have proven an effective approach to increase the fraction of NK cells capable of launching a response against otherwise “invisible” tumor target cells [ 18 ,  19 ,  20 ,  21 ]. While the recognition and cytotoxic phases of killing have been described [ 4 ], the disassembly of the immune synapse and disengagement are less well understood, although some studies have been published in recent years [ 36 ,  37 ]. Further understanding of NK cell viability and function is of particular importance for the development of approaches to increase NK cell responses in the clinic.
In this study, we used a microwell chip assay to show that the bispecific acimtamig potently increases NK‐cell activation compared with two monoclonal antibodies targeting the same CD30 epitope. These results indicate that the high‐affinity engagement of CD16A by acimtamig is superior to CD30 Enh‐mAb, leading to increased fractions of cytotoxic cells and serial killing against CD30‐expressing target cells. The strong killing response at low concentrations of acimtamig could represent a clinical advantage, providing the opportunity for quantitative clearance of malignantly transformed cells, which might otherwise give rise to residual disease and relapse.
It is well established that NK cell activation leads to MMP‐induced shedding of CD16A [ 6 ,  13 ], thus potentially leading to a decreased ability for NK cells to maintain activating signaling through this receptor in sequential encounters with target cells. However, when using the broad MMP inhibitor, Batimastat, serial killing was decreased, both for HL‐60 and Nalm‐6, with no or small differences observed in the fraction of cytotoxic NK cells. For HL‐60, this was accompanied by an increased fraction of time NK cells spent in conjugation with target cells and the formation of large cell clusters around the effectors. Long‐lasting conjugation and cluster formation were even more pronounced when using the adherent target cell line DK‐MG in combination with the CD16/EGFR bispecific ICE, AFM24. NK92 cells killed HL‐60 target cells very effectively, with high frequencies of cytotoxic cells and serial killers, also without the addition of acimtamig. Despite this background killing, it was observed that serial killing was reduced for the NK92 cell line expressing the noncleavable version of CD16A in combination with acimtamig. This difference was, however, not observed for the target cell line Nalm‐6. Together, these results show that the strong affinity induced by the ICE molecules in combination with inhibition of CD16 shedding can lead to impaired NK cell detachment and decreased serial killing. Thus, these data corroborate that some level of shedding of CD16A is an integral part of the disassembly of the immune synapse that is important for repeated killing by individual NK cells [ 9 ,  17 ].
Overall, our results are in line with previous findings, mainly based on flow cytometry or bulk cytotoxicity data, that have shown that CD16A shedding inhibition boosts cytokine production by NK cells but generally show small differences in the fraction of degranulating NK cells [ 6 ,  17 ,  38 ]. Hyperproduction of cytokines has been observed in situations where NK cell detachment is impaired, leading to abnormally long contacts with target cells [ 36 ,  37 ] and even connected to severe pathological effects. In this study, increased NK cell death was observed as another consequence of shedding inhibition with Batimastat. Batimastat‐induced NK cell death was also observed in cocultures with HL‐60 in the absence of acimtamig. This indicates that, in addition to CD16A, other surface proteins involved in the immune synapse are clipped off by the metalloprotease during detachment, giving further support that receptor shedding is an integral process during termination of cell–cell contacts. Thus, although inhibition of shedding might be effective in the short term, its complete abolishment can impact sustained targeting of distant tumor cells and, by this, limit the overall antitumoral activity.
Patients with functional ADAM17 deficiency have suffered from recurrent bacterial infections and hyperinflammation, which have been associated with premature death [ 39 ,  40 ]. Thus, it is unclear if ADAM17 inhibition may have any clinical benefits. To our knowledge, clinical translation of ADAM17 inhibitors in the context of engaging CD16A has not yet shown a pronounced clinical benefit. Bachanova et al. [ 41 ] reported a clinical study where the ADAM17 inhibitor INCB7839 was utilized in combination with Rituximab as a posttransplant consolidation strategy in patients with diffuse large B‐cell lymphoma. This study showed a difference in soluble CD16, a potential impact measurement of the ADAM17 inhibitor, and demonstrated a 97% overall survival in the test group, compared with 95% with Rituximab treatment alone in a similar cohort, over 1 year. Furthermore, the introduction of cleavage‐resistant CD16A through genetic modification of iPSC‐derived NK cells is currently being pursued by various investigators with no evaluable and comparative clinical data reported yet. Since systemic administration of ADAM17 inhibitors requires management of side effects [ 42 ], which may also be true for iPSC‐derived NK cells expressing cleavage‐resistant CD16A, risk‐benefit analyses in further prospective clinical trials are warranted.
In summary, our data show that ADCC induced by acimtamig leads to efficient NK‐cell‐mediated killing of CD30‐expressing tumor target cells with an important contribution of serial killing. This serial killing and overall NK‐cell survival are facilitated by maintaining the ability of NK cells to efficiently detach from target cells. Thus, despite a potential decrease in the recognition of target cells after sequential killing, overall and long‐term responses by NK cells may be augmented by naturally occurring shedding of CD16A.
Materials and Methods
Cell Culture
Human NK cells were isolated from peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from anonymous healthy donors provided by the Karolinska University Hospital, Sweden, or the DRK‐Blutspendedienst Baden‐Württemberg‐Hessen, Mannheim, Germany. PBMCs were isolated using Ficoll gradient, and aliquots were frozen at −80°C containing 100 million PBMCs or incubated overnight. NK cells were enriched from frozen or fresh PBMC preparations using negative selection kits (Cat. No. 130‐092‐657, Miltenyi Biotec or Cat. No. 19055, STEMCELL Technologies) according to the manufacturer's instructions. NK cells were maintained in DMEM cell culture media (Sigma‐Aldrich) supplemented with 30% Ham's F12, 10% human serum (Sigma‐Aldrich), 50 U/mL penicillin–streptomycin, 2 mM L‐glutamine, 1× nonessential amino acids, 1 mM sodium pyruvate (all from Sigma‐Aldrich), and 100 U/mL IL‐2 (Peprotech) stimulation for 2 days. Based on analysis from three independent donors, 93.4 ± 4.6% of the NK cells expressed CD16. The leukemia cell lines Karpas‐299 (Cat. No. 06072604‐1VL, Sigma‐Aldrich), Nalm‐6 cells (Cat. No ACC128, DSMZ), leukemia cell line HL‐60 (Cat. No ACC3, DSMZ), and human glioblastoma DK‐MG cells (Cat. No ACC277, DSMZ) were cultured in RPMI 1640 media supplemented with 10% FBS, and 50 U/mL penicillin–streptomycin (all from Sigma‐Aldrich). NK92 cell lines were generated from NK92 WT (Cat. No. CRL‐2407, ATCC) as described in the supporting information and cultured in stem cell growth medium (SCGM, Cat. No. 20802‐0500, CellGenix) supplemented with 20% FBS, which was enriched with 300 U/mL IL‐2 every second day.
Generation of NK92 Cell Lines
To generate NK92 cells with high‐affinity (HA) or high‐affinity noncleavable (HA NC) CD16, the LeGO‐G2 plasmid (Cat.No.25917 Addgene) was modified by adding a multiple cloning site (MCS) from pcDNA3 (Cat. No.13032 Addgene), a T2A sequence, and a linker (amino acids GSGSGSG) in frame with downstream eGFP. The CD16A sequence was retrieved from Uniprot ( P08637 ), and Phenylalanine 158 was replaced with Valine (HA) and Serine 197 with Phenylalanine (NC) [ 43 ]. These sequences were codon‐optimized using GenSmart (Genscript), and the synthesized sequences were cloned into the MCS of the LeGO‐G2 plasmid using BamHI and XbaI restriction sites. NK92 Mock contained the empty vector. All plasmids were verified by sequencing (Eurofins). Lentivirus was produced as previously described [ 44 ]. Briefly, 1 × 10 6  HEK293FT cells (Cat. No. CRL‐3216, ATCC) were plated into a poly‐D‐lysine‐coated 150 mm dish (Corning). On the following day, the cells were transfected with 30 µg of the cloned transfer plasmids encoding CD16‐HA/NC, 15 µg of pMDLg/pRRE (Cat. No.12251, Addgene), 10 µg of pRSV‐REV (Cat. No.12553, Addgene), and 5 µg of phCMV‐VSV‐G (Cat. No. 8454, Addgene) using a calcium phosphate transfection kit (Sigma‐Aldrich) in the presence of 25 µM chloroquine (Sigma‐Aldrich). The medium was changed 16 h posttransfection, and the virus particles were collected after an additional 28 h, by filtering the supernatant through a 0.45 µm filter and then stored at −80°C. Thirty thousand NK92 cells were suspended in 150 µL SCGM medium supplemented with 20% FBS in flat‐bottom 96‐well plates. Cells were transduced at a multiplicity of infection of 8, for 6 h in 8 µg/mL protamine sulphate (Sigma Aldrich) and 7 µM 5Z‐7‐Oxozeaenol (Bio‐Techne Tocris). After transduction, cells were cultured for 4 days and then stained using BV421‐conjugated anti‐human CD16A antibody (BioLegend) to confirm the expression level.

Incucyte Assay

No. 14190‐094, Gibco) for 20 min at a cell density of 4 × 10 5 /mL. All cells and reagents were prepared in MEM alpha (Cat. No. 22571‐020, Gibco) assay medium at 200 µL/well final volume, followed by incubation in the Incucyte instrument at 37°C, 5% CO 2 . Image acquisition was performed every hour for up to 8 h using the “non‐adherent cell‐by‐cell” module with the following parameters: 20× objective, 4 images/well, channels: phase contrast, red/orange, and green channel, acquisition time: green channel 300 ms; red/orange channel 400 ms. Results were normalized to the total number of Cytolight red/orange single‐positive object counts in the corresponding well. Tumor cell killing in the absence of antibodies was used as controls.

Design and Fabrication of Microwell Chip and Holder
Three different types of microwell chips that had different microwell dimensions were used. Two of the chip types had 300 µm deep, hexagonally shaped microwells that were either 60 or 80 µm in width [ 46 ]. The microwells were distributed in four separate honeycomb arrays, each made of 36 subarrays. The footprint of such a subarray matched the imaging field‐of‐view when using a 10× magnification lens on a 13 × 13 mm 2  sensor in the microscope. The chip with 60 µm wells included 224 wells in each subarray and 32,256 wells in total on the chip, and the chip with 80 µm wells included 143 wells in each subarray and 20,592 wells in total on the chip. These two types of chips were assembled in a chip holder that had the shape and dimensions of a standard culture dish to fit in a conventional microscope stage insert. The holder included a polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) gasket and a top part that included four open liquid media chambers. When the holder and the chip were assembled, the media chambers aligned with the four main well arrays on the chip. The third type of chip had 300 µm deep and 350 µm wide microwells distributed in a layout with 16 arrays, a design and fabrication that is described in earlier work [ 47 ]. All three types of microwell chips were fabricated using similar processes. In short, lithography and deep reactive ion etching were applied to form micro‐holes in Si that were closed by anodic bonding to a bottom glass substrate to form glass‐Si microwells. The glass bottom in the microwells had a thickness of 170 µm, corresponding to a #1.5H coverslip glass, for high optical quality and microscopy. The chip with 60 µm wells was used for assays with suspension cells, and the chips with the larger 80 and 350 µm wells were used with adherent cells.

Image Analysis
Microwell cytotoxicity screening data was analyzed automatically to enumerate the number of live/dead cells in each well at all time points. Image processing and cell quantifications were performed by a custom‐built MATLAB script previously described [ 29 ], now updated with adaptions to allow for identification of noncircular cells and effector cell death, as well as strategies to compensate for fluorescence bleed‐over and processes to compensate for fluorescence intensity variations over time. Briefly, images were first processed to identify the microwells using the transilluminated channel. Background subtraction was applied to the fluorescent channels by thresholding and remaining objects were smoothed and filtered to remove small objects. A watershed‐based algorithm (part of the MATLAB Imaging Toolbox) was then applied for segmentation of connected components to allow identification of individual cells. Cell identification was based on cell permanent (live) stain and the subsequent classification of live/dead was performed by manual gating on the live and dead fluorescence intensities measured within objects. All identified cells were mapped within their corresponding well together with temporal information about status.
For some experiments extensive clustering made it difficult to enumerate cells, and for some target cell lines, the SYTOX green dye was not reliably detecting dead cells, therefore, data from those experiments were analyzed manually with aid of ImageJ. Manual analysis was also used for experiments involving dynamics readouts, for example, contact times.
In general, wells with one live NK cell at the beginning of the assay were selected for analysis. Wells were excluded if a second live NK cell appeared in the well within the first 2 h of the assay and/or the NK cell died before interacting with target cells. For calculating the fraction of cytotoxic NK cells, wells with one or more live target cells at the beginning of the assay were selected for analysis, and the number of wells containing dead target cells was divided by the total number of selected cells. For serial killing analysis, wells with four or more live target cells at the beginning of the assay were selected for analysis. The fraction of serial killers was calculated by dividing the number of NK cells killing at least three (for primary NK cells) or four (for NK92 cells) target cells by the total number of cytotoxic cells (NK cells killing one or more target cells) from the selected wells. NK cell cytotoxicity was compensated for background death obtained from wells without effectors. The contacts between the effector and each target cell were scored, and the fraction of time in conjugation was calculated as the total time an NK cell spent in conjugation with one or multiple target cells, divided by the total time of observation. In cases when the NK cell died during the assay, the total observation time was up until that point. For the contact analysis, 40 primary NK cells per condition per experiment were analyzed, while for the NK92 experiments the corresponding number was 30 cells.
Statistical Analysis
Data and statistical analysis were performed using Prism (GraphPad, La Jolla). To compare two groups with matching donors, a paired  t ‐test was conducted. To compare four groups and multiple matching donors, one‐way ANOVA was performed using the “Repeated Measures ANOVA Calculator” to validate the method's parameters [ 48 ]. If residuals followed a normal distribution and sphericity was not violated, sphericity was assumed and the Geisser–Greenhouse correction applied. In both cases, the Tukey test was used for multiple comparisons. When residuals did not follow a normal distribution, a nonparametric test (Friedman test) with Dunn's test for multiple comparisons was used instead. Only significant values were shown, and they were indicated with * p  ≤ 0.05, ** p  ≤ 0.01, *** p  ≤ 0.001, and **** p  ≤ 0.0001.
