Informationally Decoupled Trajectory Design for Sim-to-Real System Identification







Sangwoo Shin

, Ashvin Anilkumar, Ryan Gao, Josiah P. Hanna

Abstract

    

Sampling-based system identification estimates physically meaningful parameters by tuning a simulator to reproduce the target system dynamics, providing an interpretable approach to improving sim-to-real transfer.
Yet when the collected trajectories do not distinguish the effects of different parameters, multiple parameter combinations can reproduce those trajectories, leading to unreliable parameter estimates.
To address this challenge, we introduce an Informationally Decoupled Trajectory Design framework (
IDTD
),
which formulates the objective for the exploration policy built on the Schur complement score derived from the Fisher information matrix.
To faithfully reflect parameter separability in the exploration objective, 
IDTD
 normalizes the score against the per-parameter information,
selects the most favorable trajectory segment for each parameter, and aggregates the resulting scores logarithmically.
The optimized trajectory is therefore composed of complementary intervals, each exposing a distinct subset of parameters whose contribution to the motion over that interval can be attributed unambiguously.
Across diverse simulation environments, ranging from a linear-dynamics system to the Go2 quadruped, G1 humanoid, and Crazyflie quadrotor, 
IDTD
 reduces the parameter identification error by 
39.6
%
39.6\%
 on average relative to the strongest prior active-exploration baseline and attains improved downstream policy transfer.
Furthermore, we validate the proposed trajectory design on a real K1 humanoid, demonstrating that the resulting identified parameters accurately capture the real-system dynamics.

  





I 
Introduction





Reinforcement learning (RL) in massively parallel simulation has become
a widely adopted approach
for learning-based robot control, yet policies trained in simulation degrade on hardware whenever the simulated dynamics deviate from the target physical system 
[
1
, 
2
]
.
A common remedy is domain randomization, which trains a policy over a distribution of physical parameters, such as inertial properties, actuator gains, and friction, so that the real system falls within its support 
[
3
, 
4
]
.
This approach, however, requires covering a wide range of parameters, which tends to produce conservative policies that sacrifice performance for robustness.






System identification (SysID) offers an alternative approach.
Rather than training against a broad range of possible dynamics, SysID explicitly estimates the physical parameters that make the simulator reproduce the target system dynamics.
Within SysID, sampling-based methods have recently gained traction 
[
5
, 
6
]
, as massively parallel GPU simulation makes black-box search over physical parameters practical.
These methods evaluate candidate parameter vectors through forward dynamics simulation and search for the parameters under which the simulated trajectories best match the recorded real-world trajectories.
By avoiding differentiation through the simulator, sampling-based SysID can directly leverage modern physics simulators with contact and actuator dynamics 
[
7
, 
8
, 
9
, 
10
]
 while retaining physically interpretable parameters.






While sampling-based approaches make the parameter search practical, their success depends critically on solving the complementary problem of determining 
which trajectories should be collected from the target system for reliable identification.

Prior work addresses this question by seeking informative trajectories, where informativeness is quantified through the Fisher Information Matrix (FIM), whose inverse lower-bounds the covariance of the parameter estimate 
[
11
]
.
Based on this bound, active exploration methods search for a policy that collects trajectories minimizing the trace of the inverse FIM, aggregating the estimation uncertainty across all parameters into a single objective 
[
6
, 
5
]
.
However, this criterion does not treat the amount of information and the separability of the parameters as distinct quantities, so increased excitation can improve the aggregate criterion even when some parameter effects become less distinguishable.
Consequently, errors in different parameters can compensate for one another, allowing simulations using the identified parameters to closely match the real-world trajectories even when the individual parameters differ substantially from their true values.
For instance, consider identifying the mass 
m

c

A trace-based excitation makes the mass move faster and more abruptly, since larger, more energetic motion carries more total information about the parameters.
Under such motion, however, the sensitivities to 
m

c

The criterion therefore keeps improving even though the two parameters remain difficult to tell apart.
In this simple example, the excitation can be manually designed to alternate between acceleration-dominant (

Extending such parameter-specific excitation to the dozens of coupled parameters in modern robot models, however, is highly challenging, as each parameter may require a different excitation regime.






To address this challenge, we present 
I

D

T
rajectory 
D
esign (
IDTD
), which searches for motions under which every parameter leaves a unique effect on the trajectory that no other parameter can reproduce.
Specifically,

IDTD
 builds on a per-parameter score derived from the Schur complement of the Fisher information, which measures how much of a parameter’s effect on the motion remains after the effects of all other parameters are accounted for.
A parameter whose effect can be reproduced by the others scores near zero, while a parameter with a distinct effect scores high; a high score thus means the parameter is cleanly distinguishable, or decoupled, from the rest.
Furthermore, to prevent the objective from being improved through excitation alone, for instance by moving a few responsive joints more aggressively without better separating their parameter effects, 
IDTD
 normalizes each score by the information carried by the corresponding parameter.
It then evaluates the normalized scores across multiple trajectory segments to encourage complementary excitation of different parameters throughout the trajectory.
As a result, 
IDTD
 designs trajectories that expose every parameter somewhere along the motion rather than accumulating information for only a small subset.






We evaluate 
IDTD
 in simulation on a linear-dynamics system, Unitree Go2, Unitree G1, and Crazyflie, demonstrating improved parameter identification, target-system prediction, and downstream policy transfer.
We further validate 
IDTD
 on a real Booster K1 humanoid, where the identified parameters more accurately capture the target-system dynamics.






The main contributions of our work are as follows.

(i)
 We address the challenging yet practical problem of designing exploration trajectories that distinguish the effects of physical parameters, enabling reliable sampling-based system identification.

(ii)
 We propose 
IDTD
, a trajectory design framework that explicitly balances information quantity with parameter distinguishability through a normalized Schur-complement score and parameter-wise trajectory coverage.

(iii)
 We demonstrate that 
IDTD
 improves parameter identification accuracy and downstream policy transfer across simulated Unitree Go2, Unitree G1, and Crazyflie platforms, and better captures real-world dynamics on a Booster K1 humanoid.








II 
Related Works





II-A
 
Sim-to-Real Transfer and System Identification





Reducing the sim-to-real gap has been approached from two complementary directions.
Domain randomization exposes the policy to a wide distribution of dynamics so that the real system falls within the training support 
[
3
, 
4
]
; the resulting robustness, however, comes at the cost of conservative behavior, and the randomization ranges themselves require tuning.






System identification instead estimates the physical parameters of the specific robot and its environment from real-world data.
Classical approaches exploit the linearity of rigid-body dynamics in the inertial parameters and estimate them by least squares over hand-designed excitation 
[
12
, 
13
]
, but they require an explicit structured dynamics model and do not scale to the contact-rich, hard-to-model effects of real robots.






As massively parallel simulation has become fast and reliable, system identification is increasingly performed by tuning simulator parameters until simulated trajectories match real trajectories.
One line of work makes the forward dynamics differentiable with respect to the parameters, so the identification objective can be minimized by backpropagating gradients directly through the simulation 
[
14
, 
15
]
. Its reliance on smoothed contact models, however, limits its scalability to contact-rich tasks.
Recent work therefore favors sampling-based identification, which pairs the simulator with a black-box search algorithm and sidesteps differentiation while keeping the estimated parameters physically interpretable 
[
5
, 
6
]
.






Our work follows the sampling-based paradigm but addresses the complementary question of how exploration should be designed so that every parameter leaves a distinct effect on the resulting trajectory, enabling reliable system identification.








II-B
 
Active Exploration and Optimal Experimental Design





The choice of excitation has long been recognized as central to identification quality.
In the classical context, exciting trajectories are optimized offline against criteria defined on the regressor matrix, which linearly relates the unknown dynamic parameters to the measured forces for a given trajectory.
A common objective is to minimize the condition number of the regressor, producing excitations that yield parameter estimates more robust to measurement noise 
[
16
, 
13
]
.
The objective is inherently coupling-aware, since an ill-conditioned regressor indicates that different parameters influence the measurements along nearly identical directions.

In the transition from regressor-based trajectory design to sampling-based identification, the Fisher information has been carried over as the measure of a trajectory,

Real-world Experiments





Setup.

We deploy 
IDTD
 on a real Booster K1 humanoid to validate its effectiveness for real-world system identification.
Our hardware setup uses onboard proprioceptive sensing from joint encoders and an IMU, without external motion capture.
We therefore focus on actuator- and joint-level parameters rather than floating-base inertial properties such as the CoM and body inertia.
Specifically, we identify a 60-dimensional parameter space consisting of actuator torque-lag time constants (8D), which capture first-order torque-response delay; velocity-dependent actuator gains (8D), which model transmission losses during joint motion; joint-friction coefficients (22D); and joint armatures (22D), which represent reflected rotor inertia.
For the actuator parameters, we group corresponding left- and right-side joints by motor class and limb segment, resulting in eight groups for both torque lag and dynamic gain.
We use the Newton physics simulator 
[
9
]
 for forward simulation and parameter identification.
The state components used in the identification objective in Eq. (
1
) are the base orientation and angular velocity from the onboard IMU and the joint positions and velocities from the encoders.

VI 
Conclusion





We presented 
IDTD
, a trajectory design framework for reliable sampling-based system identification that explicitly accounts for parameter separability during data collection.

IDTD
 combines a normalized Schur-complement score with segment-wise coverage and logarithmic aggregation to balance parameter separability and information quantity.
Across simulated Go2, G1, and Crazyflie platforms, 
IDTD
 improved identification accuracy, dynamics prediction, and parameter decoupling, while on the Booster K1 humanoid the identified parameters predicted unseen real trajectories more accurately.






Limitations and future work.

While 
IDTD
 improves forward-dynamics prediction on the real K1, downstream control improvements remain limited, possibly due to the restricted parameter space identifiable from onboard sensing.
Future work could use more accurate state estimation, such as external motion capture, to identify a broader parameter set and improve downstream transfer.
