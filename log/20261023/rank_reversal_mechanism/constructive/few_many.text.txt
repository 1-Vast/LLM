Abstract
    
Real-world systems often serve many objectives but can deploy only a few options, whose performance must be learned from noisy feedback. Modeling each option as an arm in a multi-objective bandit, we introduce few-for-many best-arm identification, which seeks a small set of arms minimizing the worst weighted degradation from the objective-wise optima, rather than the best arm for every objective. We consider both known objective groups and latent groups specified only by a representative budget, and propose GFM-Elim and AFM-Elim with instance-dependent sample-complexity guarantees. For Gaussian rewards, we further derive a lower bound for the latent-group setting and show that it is asymptotically attainable with high probability by an oracle-based Track-and-Stop procedure. Our results show that sharing observations across objectives and ignoring irrelevant objective-wise distinctions can substantially reduce the sampling cost, as confirmed by experiments on synthetic and real-world tasks.
  


1 Introduction


Modern decision systems frequently need to serve many evaluation criteria or user groups with only a handful of deployable choices. This motivates the few-for-many paradigm, which selects a small set of complementary solutions that jointly cover many objectives (Liu et al., 2024; Lin et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025; Shang et al., 2026). For example, clinical trials may seek a few treatment options that collectively perform well across multiple efficacy and safety outcomes (Kavelaars et al., 2020; Villar et al., 2015); online advertising platforms may use a few campaigns to reach heterogeneous target audiences (Geng et al., 2020; Schwartz et al., 2017); and LLM services may route diverse queries among a limited pool of models with different capabilities and inference costs (Chen et al., 2024; Ong et al., 2025).


A key challenge in these applications is that the multi-objective performance of each candidate is unknown and must be learned from noisy observations, such as patient outcomes, user responses to advertisements, or repeated model evaluations. Few-for-many selection thus becomes a statistical identification problem: the learner must find a small representative set whose members jointly serve many objectives, while simultaneously learning their unknown performance. Multi-objective multi-armed bandits offer a natural framework for this setting, associating each arm with an unknown vector-valued mean reward (Drugan and Nowe, 2013; Lattimore and Szepesvári, 2020). In the pure-exploration regime, the goal is to identify the desired representative set with probability at least 1−δ1-\delta using as few arm pulls as possible (Audibert and Bubeck, 2010; Garivier and Kaufmann, 2016).


Existing multi-objective formulations typically lie at one of two extremes. At one end, Pareto-front identification (Auer et al., 2016; Ararat and Tekin, 2023; Crepon et al., 2024) seeks all nondominated arms; as the number of objectives grows, however, the Pareto set can become large, increasing both the statistical cost of recovering it and the burden of choosing among its members. At the other end, scalarization-based methods (Paria et al., 2020; Lin et al., 2024) collapse multiple objectives into a scalar utility and return a single solution for a given preference, which can be overly restrictive when different objectives favor different arms. These limitations call for an intermediate goal: identifying a few complementary arms that jointly serve many objectives.


The closest bandit formulation, multi-objective best-arm identification (MO-BAI), seeks the best arm for every objective (Chen et al., 2025). This target is appropriate when a separate decision can be deployed for each objective, but it can be unnecessarily costly when many objectives must be served under a limited deployment budget. The few-for-many principle instead selects a prescribed number of complementary solutions such that every objective is well served by at least one of them (Lin et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025). However, existing studies apply this principle to deterministic many-objective optimization, whereas in the bandit setting both the objective-wise ideal values and the quality of any candidate representative set must be inferred from noisy observations.


To bridge this gap, we formulate a stochastic counterpart of few-for-many optimization, which we call few-for-many best-arm identification. The quality of a representative set is measured by its worst weighted degradation from the objective-wise ideal values. We consider two forms of prior structure. In the known-group setting, the objectives are partitioned in advance and one arm is selected for each group. In the latent-group setting, only a representative budget LL is specified, and the selected arms implicitly induce groups according to which representative best serves each objective. In the latter setting, L=1L=1 recovers a single robust representative, whereas a sufficiently large LL recovers objective-wise identification; the intermediate regime 1<L≪M1<L\ll M captures the practically relevant case in which a few deployable arms must jointly serve many objectives. Our main contributions are as follows:

• 

We introduce a few-for-many bandit framework that covers both known and latent objective groups. It bridges MO-BAI (Chen et al., 2025) and few-for-many optimization (Lin et al., 2025), accommodating both noisy observations and a limited representative budget.

• 

We develop two phase-based elimination algorithms, GFM-Elim for known objective groups and AFM-Elim for latent groups, and establish finite-time sample-complexity guarantees for both. Unlike MO-BAI (Chen et al., 2025), our algorithms need not resolve objective-wise distinctions that do not affect the optimal representatives, which can substantially reduce the sampling cost in many-objective settings.

• 

For Gaussian rewards, we establish an information-theoretic lower bound for the latent-group setting that characterizes its fundamental instance-dependent sample complexity. We further show in Appendix C that this lower bound is asymptotically attainable with high probability by an oracle-based Track-and-Stop procedure.

• 

We evaluate the proposed algorithms on synthetic and real-world tasks. The results show that GFM-Elim and AFM-Elim reliably identify compact representative sets while requiring fewer samples than existing multi-objective identification baselines.




Related Work.


Best-arm identification (BAI) aims to identify the optimal decision using as few samples as possible. Garivier and Kaufmann (2016) established the instance-dependent asymptotic lower bound for classical BAI and introduced Track-and-Stop, which has since been extended to structured pure-exploration problems, including combinatorial answer spaces and multiple correct answers (Chen et al., 2014; Degenne and Koolen, 2019; Jourdan et al., 2021). For vector-valued rewards, multi-objective bandits have been studied under Pareto-based and preference-based criteria (Drugan and Nowe, 2013; Auer et al., 2016; Shukla and Basu, 2024; Xue et al., 2026c; Xue et al., 2026a; Xue et al., 2026b). In particular, Chen et al. (2025) identify the best arm for every objective and develop asymptotically optimal sampling strategies. In parallel, few-for-many optimization seeks a small set of complementary solutions that jointly serve many objectives, as exemplified by the many-objective cover problem (Liu et al., 2024), Tchebycheff set scalarization (Lin et al., 2025), and the general F4M framework (Liu et al., 2025; Shang et al., 2026). These works mainly consider deterministic optimization, where objective evaluations or gradients are directly accessible. Our work connects these two lines of research by studying few-for-many identification with unknown vector-valued means, where only the distinctions that can affect the optimal representatives need to be resolved from noisy sequential observations. A more detailed discussion is provided in Appendix A.

Few-for-Many Identification.


When the number of objectives is large, identifying a separate best arm for every objective may yield many arms, whereas requiring a single arm to perform well on all objectives can be overly restrictive. Motivated by the few-for-many paradigm (Lin et al., 2025), we seek a small number of representative arms that together perform well across many objectives, with each representative serving a group of objectives. We consider two complementary settings, depending on whether the objective groups are known in advance.



Known Objective Groups.


In the known-group setting, objectives are partitioned in advance, and all objectives in a group share one representative arm. Let 1≤Q≪M1\leq Q\ll M and let 𝒢={G1,…,GQ}\mathcal{G}=\{G_{1},\ldots,G_{Q}\} be a partition of [M][M], i.e., [M]=∪q=1QGq[M]=\cup_{q=1}^{Q}G_{q}, where GqG_{q} contains the objectives in the qq-th group. Let λm>0\lambda_{m}>0 be a known weight for objective m∈[M]m\in[M], which reflects its relative importance.


For every group q∈[Q]q\in[Q] and arm a∈[K]a\in[K], we define the group coverage loss as




Rq​(a)=maxm∈Gq⁡λm​(μm⋆−μa,m).R_{q}(a)=\max_{m\in G_{q}}\lambda_{m}\left(\mu_{m}^{\star}-\mu_{a,m}\right).


Thus, Rq​(a)R_{q}(a) measures the largest weighted degradation incurred when arm aa represents all objectives in GqG_{q}. Accordingly, the identification target is




𝒂𝒢⋆=(a1⋆,…,aQ⋆),aq⋆=arg⁡mina∈[K]​Rq​(a).\bm{a}_{\mathcal{G}}^{\star}=(a_{1}^{\star},\ldots,a_{Q}^{\star}),\qquad a_{q}^{\star}=\arg\min_{a\in[K]}R_{q}(a).


When Q=1Q=1, the problem reduces to identifying a single robust representative for all objectives. When Q=MQ=M and Gq={q}G_{q}=\{q\}, it reduces to identifying the best arm for every objective.



Latent Objective Groups.


In the latent-group setting, the objective partition is not known in advance. Instead, the learner is given a representative budget 1≤L≪M1\leq L\ll M and seeks LL arms that jointly provide good coverage of all objectives, with the objective groups implicitly induced by the selected representatives.


For any nonempty arm set S⊆[K]S\subseteq[K], we define its overall coverage loss as




Φ⁡(S)=maxm∈[M]⁡λm​(μm⋆−Vm​(S)),Vm​(S)=maxa∈S⁡μa,m.\Phi(S)=\max_{m\in[M]}\lambda_{m}\left(\mu_{m}^{\star}-V_{m}(S)\right),\qquad V_{m}(S)=\max_{a\in S}\mu_{a,m}.


Hence, Φ⁡(S)\Phi(S) measures the largest weighted degradation incurred when each objective is served by its best arm in SS rather than by its best arm in [K][K]. Accordingly, the identification target is




SL⋆=arg⁡minS∈𝒮L⁡Φ⁡(S),𝒮L={S⊆[K]:|S|=L}.S_{L}^{\star}=\arg\min_{S\in\mathcal{S}_{L}}\Phi(S),\qquad\mathcal{S}_{L}=\left\{S\subseteq[K]:|S|=L\right\}.


The optimal set SL⋆S_{L}^{\star} naturally induces an assignment of objectives to representatives. Specifically, let πL⋆​(m)=arg⁡maxa∈SL⋆⁡μa,m\pi_{L}^{\star}(m)=\arg\max_{a\in S_{L}^{\star}}\mu_{a,m}. The objectives assigned to the same representative arm then form a latent group:




Ga⋆={m∈[M]:πL⋆​(m)=a},a∈SL⋆.G_{a}^{\star}=\left\{m\in[M]:\pi_{L}^{\star}(m)=a\right\},\quad a\in S_{L}^{\star}.


Thus, the objective partition is induced directly by SL⋆S_{L}^{\star} rather than identified separately. When L=1L=1, the problem reduces to identifying a single robust representative for all objectives, which coincides with the known-group setting with Q=1Q=1. At the other extreme, once L≥|{bm⋆:m∈[M]}|L\geq|\{b_{m}^{\star}:m\in[M]\}| (in particular, whenever L≥ML\geq M), the representative set can contain all objective-wise best arms and attains zero coverage loss.




3 Finite-Time Elimination Algorithms


This section presents finite-time elimination algorithms for the two few-for-many settings: known objective groups and latent objective groups.


3.1 Grouped Few-for-Many Elimination


We begin with the known-group setting. Although the objective groups are given, the group coverage losses cannot be evaluated directly because the mean rewards are unknown. To address this, we propose GFM-Elim, a phase-based elimination algorithm that jointly learns the mean rewards and the representative of each group. The complete procedure is summarized in Algorithm 1.

3.2 Adaptive Few-for-Many Elimination


We next turn to the latent-group setting, where no objective partition is available and the learner must identify the optimal representative set directly. We propose AFM-Elim, which lifts the elimination principle of GFM-Elim from individual arms to candidate sets. Importantly, although elimination is performed at the set level, sampling remains at the base-arm level, so that observations are shared across candidate sets. The complete procedure is summarized in Algorithm 2.


At phase rr, AFM-Elim maintains a benchmark candidate set ℬm,r\mathcal{B}_{m,r} for each objective m∈[M]m\in[M] and a family 𝒞r\mathcal{C}_{r} of candidate representative sets, initialized as ℬm,1=[K]\mathcal{B}_{m,1}=[K] and 𝒞1=𝒮L\mathcal{C}_{1}=\mathcal{S}_{L}, respectively. The algorithm samples only the base arms that either belong to an active candidate set or remain relevant for estimating an objective-wise benchmark, i.e.,
𝒰r=(∪S∈𝒞rS)∪(∪m=1Mℬm,r).\mathcal{U}_{r}=\left(\cup_{S\in\mathcal{C}_{r}}S\right)\cup\left(\cup_{m=1}^{M}\mathcal{B}_{m,r}\right).
Thus, a single pull of an arm simultaneously refines the estimates of all active candidate sets containing that arm, as well as all objectives for which it remains a benchmark candidate.

Exact latent-set identification in AFM-Elim requires optimizing over the combinatorial family 𝒮L={S⊆[K]:|S|=L}\mathcal{S}_{L}=\{S\subseteq[K]:|S|=L\}, which is computationally intractable in general. Specifically, with probability at least 1−δ1-\delta, the relaxed procedure returns a set S^\widehat{S} satisfying

The key to computational efficiency is that, at any fixed loss threshold, feasibility reduces to a set-cover problem over the objectives. Here, a loss threshold specifies the largest weighted degradation from the objective-wise ideal value that we are willing to tolerate on each objective, and an arm covers exactly those objectives whose weighted degradation does not exceed the threshold. Building on this observation, we propose BiAFM-Greedy, which first estimates the reward matrix by uniform sampling, then scans candidate thresholds in increasing order and applies the greedy set-cover rule at each one. It returns the first cover that satisfies the relaxed budget, thereby avoiding enumeration of all size-LL candidate sets. Compared with AFM-Elim, this procedure forgoes exact size-LL identification and instance-adaptive sampling, but in return offers a polynomial-time bicriteria guarantee: with high probability, its coverage loss is within ϵ\epsilon of the optimal size-LL loss while using at most O⁡(L​log⁡M)O(L\log M) arms. The formal procedure and its theoretical guarantee are given in Appendix B.

We evaluate the proposed methods on the Adult dataset (Kohavi, 1996). We construct 12 arms by combining three classifiers (logistic regression, random forest, and histogram gradient boosting) and four decision thresholds in {0.35,0.45,0.55,0.65}\{0.35,0.45,0.55,0.65\}. Real-world results.


As shown in Figure 2(c), all methods identify the optimal two-model portfolio in every trial, but their sampling costs differ substantially. Uniform-AFM samples every model throughout the elimination process and thus cannot exploit the fact that several classifiers are quickly revealed to be irrelevant to the best size-two portfolio. AFM-Elim eliminates such arms and candidate sets earlier, reducing the cost by roughly a factor of two. The MO-BAI-based baseline is even more expensive, because its first stage attempts to identify the best model for every subgroup before solving the portfolio problem. On Adult, several subgroup accuracies are close across neighboring thresholds, so resolving every subgroup-wise winner requires many additional samples. The few-for-many objective, in contrast, only needs to determine which pair jointly covers the subgroups well, and can therefore stop without resolving all objective-wise winners. Consequently, AFM-Elim uses only about one third of the pulls of MOBAI+AFM. Overall, the real-world experiment supports the same conclusion as the synthetic study: directly targeting the deployable representative set can be much cheaper than solving the stronger objective-wise identification problem.

6 Conclusion and Future Work


We formulated few-for-many best-arm identification as a fixed-confidence problem whose goal is to find a small set of arms such that every objective is well served by at least one selected arm. We considered two settings: known objective groups, where one representative is identified for each group, and latent objective groups, where a budgeted representative set is selected without a predefined partition. For these settings, we developed GFM-Elim and AFM-Elim, which exploit vector-valued feedback and eliminate arms as soon as they become irrelevant to the final decision. We established instance-dependent sample-complexity guarantees for both algorithms and an information-theoretic lower bound for the latent-group setting, and further showed that this lower bound is asymptotically attainable with high probability by an oracle-based Track-and-Stop procedure. Our experiments demonstrate that directly targeting the few-for-many solution can substantially reduce the sample complexity by sharing observations and avoiding unnecessary objective-wise distinctions.


An important direction for future work is to better reconcile statistical efficiency with computational scalability in the latent-group setting, where exact identification involves combinatorial optimization. It would also be of interest to extend the framework beyond the current setting, for example, to adaptive selection of the representative budget and to partial feedback.

Appendix A Extended Related Work


In this section, we review three lines of research most relevant to our work: fixed-confidence best-arm identification, multi-objective bandits, and few-for-many optimization.


Fixed-confidence best-arm identification.


Fixed-confidence best-arm identification (BAI) seeks to identify the optimal arm with a prescribed confidence level while minimizing the number of samples (Audibert and Bubeck, 2010; Gabillon et al., 2012). A major line of work studies its instance-dependent sample complexity and develops sampling strategies that attain the corresponding information-theoretic limits. In the classical setting, Garivier and Kaufmann (2016) established an asymptotically tight lower bound and proposed Track-and-Stop, which adaptively tracks the optimal sampling proportions. This framework has since been extended to more structured pure-exploration problems, including combinatorial identification (Chen et al., 2014; Chen et al., 2016; Jourdan et al., 2021), identification with multiple correct answers (Degenne and Koolen, 2019), and linear and transductive bandits (Fiez et al., 2019; Degenne et al., 2020; Jedra and Proutiere, 2020). These studies show that the structure of the answer space and of the feedback model plays a central role in determining both the optimal sampling allocation and the resulting sample complexity.



Multi-objective bandits.


Multi-objective bandits extend classical bandit models to vector-valued rewards and have been studied extensively in both online decision making and pure exploration (Drugan and Nowe, 2013). In the online setting, one line of work targets Pareto-efficient decisions without imposing an explicit scalar preference, including contextual Pareto regret (Turgay et al., 2018), Pareto regret under stochastic and adversarial feedback (Xu and Klabjan, 2023), and Pareto-regret minimization in linear contextual bandits (Park et al., 2025). Another line incorporates user preferences through scalarizations or structured orderings. Representative examples include nonlinear scalarizations for exploring the Pareto front with hypervolume guarantees (Zhang, 2024), as well as lexicographic and Pareto–lexicographic bandits that optimize objectives according to prescribed priority structures (Xue et al., 2024; Cheng et al., 2024). Recent extensions study cost-aware multi-objective evaluation (Xue et al., 2026c), lexicographic generalized low-rank matrix bandits (Xue et al., 2026a), safe multi-objective linear bandits under hierarchical preferences (Xue et al., 2026b), and multi-objective reinforcement learning with bandit feedback (Qiu et al., 2024).


In pure exploration, most existing work focuses on identifying the Pareto-optimal set under fixed-budget or fixed-confidence criteria (Auer et al., 2016; Ararat and Tekin, 2023; Kone et al., 2023; Crepon et al., 2024). More recent studies incorporate preference information through ordering cones, which induce partial orders over vector-valued rewards and encode structured trade-offs among objectives (Shukla and Basu, 2024; Das et al., 2025). Most closely related to our work, Chen et al. (2025) study fixed-confidence multi-objective best-arm identification, in which the learner identifies the best arm for each objective, and characterize asymptotically optimal sampling. In contrast, few-for-many identification seeks only a small set of representative arms that collectively serve all objectives. Consequently, it need not recover the entire Pareto set or resolve every objective-wise best arm, but only those distinctions that can affect the optimal representatives.



Few-for-many optimization.


The few-for-many paradigm has emerged as an alternative to approximating a large Pareto set when only a small number of solutions can ultimately be deployed. Liu et al. (2024) formulate the many-objective cover problem (MaCP), which selects k≪Mk\ll M solutions such that each objective is well served by at least one of them, and propose a clustering-based method for the resulting NP-hard problem. Building on this idea, Lin et al. (2025) introduce Tchebycheff set scalarization, which evaluates a set through its worst objective-wise performance, together with a smooth variant amenable to gradient-based optimization. Liu et al. (2025) further develop a conditional set-optimization framework that supports different scalarizations and both continuous and discrete many-objective problems. Subsequent studies improve the computational efficiency of few-for-many optimization (Shang et al., 2026) and extend the paradigm to personalized federated learning, where a small collection of shared models is optimized to serve heterogeneous clients (Guo et al., 2026).


These works primarily address deterministic optimization, where objective evaluations or optimization feedback are directly available. In contrast, we study few-for-many identification under statistical uncertainty, where the vector-valued means of the candidate arms are unknown and must be inferred from noisy sequential observations.

It first samples all arms uniformly to estimate the unknown means, and then computes the corresponding empirical ideal values and weighted deficits. Appendix C Oracle-Based Track-and-Stop


This section presents AFM-TaS, an oracle-based, asymptotically optimal counterpart of AFM-Elim. Rather than providing a practical finite-time procedure, its purpose is to show that, under Gaussian rewards and given the required optimization oracles, the information-theoretic lower bound in Theorem 3 is asymptotically attainable with high probability. Appendix D Additional Experiments


This appendix provides additional experiments that complement the main results in Section 5. We first examine confidence calibration, scaling with the number of objectives, and sensitivity to the gaps that directly determine the few-for-many answer. We then study the computational behavior of the polynomial-time bicriteria algorithm in Appendix B. We do not include AFM-TaS, since it is an oracle-based construction that requires solving the characteristic-time allocation problem exactly and repeatedly computing GLR stopping statistics against all alternative representative sets.


D.1 Confidence Calibration


We first examine whether the empirical error rate is consistent with the prescribed confidence level. We consider one known-group instance and one latent-group instance from the synthetic setting in Section 5, fix ξ=0.1\xi=0.1, and vary δ∈{0.2,0.1,0.05,0.02}\delta\in\{0.2,0.1,0.05,0.02\}. For each δ\delta, we run 200 independent trials, using GFM-Elim on the known-group instance and AFM-Elim on the latent-group instance.


Figure 3: Confidence calibration. The left panel compares the empirical error rate with the target confidence level, and the right panel reports the
corresponding sample complexity.


As shown in Figure 3, both algorithms return the correct answer in all trials at every tested confidence level, which is consistent with the conservative finite-time confidence allocation used by the elimination procedures. The sample complexity increases only mildly as δ\delta decreases, in line with the logarithmic dependence on 1/δ1/\delta in the theoretical bounds. Hence, the algorithms remain reliable across confidence levels, and the sample budget grows gradually as the confidence requirement becomes stricter.



D.2 Scaling with the Number of Objectives


We next isolate the many-objective effect in the known-group setting. We fix Q=4Q=4 groups and increase the number of objectives per group from 22 to 1616, so that MM ranges from 88 to 6464. The optimal representative of each group remains unchanged, while every objective has its own specialist and near-specialist. Consequently, methods that first identify all objective-wise best arms must resolve a growing number of distinctions that are unnecessary for the few-for-many answer.


Figure 4: Scaling with the number of objectives in the known-group setting.


As shown in Figure 4, GFM-Elim consistently identifies the group representatives using substantially fewer pulls than the two-stage MOBAI+GFM baseline. The gap widens as MM grows, because MOBAI+GFM spends many samples resolving objective-wise specialists before solving the grouped problem. GFM-Elim also improves over Independent-GFM by sharing each vector observation across group decisions. Its curve approaches that of Uniform-GFM for larger MM, because this experiment adds a specialist and a near-specialist arm for each new objective while keeping the number of easily discarded dominated arms fixed; hence, a growing fraction of arms remains relevant to benchmark estimation. These results confirm the main message of the synthetic study: as the number of objectives grows, directly identifying representatives is more sample-efficient than recovering every objective-wise winner.



D.3 Sensitivity to Representative Gaps


The main experiment in Figure 1 varies an objective-wise gap that is irrelevant to the answer. Here, we instead vary the gap between the optimal representative and its closest competitor, while keeping the near-specialist gap fixed at ξ=0.1\xi=0.1. This directly changes the difficulty of the few-for-many decision.


Figure 5: Sensitivity to the representative gap. Both known-group and latent-group identification become harder as the representative gap shrinks.


Figure 5 exhibits the expected quadratic growth in sample complexity as the representative gap decreases. In the known-group case, the average sample complexity of GFM-Elim increases from the order of thousands to the order of hundreds of thousands as the representative gap shrinks from 0.20.2 to 0.0250.025; the latent-group results for AFM-Elim follow the same pattern. This contrasts with Figure 1: the proposed methods are insensitive to gaps that do not change the few-for-many answer, yet correctly spend more samples on gaps that determine the representative decision. Together, the two experiments indicate that the algorithms adapt to the right notion of difficulty.

ZL​(t)t⟶1TL⋆​(𝝁)almost surely.\frac{Z_{L}(t)}{t}\longrightarrow\frac{1}{T_{L}^{\star}(\bm{\mu})}\quad\text{almost surely}.
