Abstract
This paper investigates the convergence properties of spectral algorithms—a class of regularization methods originating from inverse problems—under covariate shift. In this setting, the marginal distributions of inputs differ between source and target domains, while the conditional distribution of outputs given inputs remains unchanged. To address this distributional mismatch, we incorporate importance weights, defined as the ratio of target to source densities, into the learning framework. This leads to a weighted spectral algorithm within a nonparametric regression setting in a reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS). More importantly, in contrast to prior work that largely focuses on the well-specified setting, we provide a comprehensive theoretical analysis of the more challenging misspecified case, in which the target function does not belong to the RKHS. Under the assumption of uniformly bounded density ratios, we establish minimax-optimal convergence rates when the target function lies within the RKHS. For scenarios involving unbounded importance weights, we introduce a novel truncation technique that attains near-optimal convergence rates under mild regularity conditions, and we further extend these results to the misspecified regime. By addressing the intertwined challenges of covariate shift and model misspecification, this work extends classical kernel learning theory to more practical scenarios, providing a systematic framework for understanding their interaction.

1 Introduction
Supervised learning, a cornerstone of modern machine learning, aims to develop predictive models from labeled training data drawn from a source distribution. Classical statistical learning theory establishes that, under the idealized assumption of identical source and target distributions, basic empirical risk minimization can learn a function that generalizes well to unseen target instances [34]. However, practical applications frequently violate such distributional stationarity. Temporal variations, sampling biases, or environmental changes often create discrepancies between source and target distributions, a challenge collectively termed distribution shift or dataset shift [28]. This phenomenon represents a critical obstacle to robust machine learning deployment.
This work focuses specifically on covariate shift, a prevalent form of distribution shift characterized by differing marginal distributions while maintaining identical conditional distributions. Such shifts arise in many practical scenarios. For example, medical datasets collected across different hospitals may exhibit varying demographic compositions (covariate distributions), while diagnostic criteria for individual patients (conditional distributions) remain consistent [13]. Various techniques have been developed to mitigate the effects of covariate shifts. Among these, importance weighting [31], which adjusts the source data based on the density ratio, appears particularly effective, and there has been a number of work investigating its theoretical properties [15, 26, 12, 16, 10].
We formalize our problem within a regression framework utilizing the square loss. Given a training sample 𝒛={(xi,yi)}i=1n\displaystyle\boldsymbol{z}=\left\{(x_{i},y_{i})\right\}_{i=1}^{n} drawn from a source distribution ρS​(x,y)\displaystyle\rho^{\textnormal{S}}(x,y), where xi∈𝒳\displaystyle x_{i}\in\mathcal{X}, yi∈𝒴\displaystyle y_{i}\in\mathcal{Y}, the input space 𝒳\displaystyle\mathcal{X} is a separable and compact metric space, and the output space 𝒴\displaystyle\mathcal{Y} is a subset of ℝ\displaystyle\mathbb{R}, our goal is to find a predictor f\displaystyle f that minimizes the expected risk on the target distribution ρT​(x,y)\displaystyle\rho^{\textnormal{T}}(x,y):
ℰ⁡(f)=𝔼(x,y)∼ρT⁡[(y−f⁡(x))2].\mathcal{E}(f)=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{(x,y)\sim\rho^{\textnormal{T}}}\left[(\,y-f(x)\,)^{2}\right].
Under covariate shift, a setting where marginal distributions ρ𝒳S​(x)\displaystyle\rho^{\textnormal{S}}_{\mathcal{X}}(x) and ρ𝒳T​(x)\displaystyle\rho^{\textnormal{T}}_{\mathcal{X}}(x) differ but conditional distribution ρ⁡(y∣x)\displaystyle\rho(y\mid x) remains identical, the source and target distributions factorize as:

with ρ𝒳S≠ρ𝒳T\displaystyle\rho^{\textnormal{S}}_{\mathcal{X}}\neq\rho^{\textnormal{T}}_{\mathcal{X}}. In this setting, the optimal predictor over all measurable functions is the regression function
fρ​(x)=∫y​𝑑ρ​(y∣x).f_{\rho}(x)=\int y\,\mathrm{d}\rho(y\mid x).
Nevertheless, learning over the entire space of measurable functions is infeasible in practice, making the specification of a suitable hypothesis space fundamental. Here, we work within reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces (RKHS). Specifically, let K:𝒳×𝒳→ℝ\displaystyle K\colon\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R} be a Mercer kernel, which is a continuous, symmetric, and positive semi-definite function. This kernel induces an RKHS ℋ\displaystyle\mathcal{H} with the reproducing property
f⁡(x)=⟨f,K⁡(⋅,x)⟩ℋ,∀f∈ℋ.f(x)=\left\langle f,K(\cdot,x)\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}},\quad\mathrel{\forall}f\in\mathcal{H}.
Additionally, we assume uniform boundedness supx∈𝒳K⁡(x,x)≤κ2\displaystyle\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}K(x,x)\leq\kappa^{2}, where κ≥1\displaystyle\kappa\geq 1. The regression problem is categorized based on the relationship between fρ\displaystyle f_{\rho} and ℋ\displaystyle\mathcal{H}: it is well-specified when fρ∈ℋ\displaystyle f_{\rho}\in\mathcal{H}, and misspecified otherwise. In the latter case, misspecification typically implies reduced regularity of fρ\displaystyle f_{\rho}, which introduces difficulties in the learning problem.
To approximate fρ\displaystyle f_{\rho} within the RKHS ℋ\displaystyle\mathcal{H}, we minimize the expected risk 𝔼(x,y)∼ρT⁡[(y−f⁡(x))2]\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{(x,y)\sim\rho^{\textnormal{T}}}\left[(\,y-f(x)\,)^{2}\right] over f∈ℋ\displaystyle f\in\mathcal{H}. As established in prior work (e.g., Proposition 2 of [35]), this minimization is equivalent to solving the operator equation:
LK​f=LK​fρ,f∈ℋ,L_{K}\,f=L_{K}\,f_{\rho},\quad f\in\mathcal{H},
where LK\displaystyle L_{K} is the integral operator defined as
LK:L2​(𝒳,ρ𝒳T)→L2​(𝒳,ρ𝒳T),f↦∫𝒳f⁡(x)​K​(⋅,x)​d​ρ𝒳T​(x).L_{K}\colon L^{2}(\mathcal{X},\rho^{\textnormal{T}}_{\mathcal{X}})\to L^{2}(\mathcal{X},\rho^{\textnormal{T}}_{\mathcal{X}}),\quad f\mapsto\int_{\mathcal{X}}f(x)K(\cdot,x)\,\mathrm{d}\rho^{\textnormal{T}}_{\mathcal{X}}(x).
Note that ℋ\displaystyle\mathcal{H} continuously embeds into L2​(𝒳,ρ𝒳T)\displaystyle L^{2}(\mathcal{X},\rho^{\textnormal{T}}_{\mathcal{X}}), hence LK\displaystyle L_{K} acts on f∈ℋ\displaystyle f\in\mathcal{H}. Given only a finite sample 𝒛={(xi,yi)}i=1n\displaystyle\boldsymbol{z}=\left\{(x_{i},y_{i})\right\}_{i=1}^{n} drawn from ρS\displaystyle\rho^{\textnormal{S}}, the empirical version of this equation is expressed as

(1)
where we use the Radon-Nikodym derivative (commonly referred to as the density ratio) w\displaystyle w to weight the sample:
w​(x)=d​ρ𝒳Td​ρ𝒳S​(x).w(x)=\frac{\mathrm{d}\rho^{\textnormal{T}}_{\mathcal{X}}}{\mathrm{d}\rho^{\textnormal{S}}_{\mathcal{X}}}(x).
This strategy is known as importance weighting [31], which aligns the source and target distributions in expectation. For notational simplicity, we define the empirical integral operator:

L^K​f=S^K∗​𝒚.\widehat{L}_{K}\,f=\widehat{S}_{K}^{\ast}\,\boldsymbol{y}.
Since L^K\displaystyle\widehat{L}_{K} is generally non-invertible, regularization is required to solve (1). To address this, we employ spectral algorithms—a class of regularization techniques designed to produce a stable inverse operator. Originally developed for ill-posed linear inverse problems (see, e.g., [8]), these algorithms bridge learning theory and inverse problems, rendering them highly effective for regression tasks [36]. Regularization is achieved through filter functions that amplify significant eigencomponents while suppressing less influential ones:
Definition 1.1 (Filter functions).
A family of functions gλ:[0,κ2]→[0,∞)\displaystyle g_{\lambda}\colon[0,\kappa^{2}]\to[0,\infty), parameterized by λ>0\displaystyle\lambda>0, constitutes filter functions if:
(1)
There exists E≥0\displaystyle E\geq 0 such that for all θ∈[0,1]\displaystyle\theta\in[0,1]:

(2)
(2)
There exist τ≥1\displaystyle\tau\geq 1 and F≥0\displaystyle F\geq 0 such that for all θ∈[0,τ]\displaystyle\theta\in[0,\tau]:
supt∈[0,κ2]tθ​|1−t​gλ​(t)|≤F​λθ.\sup_{t\in[0,\kappa^{2}]}t^{\theta}|1-tg_{\lambda}(t)|\leq F\lambda^{\theta}.
(3)
Intuitively, condition (2) ensures that the regularized inverse defined by gλ​(t)\displaystyle g_{\lambda}(t) remains bounded, thereby guaranteeing numerical stability. Condition (3) controls the approximation error by requiring the residual term |1−t​gλ​(t)|\displaystyle|1-tg_{\lambda}(t)| to vanish at a prescribed rate as λ→0\displaystyle\lambda\to 0. The parameter τ\displaystyle\tau, known as the qualification of the regularization method, quantifies the maximum degree of source smoothness that the spectral algorithm can effectively exploit. Specifically, it characterizes the class of target functions for which optimal convergence rates are attainable (see Assumption 2). Through the filter function framework, spectral algorithms encompass a broad family of regularization methods. Common examples include:
•
Kernel ridge regression:

•
Early-stopped gradient descent:

•

gλcut(t)=t−1𝟏{t≥λ}\displaystyle g_{\lambda}^{\text{cut}}(t)=t^{-1}\mathbf{1}_{\left\{t\geq\lambda\right\}}, with arbitrary τ≥1\displaystyle\tau\geq 1 and E=F=1\displaystyle E=F=1; the cutoff threshold is λ\displaystyle\lambda.
Given a filter function gλ\displaystyle g_{\lambda}, the weighted spectral algorithm then takes the form:
f^𝒛,λ=gλ​(L^K)​S^K∗​𝒚.\widehat{f}_{\boldsymbol{z},\lambda}=g_{\lambda}(\widehat{L}_{K})\,\widehat{S}_{K}^{\ast}\,\boldsymbol{y}.
(4)
Recently, Gizewski et al. [15] studied spectral algorithms (4) under covariate shift, assuming that the density ratio is uniformly bounded. Ma et al. [26] and Feng et al. [12] investigated kernel ridge regression—a special case of spectral algorithms (4)—under the condition that the density ratio is either bounded or unbounded but has finite second moment. Gogolashvili et al. [16] also studied kernel ridge regression, but employed a more general moment condition on the density ratio, as detailed in Assumption 2. Fan et al. [10] adopted the moment condition from [16] and analyzed spectral algorithms (4) within covariate shift. All these works are confined to well-specified settings, where fρ∈ℋ\displaystyle f_{\rho}\in\mathcal{H}.
Remark 1.2.
Without covariate shift and importance weighting, the empirical integral operator L^K\displaystyle\widehat{L}_{K} has an operator norm uniformly bounded by κ2\displaystyle\kappa^{2} under any sampling, ensuring that it is compact, self-adjoint, and positive. Thus, filter functions apply to L^K\displaystyle\widehat{L}_{K} as intended. Under covariate shift, however, the density ratio w\displaystyle w may be unbounded, potentially causing the eigenvalues of L^K\displaystyle\widehat{L}_{K} to exceed the filter’s domain [0,κ2]\displaystyle[0,\kappa^{2}], thereby causing the estimator f^𝐳,λ\displaystyle\widehat{f}_{\boldsymbol{z},\lambda} ill-defined. Nevertheless, with appropriate moment conditions on w\displaystyle w (Assumption 2), concentration inequalities demonstrate that as n→∞\displaystyle n\to\infty, ‖L^K‖\displaystyle\left\|\widehat{L}_{K}\right\| can be bounded arbitrarily close to ‖LK‖\displaystyle\left\|L_{K}\right\| with high probability (see Lemma A.14). Therefore, we may proceed with our analysis under sampling scenarios where gλ​(L^K)\displaystyle g_{\lambda}(\widehat{L}_{K}) remains well-defined, assuming without loss of generality that ‖L^K‖≤κ2\displaystyle\left\|\widehat{L}_{K}\right\|\leq\kappa^{2}. We can still establish probabilistic bounds while maintaining mathematical rigor.
This paper analyzes the approximation ability of f^𝒛,λ\displaystyle\widehat{f}_{\boldsymbol{z},\lambda} to fρ\displaystyle f_{\rho} under covariate shift, with a particular emphasis on the case of misspecification of fρ\displaystyle f_{\rho}. Our work advances the machine learning theory by making two principal contributions:
1.
This paper presents a unified theoretical framework for analyzing the convergence of spectral algorithms under covariate shift. This framework establishes explicit connections between the degree of model misspecification (quantified via a source condition parameter) and the severity of the distribution shift (characterized by moment conditions on the density ratio). Specifically, when the density ratio is uniformly bounded, our analysis achieves minimax optimal convergence rates (Corollary 2.4). Moreover, when the underlying kernel possesses favorable embedding properties, we demonstrate that near-optimal convergence rates remain attainable even for scenarios involving unbounded density ratios (Corollary 2.5).
2.
We introduce a truncation scheme specifically designed to handle unbounded density ratios. This scheme enables spectral algorithms to achieve near-optimal convergence rates when the regression problem is well-specified (Corollary 2.7). Notably, fast convergence rates for misspecified scenarios are also provided.
The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: Section 2 first states necessary assumptions, then presents our main theorems and corollaries. Section 3 provides a literature review and a comparative analysis with existing works; Section 4 proves the main theorems, with auxiliary lemmas deferred to Appendix.
2 Main Results
In this section, we establish the convergence rates of the weighted spectral algorithm estimator f^𝒛,λ\displaystyle\widehat{f}_{\boldsymbol{z},\lambda} to the regression function fρ\displaystyle f_{\rho}. We begin by presenting key assumptions for our convergence analysis. The first assumption, adopted from [16], characterizes the severity of covariate shift through moment conditions on the density ratio w\displaystyle w.

The boundedness condition ‖fρ‖∞≤G\displaystyle\left\|f_{\rho}\right\|_{\infty}\leq G is commonly employed in misspecification analyses [17, 24], which additionally guarantees |y|≤G\displaystyle|y|\leq G holds ρT\displaystyle\rho^{\textnormal{T}}-a.e. While recent work [40] relaxes this via Lp\displaystyle L^{p}-embedding techniques, their approach does not extend to covariate shift due to potential discrepancies between Lp​(𝒳,ρ𝒳T)\displaystyle L^{p}(\mathcal{X},\rho^{\textnormal{T}}_{\mathcal{X}}) and Lp​(𝒳,ρ𝒳S)\displaystyle L^{p}(\mathcal{X},\rho^{\textnormal{S}}_{\mathcal{X}}).
Our next assumption concerns the decay rate of the eigenvalues {tj}j∈N\displaystyle\left\{t_{j}\right\}_{j\in N} of LK\displaystyle L_{K}, which fundamentally determines the capacity of the induced RKHS ℋ\displaystyle\mathcal{H}. Rapid eigenvalue decay induces a small RKHS ℋ\displaystyle\mathcal{H}, typically enabling faster learning when fρ∈ℋ\displaystyle f_{\rho}\in\mathcal{H}. Conversely, slow decay corresponds to a larger ℋ\displaystyle\mathcal{H}, which may hinder the learning process but increases the chance that fρ\displaystyle f_{\rho} resides within ℋ\displaystyle\mathcal{H}.
{assumption}[Eigenvalue decay rate]
The eigenvalues {tj}j∈N\displaystyle\left\{t_{j}\right\}_{j\in N} of LK\displaystyle L_{K} exhibit a polynomial decay rate of order β>1\displaystyle\beta>1. Specifically, there exist positive constants c\displaystyle c and C\displaystyle C such that
c​j−β≤tj≤C​j−β,∀j∈N.c\,j^{-\beta}\leq t_{j}\leq C\,j^{-\beta},\quad\mathrel{\forall}j\in N.
In Assumption 2, the upper bound quantifies the capacity of ℋ\displaystyle\mathcal{H} and determines the convergence rates in our main results, while the lower bound ensures that these rates are optimal in the minimax sense. The requirement β>1\displaystyle\beta>1 arises from the trace-class property of LK\displaystyle L_{K}:
∑j∈Ntj=Tr⁡(LK)≤κ2<∞.\sum_{j\in N}t_{j}=\operatorname{Tr}\left(L_{K}\right)\leq\kappa^{2}<\infty.

{assumption}[Embedding index]

3 Related Work and Discussion
Kernel methods offer a powerful nonparametric framework for function approximation in reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces (RKHS). Foundational work by Caponnetto and de Vito [5] established minimax optimal convergence rates for kernel ridge regression (KRR) in well-specified settings, where the true regression function belongs to the RKHS. These rates depend critically on two parameters: the source condition r\displaystyle r, which characterizes the smoothness of the target function; and the eigenvalue decay rate β\displaystyle\beta of the integral operator, which characterizes the capacity of the RKHS. Subsequent research has extended this framework to various settings, including gradient descent [29, 4], robust regression [17, 19], and random feature methods [30, 23]. Through spectral filtering, spectral algorithms generalize KRR to encompass a broader class of regularization families [25]. In well-specified regimes (1/2≤r≤τ\displaystyle 1/2\leq r\leq\tau), where τ\displaystyle\tau denotes the qualification parameter, these algorithms achieve minimax optimality [18, 2, 9]. Subsequent advances address misspecification (0<r<1/2\displaystyle 0<r<1/2), demonstrating that spectral algorithms retain optimality under the condition 2​r>1−1/β\displaystyle 2r>1-1/\beta [24]. Recently, the embedding index α0∈[1/β,1]\displaystyle\alpha_{0}\in[1/\beta,1], introduced by Fischer and Steinwart [14], refines the analysis of RKHS capacity. Building on this, a broader optimality range 2​r>α0−1/β\displaystyle 2r>\alpha_{0}-1/\beta is obtained [40]. Despite these advances, all the aforementioned works remain confined to identical source and target distributions.
Prior analyses of misspecified kernel methods typically yield convergence rates governed by a threshold 𝐓≤1\displaystyle\mathbf{T}\leq 1:
‖f^𝒛,λ−fρ‖ρ𝒳T≤{O⁡(n−r2​r+1/β),2​r>𝐓;O⁡(n−r𝐓+ϵ+1/β),2​r≤𝐓.\left\|\widehat{f}_{\boldsymbol{z},\lambda}-f_{\rho}\right\|_{\rho^{\textnormal{T}}_{\mathcal{X}}}\leq\begin{cases}O\left(n^{-\frac{r}{2r+1/\beta}}\right),&2r>\mathbf{T};\\
O\left(n^{-\frac{r}{\mathbf{T}+\epsilon+1/\beta}}\right),&2r\leq\mathbf{T}.\end{cases}
Here, the rate O⁡(n−r2​r+1/β)\displaystyle O(n^{-\frac{r}{2r+1/\beta}}) for 2​r>𝐓\displaystyle 2r>\mathbf{T} is minimax optimal; thus, a smaller 𝐓\displaystyle\mathbf{T} enlarges the minimax optimal range. Before the introduction of the embedding index (Assumption 2), the best known threshold was 𝐓=1−1/β\displaystyle\mathbf{T}=1-1/\beta [24]. Recently, Zhang et al. [40] improved this to 𝐓=α0−1/β\displaystyle\mathbf{T}=\alpha_{0}-1/\beta using the embedding index. In contrast, our Theorem 2.3 achieves 𝐓=α0\displaystyle\mathbf{T}=\alpha_{0}, which appears weaker by 1/β\displaystyle 1/\beta. This gap arises from their reliance on concentration inequalities that require uniform boundedness of empirical operators (e.g., Lemma 32 in [40])—a condition that fails under covariate shift where the density ratio w\displaystyle w may be unbounded. We conjecture that with bounded w\displaystyle w, our threshold could similarly reach 𝐓=α0−1/β\displaystyle\mathbf{T}=\alpha_{0}-1/\beta. As for Theorem 2.6, the threshold is 𝐓=1\displaystyle\mathbf{T}=1. Although we incorporate the embedding index α0\displaystyle\alpha_{0} in our proof (see Section 4.2), the final result becomes independent of α0\displaystyle\alpha_{0}. Whether this threshold can be improved to 𝐓=α0\displaystyle\mathbf{T}=\alpha_{0} remains an open problem.
For covariate shift adaptation, Shimodaira [31] pioneered importance weighting (IW) to correct distributional bias in parametric regression. Their analysis demonstrates that IW compensates for discrepancies induced by model misspecification. Extensions confirm that IW improves convergence rates for parametric models under misspecification [20, 37]. However, the role of IW in nonparametric regimes is less clear: empirical studies show that the effects of IW gradually attenuate during training on neural networks [3], while theoretical analyses also challenge conventional IW paradigms [38]. Even for well-studied kernel methods, analyses under covariate shift remain sparse. To our knowledge, only Gogolashvili et al. [16] investigate the scenario where the regression function lies outside the RKHS, but their analysis is restricted to KRR, leaving broader spectral algorithms unaddressed; moreover, their work guarantees convergence only to RKHS projections rather than the regression function itself. Finally, to implement IW in practice, one must estimate the density ratio using unlabeled data. Traditional estimation methods require strict boundedness assumptions [33, 27], while recent advances relax these restrictions through neural networks [11, 39]. It is also worth noting that the standard definition w=d​ρ𝒳T/d​ρ𝒳S\displaystyle w=\mathrm{d}\rho^{\textnormal{T}}_{\mathcal{X}}/\mathrm{d}\rho^{\textnormal{S}}_{\mathcal{X}} represents only one particular formulation among various weighting paradigms, as systematically cataloged in [21].
Compared to existing results, we extend the work of Fan et al. [10] in two key directions: (1) we incorporate the embedding index to address model misspecification; (2) we generalize their L2\displaystyle L^{2}-norm convergence results to the norms of interpolation spaces ‖⋅‖[ℋ]γ\displaystyle\left\|\cdot\right\|_{[\mathcal{H}]^{\gamma}}, which encompasses both the RKHS norm and the L2\displaystyle L^{2}-norm as special cases. Our work also relates to Gizewski et al. [15], Ma et al. [26], Gogolashvili et al. [16], and Feng et al. [12]. While these studies focus exclusively on well-specified settings, several critical distinctions emerge:
•
Gizewski et al. [15] analyze spectral algorithms under covariate shift, assuming a uniformly bounded density ratio w\displaystyle w and a well-specified model (i.e., fρ∈ℋ\displaystyle f_{\rho}\in\mathcal{H}). Furthermore, they introduce a framework for estimating the density ratio w\displaystyle w, this estimated ratio is then integrated into the spectral algorithm to produce the final estimator. However, their approach requires the restrictive assumption that w\displaystyle w belongs to the RKHS ℋ\displaystyle\mathcal{H}, which implies uniform boundedness of the density ratio.
•
Ma et al. [26] investigate kernel ridge regression—a special case of spectral algorithms—under covariate shift for the well-specified model (i.e., fρ∈ℋ\displaystyle f_{\rho}\in\mathcal{H}). They study two cases: 1) with a uniformly bounded density ratio, KRR achieves minimax optimal convergence rates (up to logarithmic factors); 2) when the density ratio is unbounded but has finite second moment, a truncated ratio also yields minimax optimal rates (up to logarithmic factors). Feng et al. [12] extend this analysis to general loss functions beyond squared loss. However, these analyses are limited by their requirement that the eigenfunctions be uniformly bounded—an assumption that is difficult to verify.
•
Our moment condition on the density ratio is adopted from Gogolashvili et al. [16], who also employ ratio truncation to achieve near-optimal convergence rates when the ratio is unbounded. In our work, we extend their kernel ridge regression framework to broader spectral algorithms and establish convergence guarantees under misspecified settings.

4 Proof of Main Theorems
This section presents the proofs of Theorem 2.3 and Theorem 2.6. Our analysis proceeds in three main steps: first, we decompose the estimation error into distinct components; second, we establish individual bounds for each component through auxiliary propositions; and third, we combine these bounds to complete the overall argument. Auxiliary technical results supporting these propositions are deferred to Appendix.
