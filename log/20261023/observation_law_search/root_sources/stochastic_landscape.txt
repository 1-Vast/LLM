The temporal dynamics and stochasticity of gene expression are critical to cell fate decisions, yet integrating snapshot omics data across multiple time points remains a major challenge. Here, we introduce DiffusionOT, a dynamic machine learning framework that infers cellular trajectories from multi–time point single-cell transcriptomics by incorporating stochastic effects. DiffusionOT transforms stochastic differential equations into ordinary differential equations, using optimal transport and neural networks to solve a high-dimensional landscape model. Through an unsupervised learning of the stochastic force in the data, DiffusionOT allows robust inference of the underlying stochastic dynamics of cell-state transitions. The framework includes a stochastic trajectory analysis module for lineage tracing and a gene perturbation module for in silico knockout and overexpression experiments. Benchmarks on simulated and four real-world datasets, including a spatial Stereo-seq dataset, demonstrate DiffusionOT’s accuracy and efficiency in inferring state-transition velocities, cellular trajectories, population growth, gene regulatory networks, and cell-fate landscape.

In recent years, the rapid development of single-cell transcriptomics has provided unprecedented opportunities for elucidating cellular dynamics during development and disease processes (1, 2). Typically, inferring temporal dynamic information from snapshot transcriptomics data is essential for understanding the underlying mechanisms of biological networks.

For a single snapshot transcriptomics dataset, pseudotime ordering, which assumes gene expression similarity among developmental neighbors (3–10), is a popular concept for inferring temporal information. Reversed graph embedding is used to describe multiple fate decisions in a fully unsupervised manner (4). RNA velocity characterizes the direction of cell evolution by the spliced-to-unspliced mRNA ratio (5, 6). CellRank integrates similarity-based robustness and RNA velocity to infer directed, probabilistic trajectories under normal and perturbed conditions (8). PhyloVelo estimates transcriptomic velocity via monotonically expressed genes and reconstructs a velocity field by integrating single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data and lineage information (10).

The landscape approach, likening cell development to a ball rolling from high to low potential energy with bifurcations as differentiations, provides another insightful perspective for inferring temporal dynamics from single snapshot data (11–17). For instance, scEpath reconstructs developmental trajectories by calculating the energy landscape and constructing probabilistic directed graphs (13). Markov chain entropy is used to quantify the differentiation potency of single cells within the energy landscape (15). MuTrans uses multiscale reduction to quantify attractors and their transition probabilities in snapshot data while also constructing a low-dimensional landscape (16). In addition, the landscape-flux theory has been used to explore the physical principles of cell differentiation and reprogramming from single-cell data (17). However, as temporal information is lost in a single snapshot, the need to manually determine the start and end points of differentiation using prior biological knowledge limits the application of these algorithms.

Time-series snapshot data inherently contain temporal information that aids in revealing underlying developmental dynamics. However, the destructive nature of sequencing experiments typically results in unpaired snapshot data from multiple time points. This situation presents two major challenges: (i) Connecting these unpaired snapshots and robustly inferring their continuous dynamic behavior and lineage tracing are core challenges. (ii) Incorporating stochasticity in the continuous dynamics during inference is also crucial.

Many algorithms based on optimal transport (OT), a classic mathematical theory on transporting masses between two distributions (18), have been recently proposed to address the pairing problem in time-series snapshot data (19–29). Waddington-OT assumes that cells develop in a locally linear manner in gene expression space, inferring ancestor-descendant relationships and developmental trajectories based on OT theory (19). TrajectoryNet first combines the continuity equation, continuous normalizing flows, and growth to solve the dynamic unbalanced OT (UOT) problem (20). To account for biological noise, PRESCIENT models cellular evolution as a stochastic differential equation (SDE) and learns an underlying differentiation landscape (21). PI-SDE extends the PRESCIENT framework by leveraging the Hamilton-Jacobi equation, which governs the potential energy function, to reconstruct the underlying cellular potential energy landscape (22). MIOFlow extended TrajectoryNet by incorporating manifold learning and autoencoder (AE) to infer stochastic dynamics in latent space (23). TIGON generalized the approach by introducing a growth term to measure cell proliferation and death and using a dimensionless formulation to simplify high-dimensional integrals, enabling an integrated framework to simultaneously train and estimate driving forces and growth (24). However, TIGON did not consider the influence of noise and faced difficulties in algorithm convergence. Recently, the DeepRUOT algorithm explored stochastic effects and theoretically discussed the connection between regularized unbalanced optimal transport (RUOT) and the Schrödinger bridge problem (SBP) (25, 30), effectively modeling the stochastic state transitions and cell proliferation simultaneously and overcoming convergence difficulties. However, it assumes constant noise without training or estimation, thereby facing challenges in effectively capturing varying noise levels across different biological systems. In summary, while these models effectively capture cellular dynamics from time-series snapshot data, most of these approaches only partially address the inherent stochasticity of single-cell systems or the simultaneous estimation of driving force, growth rate, and noise level.

In this study, we introduce DiffusionOT, which extends the UOT framework of TIGON to a RUOT problem. The pivotal methodological advancement of DiffusionOT is its data-driven, unsupervised estimation of the diffusion coefficient, a key parameter that directly quantifies system stochasticity from the data. We achieve this by transforming the original deterministic ordinary differential equation (ODE) into an SDE, which enables the joint learning of drift, diffusion, and growth parameters directly from the data. DiffusionOT has several outputs: (i) DiffusionOT uses SDE modeling to describe cellular evolution and provides a method for effectively estimating the diffusion coefficient to improve robustness against noise; (ii) by estimating the score function and the log probability density function, DiffusionOT can reconstruct the developmental landscape in a data-driven manner; and (iii) it provides a stochastic trajectory analysis (STA) approach to predict cell fate decisions and estimate cell ancestry and a gene perturbation method to simulate in silico gene knockouts and overexpression experiments. Notably, STA can effectively predict cell fate decisions and reconstruct the historical trajectories of cells to estimate cell ancestry using a probabilistic language. This approach provides a continuous, dynamic, and stochastic description that characterizes both the historical development and future progression of cells.

We validate DiffusionOT using simulated data with known dynamics, demonstrating its robustness and accuracy in trajectory inference, growth rate prediction, and gene regulatory network (GRN) inference compared to existing methods. We test and compare DiffusionOT using four time-series single-cell transcriptomic datasets from various biological processes, including epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), mouse hematopoietic differentiation with multibifurcations, high-dimensional zebrafish hair cell (HC) regeneration, and spatially resolved embryonic development. DiffusionOT effectively infers stochastic dynamics among velocity, trajectory, cell growth, and temporal GRNs; reconstructs the cell fate landscape; predicts cell fate decisions and performs cell lineage tracing; and quantifies the role of genes through perturbation analysis.

Here, x represents gene expression or system state, t is time, v(x,t) is the deterministic drift guiding cell state transitions, g(x,t) describes net cell birth and death (growth), d is the diffusion coefficient characterizing the intensity of stochastic noise, and p0,pT are the empirical cell state distributions at the initial and final time points, respectively. Without any constraints, this problem would have infinitely many solutions that satisfy the equation. Typically, it is naturally assumed that the evolution follows the principle of minimum energy (or shortest distance), balancing the cost of moving cells (transport) and the cost of changing cell population size (growth). This is characterized by minimizing the Wasserstein-Fisher-Rao (WFR) distance (31–33): WFR0,T=T∫0T∫Rd12(∣∣v∣∣2+αg2)pdxdt. Here, the cost penalizes high velocities and large growth rates, enforcing biologically plausible, smooth dynamics. The above equation (Eq. 1) can be viewed as an SDE in terms of gene expression (Materials and Methods) (34, 36). Solving it directly with neural SDEs might suffer from computational instability and slow training (22, 37).

We define the flux Vp=vp−d∇p, which converts the problem into a more stable ODE form, thereby enhancing the stability and speed of the algorithm (Materials and Methods) (25, 38, 39). In the loss function, the high-dimensional integrals in gene expression space are typically computationally intractable. Similar to a previous study, we use a dimensionality reduction (DR) theorem to transform the high-dimensional integrals into path integrals, substantially reducing the computational complexity (24). The transformed loss function also includes the diffusion coefficient, probability density, and score function. Here, we parameterize the velocity, growth, and score function using neural networks (Fig. 1B). We provide a statistic to estimate the diffusion coefficient and use score matching (40) and flow matching (41) to match the score function velocity and growth, respectively, thereby constructing the overall loss function (Materials and Methods). Last, we use neural ODEs for optimization and solution (42, 43).

After reconstructing the stochastic dynamics, DiffusionOT enables a suite of downstream biological analyses directly derived from the learned model. First, the inferred velocity field can be used to indicate the direction of cellular evolution, and integration along the vector field yields cellular evolutionary trajectories (Fig. 1C). In addition, we infer a directed, signed, and weighted GRN by computing the Jacobian matrix of the velocity field J=[∂vi∂xj]∈ℝd×d with respect to genes (24, 44). The element ∂vi∂xj can be interpreted as the regulatory effect of the jth gene on the ith gene. A positive value indicates activation, while a negative value indicates inhibition. In single-cell trajectory inference, methods for modeling GRNs are broadly categorized into two paradigms: those that assume a GRN structure before simulating dynamics [e.g., FLECS (45)] and those that first learn dynamics from data and then extract the GRN [e.g., SF2M (46)]. DiffusionOT belongs to the latter category without prior regulatory knowledge (Materials and Methods).

The growth rate reflects changes in cell abundance, and its gradient ∇g=[∂g∂x1,…,∂g∂xd]T∈ℝd can be used to represent the contribution of genes to growth, with the most positively and negatively ranked genes being considered as growth related (Fig. 1D). Moreover, the combination of the estimated score function and the corresponding probability density function allows us to construct the energy landscape of the system (Fig. 1E).

Notably, by incorporating the estimated diffusion coefficient, we introduce a new STA approach (Materials and Methods). This adds controlled noise during trajectory simulation, generating an ensemble of possible paths that reflect biological variability. The forward process predicts cell fate decisions, while the backward process can estimate cell ancestry, such as retrodicting the progenitor sources of HCs in the zebrafish sensory HC regeneration. Compared with ODE trajectories, SDE trajectories provide a probabilistic description that more closely reflects the real biological environment (Fig. 1F). We also introduce an in silico gene perturbation analysis, which simulates gene knockout and overexpression by perturbing the initial values of the concerned genes (Fig. 1G). Comparing the changes in fate distribution with the wild type allows us to predict the function of the gene, providing valuable biological insights.

To effectively evaluate the performance of the DiffusionOT model, we validated it on a two-GRN model known as the mutual inhibited self-activation (MISA) model, which includes mutual inhibition between the two genes and self-activation, modeled with Hill functions (Fig. 2A and Materials and Methods). This model is widely used to simulate cell fate decision processes such as stem cell differentiation (47).

Under the model parameter settings used in this study, the generated data contain two attractors. When cells differentiate toward attractor 1, they also undergo cell division. In contrast, when cells differentiate toward attractor 2, they undergo apoptosis (Fig. 2B). We sampled 500 initial cells from a normal distribution N([2,2],0.15) and simulated subsequent time points using an SDE, resulting in data at four time points: 0, 1, 2, and 6. Using the simulated snapshot data with a diffusion coefficient d of 0.04, we reconstructed the potential landscape using the trained score function and estimated probability density function to characterize cellular evolution within DiffusionOT (Fig. 2C). A clear bifurcation in the landscape at time point 2 leading to the formation of two attractors is evident. We further compared the training results of BalancedOT, TIGON, DeepRUOT, and DiffusionOT with the ground truth. TIGON and DeepRUOT are established representatives of UOT and RUOT problems, respectively. In velocity estimation, BalancedOT exhibited the wrong direction near the sparsely sampled attractor 2 (indicated by the gray dashed box), whereas DiffusionOT closely matched the ground truth (Fig. 2D and fig. S1A). Trajectory inference results also showed that DiffusionOT had smaller prediction errors compared to the other three methods (fig. S1, B and C). Regarding the growth rate, the cell population rapidly increased initially and then stabilized over time (fig. S2A), with gene X1 driving cell division and gene X2 driving apoptosis. DiffusionOT accurately identified the growth-related genes (fig. S2B) and showed better alignment with the ground truth in growth rate prediction compared to TIGON and DeepRUOT (Fig. 2E and fig. S2, C to G). In the underlying cellular evolution process, genes exhibit self-activation and mutual inhibition. DiffusionOT successfully inferred the toggle-switch interactions between genes X1 and X2 (Fig. 2, F and G, and fig. S3A). Compared to other methods, DiffusionOT achieved a notable improvement in mean squared error (MSE) (fig. S3, A and B, and text S4).

To systematically assess the robustness of DiffusionOT across varying noise levels, we generated simulated datasets with diffusion coefficients d = 0.01, 0.02, 0.04, and 0.1 (fig. S4) and systematically quantified the performance metrics including velocity accuracy, trajectory accuracy, GRN accuracy, and population accuracy (Fig. 2, H to J). We further incorporated the estimated diffusion into DeepRUOT, resulting in a variant termed DeepRUOT+, to assess whether it achieves performance comparable to DiffusionOT. Using MSE to evaluate deviations in velocity and trajectory predictions from ground truth values, we observed that while error magnitudes increase with elevated diffusion coefficients (Fig. 2H and fig. S1, D and E), DiffusionOT demonstrated the smallest and most stable error increments compared to DeepRUOT, DeepRUOT+, TIGON, and BalancedOT, which exhibited accelerating error accumulation without stabilization. In addition, DeepRUOT+ demonstrated improved performance and robustness over the original DeepRUOT.

Notably, DiffusionOT consistently achieved better performance in GRN reconstruction when measured by MSE against the true network (fig. S3B). In AUROC and AUPRC evaluations, DiffusionOT showed comparable performance to TIGON at lower noise levels (d = 0.01 to 0.02) and outperformed both competitors at higher noise intensities (d = 0.1) while maintaining consistent advantages over BalancedOT across all conditions (Fig. 2I and fig. S3C). Similarly, DiffusionOT matched the performance of DeepRUOT and DeepRUOT+ at low noise (d = 0.01) but surpassed them at high noise (d = 0.1), with DeepRUOT+ consistently outperforming DeepRUOT. Correlation analyses revealed that DiffusionOT’s superiority in Spearman and Pearson coefficients increased as noise levels escalated (fig. S3, D and E). Growth rate comparisons further demonstrated that although TIGON and DeepRUOT+ initially outperformed DiffusionOT at d = 0.01, DiffusionOT maintained stable performance as diffusion coefficients increased. This contrasts the error inflation observed among DeepRUOT, DeepRUOT+, TIGON, and BalancedOT (Fig. 2J).

We further assessed DiffusionOT under anisotropic noise by fixing d1=0.04 and varying d2∈{0.01,0.02,0.08,0.10} (fig. S5). Moderate anisotropy caused only minor performance loss relative to the isotropic baseline, while AUROC and AUPRC remained stable even at high d2, confirming topological robustness. However, edge-weight accuracy (Pearson and MSE) degraded substantially with increasing noise, with a milder decline also observed when d2<d1. These findings suggest that the isotropic assumption primarily affects weight quantification under severe anisotropy, while topology inference remains reliable under mild to moderate anisotropy.

To evaluate performance in identifying state transitions, we benchmarked the methods on a synthetic three-gene regulatory model (fig. S6). While BalancedOT produced false transitions (fig. S6B), the other methods correctly inferred the overall trajectory direction (fig. S6, B to F). Notably, trajectories from TIGON and DeepRUOT exhibited greater variance (dashed boxes; fig. S6, G and H), in contrast to the more uniform and smoother paths generated by DiffusionOT. The static coupling method (48, 49), while achieving accurate inter–time-point matching (fig. S6H), is incapable of inferring continuous intermediate states or velocity fields and thus cannot support downstream analysis. The hyperparameters used for each dataset in DiffusionOT, TIGON, and DeepRUOT are summarized in tables S1 to S3, respectively. Overall, using simulated data, we demonstrated the robustness and accuracy of DiffusionOT in velocity estimation, trajectory inference, GRN identification, and population density prediction under diverse noise conditions.

The EMT represents a critical mechanism in cancer evolution, widely recognized as both a hallmark of tumor aggressiveness and a potential driver of malignant progression (50–52). Investigating this dynamic process holds particular significance for identifying key genetic regulators governing EMT activation. We analyzed scRNA-seq data from a longitudinal lung cancer EMT model spanning seven temporal stages: 0d, 2d, 6d, 10d, 2dW, 6dW, and 10dW (where “W” denotes withdrawal of TGFβ following EMT induction) (51). This system captures four cellular states: epithelial (E1 to E3), MET (mesenchymal-epithelial transition), partial EMT (pEMT1 to pEMT3), and mesenchymal (M) states (fig. S7, A to E).

To efficiently train the DiffusionOT model, we performed principal components analysis (PCA) to retain the first two principal components (PCs) as input for DiffusionOT (cumulative contribution rate of 46.92%). The inferred velocity and trajectories accurately reconstructed the transition from the epithelial (E) state through two intermediate states (pEMT and MET) to the final M state (Fig. 3A and fig. S7F). The reconstructed dynamic energy landscape and score function also showed consistent state-transition dynamics (Fig. 3B). Furthermore, to explore differentiation dynamics, we initiated STA from the center point of E type (Fig. 3C). Deterministic ODE-based predictions constrained differentiation exclusively toward the mesenchymal fate, whereas SDE modeling incorporating noise effects revealed probabilistic differentiation outcomes: While most of the cells (60%) progressed toward the M state, subpopulations diverged into MET (11%) and pEMT (29%) intermediate states (fig. S8D). This probabilistic resolution better reflects biological reality where stochasticity results in heterogeneous fate decisions rather than uniform differentiation. We also performed gene expression analysis along EMT trajectories to identify critical genes (Fig. 3E). The top 10 EMT-promoting genes included M markers ITGB4, PDGFRA, SNAI2, and VIM, while the top 10 EMT-inhibiting genes included E markers KRT8, MUC1, and CDH1 (52). Notably, Axl, Notch3, and CTNNB1 emerged as potential regulators involved in EMT processes, suggesting their involvement in modulating phenotypic plasticity during state transitions (53–55). These findings demonstrate that DiffusionOT helps to resolve both differentiation dynamics and identify mechanistically relevant genetic drivers.

Furthermore, we conducted gene perturbation analysis to quantify the roles of genes. We first trained a random forest classifier on the original data to predict cell type (fig. S8, A to C). When simulating overexpression of VIM, we applied the random forest classifier to the terminal cells obtained from STA using SDE and found that all cells differentiated into the M state (Fig. 3F). In contrast, when simulating knockout of VIM, all cells differentiated into the E state (Fig. 3F), indicating that VIM expression promotes the EMT process, consistent with experimental findings (56). When simulating overexpression of Notch3, the cells at the terminal time initially differentiated mainly into the pEMT state, and subsequently, with increasing perturbation intensity, all cells differentiated into the E state (Fig. 3F). In contrast, when simulating knockout of Notch3, the fate distribution at the terminal time point showed little difference from the wild type (perturbation intensity of 0) (54). In addition, we predicted the functions of ITGB4, CD117, POU2F1, and TROP2 in the EMT process (Table 1) from the corresponding gene perturbation analysis results (fig. S8, D and E).

CDH1 and MUC1 are known E marker genes, while VIM, CTNNB1, CD44, and CD24 are known M marker genes (52). To gain a deeper understanding of the EMT mechanism, we investigated changes in the GRNs of those marker genes across different cell states. By analyzing the GRN at specific time points (0d and 10d), we found clear inhibitory interactions between E and M markers. For example, VIM, CD44, and CD24 inhibit CDH1 and MUC1, and there is also notable self-activation, such as that of VIM and CD44 (Fig. 3, G and H). Of course, differences in the GRN were also observed across different cell states (fig. S9, A and B). For instance, at the terminal time point (10d), the regulatory strength between genes was notably weakened, with only the self-activation of the M marker gene VIM remaining prominent (Fig. 3, G and H). This may also indicate that different cell states are regulated by corresponding GRNs.

To analyze the cell proliferation pattern during EMT, we checked the training of the growth rate (Fig. 3D) and found that the growth rate is relatively high at time point 6d, when cells are primarily in intermediate states such as pEMT and are gradually differentiating into the M state. Moreover, we identified the top 10 genes most strongly associated with growth during this process (Fig. 3I). A search of the UniProtKB database revealed that CD45, ITGB4, SNAI2, PDGFRA, and PECAM1 are related to growth (57). In addition, with withdrawal at 10d enabling observation of MET in subsequent stages (fig. S7D), we trained the model on the MET process (figs. S10 and S11) and found growth-related genes, as well as mutual inhibition and self-activation between E marker genes and M marker genes. In summary, DiffusionOT reconstructed the EMT dynamics and identified critical genes involved in the EMT process through downstream analysis.

To investigate the effectiveness of DiffusionOT in identifying multibifurcating systems, we applied it to single-cell mouse hematopoietic datasets. Using an AE, we projected the gene expression data onto a two-dimensional space. The resulting visualization showed that cells differentiated from an undifferentiated state through five bifurcations into various mature cell types (fig. S12, A to C), which aligned with previous studies (21, 22, 58). To better demonstrate the superior performance of DiffusionOT, we compared the training results of DiffusionOT and TIGON. We found that TIGON failed to accurately identify the direction of differentiation (the gray dashed-line boxes), whereas DiffusionOT accurately indicated the differentiation paths from undifferentiated cells to mature cells (Fig. 4, A and B). Specifically, in the gray dashed-line box, where the sample size of erythrocyte (Er) and megakaryocytes (Mk) cells is relatively small, TIGON’s inferred velocity field showed that Mk cells appeared to self-renew, and Er cells seemed to differentiate from Mk cells rather than from undifferentiated cells. In contrast, DiffusionOT correctly identified the differentiation direction, and its reconstructed energy landscape demonstrated similar transition dynamics (fig. S12D), consistent with the original findings (58).

We initiated STA from the centroid of undifferentiated cells to examine their subsequent evolution. The results from 300 generated stochastic trajectories (Fig. 4C and fig. S12E) revealed that, at the terminal state, 32.66% of the cells differentiated into the monocyte (Mo) state, 57.91% differentiated into the basophil (Ba) type or remained in the undifferentiated cell type, and 9.43% followed trajectories differentiating into the neutrophil (Neu) cell state. In contrast, ODE simulations only showed differentiation into the Ba type. To quantify the roles of the predicted genes, we performed gene perturbation analysis on critical genes Irf8 and Cebpa for this biological process (Fig. 4D), based on previous studies (58). When simulating overexpression of Irf8, cells progressively differentiated into the Mo state at the terminal time point, indicating that Irf8 is a key driver gene for Mo differentiation, consistent with previous experimental studies (59). When simulating overexpression of Cebpa, the proportion of Er and undifferentiated cells increased, while other categories such as Mo, Ba, and Neu states showed varying degrees of proportion decline, suggesting that Cebpa is likely a key driver gene for Er state differentiation (60). In addition, we analyzed several critical genes associated with the trajectories obtained from STA (fig. S13C). For instance, when simulating overexpression of Retnlg, all cells differentiated into the Mo state at the terminal time point. When simulating overexpression of Igfbp7, all cells differentiated into the Mk state. When simulating overexpression of Pdk3, the proportion of undifferentiated cells notably increased, while the Mo state cells decreased, and Mk cells increased. Similarly, overexpression of Rpl5 led to a notable increase in undifferentiated cells and a decrease in Mo state cells, with an increase in Ba and mast (Ma) state cells (fig. S13E).

Consistent with the previous studies (58–62), we identified marker genes for the Er (Hba-a2, Epor, Gata2, and Gata1), Mk (Mpl and Gp1bb), Mo (Klf4, Csf1r, and Irf8), and Neu (Elane, Mpo, and Gfi1) states (fig. S12, F to I) and estimated their corresponding GRNs (Fig. 4, E and F, and fig. S14, A to D). We observed a high degree of consistency between the GRN structures identified by DiffusionOT and TIGON. For instance, in the Mo state, Csf1r emerged as a critical gene, with other genes exhibiting notable inhibitory effects on it. Gfi1 also stood out as a key regulator, demonstrating strong activating effects on Csf1r while generally inhibiting other genes. Similarly, Neu marker genes showed highly consistent regulatory relationships in the Neu state GRNs across both methods. Nevertheless, certain differences were noted. For example, DiffusionOT identified an inhibitory effect of Gfi1 on Klf4 (63), whereas TIGON did not detect such regulation (dashed boxes). Moreover, the GRN in the Neu state differed markedly between the two approaches. In the GRN inferred by DiffusionOT, Mo marker genes showed almost no regulatory influence, while interactions among Neu marker genes appeared more complex, dominated by mutual inhibition and self-inhibition. In contrast, TIGON predicted strong regulatory effects from Mo marker genes (dashed boxes). Similarly, the GRNs for the Er and Mk states at the 6d time point are shown in fig. For example, both approaches indicated that Hba-a2 and Gata2 act as central nodes in the Er state, with Hba-a2 primarily subject to inhibition from other genes while Gata2 receives activating inputs. DiffusionOT is a deep learning framework that integrates OT with stochastic dynamics, extending it to dynamic RUOT to infer continuous stochastic dynamics from observed snapshots. This extension allows DiffusionOT to not only capture cell division and death but also characterize stochasticity in real biological data. Accurate estimation of the diffusion coefficient is essential, as incorrectly specified noise inevitably leads to biased drift inference (71, 72). Furthermore, DiffusionOT supports a suite of downstream analyses, including GRN inference, dynamic landscape reconstruction, and STA. Specifically, STA approach is introduced for lineage tracing, such as using the forward process to predict cell fate decisions and the backward process (reverse-time SDE) to trace cell ancestry (38). A gene perturbation analysis is also introduced to simulate in silico gene knockouts and overexpression experiments. Moreover, comparative evaluations on both simulated and real-world datasets confirm the robustness of the DiffusionOT framework.

The model comprises three losses: reconstruction error, loss of score matching (40), and the cost function in WFR distance (32). It uses long-short reconstruction errors and a two-stage training procedure to boost convergence speed and stability (24, 25). By transforming SDEs into ODEs, DiffusionOT parameterizes the velocity, growth, and score function using neural networks and optimizes them via neural ODEs. Specifically, compared to previous models (20, 24), through unsupervised learning of diffusion coefficient, DiffusionOT quantifies stochastic effect, thereby adapting to systems with different noise levels and enhancing the robustness of trajectory inference against measurement noise and data sparsity, addressing a key limitation of existing methods that assume constant noise levels (22, 25).

To validate DiffusionOT, we applied it to both a simulated dataset and four single-cell transcriptomics datasets. By benchmarking against known dynamics and quantifying inferred velocity, trajectories, growth, and GRN identification, we demonstrated the robustness and accuracy of DiffusionOT compared to existing methods (Fig. 2). In the example of EMT, DiffusionOT successfully reconstructed the underlying dynamics, mapped the cell fate landscape, and predicted key regulatory genes through perturbation analysis, with results consistent with prior studies (Fig. 3 and Table 1). Next, we applied DiffusionOT to mouse hematopoiesis, a complex multibifurcation system where undifferentiated progenitors undergo five successive branching events to generate diverse mature cell types. Compared to TIGON, DiffusionOT more accurately resolved differentiation trajectories even in sparsely sampled regions (Fig. 4, A and B). Moreover, STA reliably predicted differentiation dynamics initiated from undifferentiated cells (Fig. 4C). To evaluate scalability in high-dimensional settings, we inferred the cell fate dynamics of zebrafish sensory HC regeneration, demonstrating DiffusionOT’s ability to handle complex, high-dimensional data (Fig. 5). Notably, the backward process in STA retrodicted the progenitor sources of HCs, suggesting a novel approach for lineage tracing (Fig. 5B). Last, by successfully reconstructing embryonic developmental dynamics from transcriptomic data alone, DiffusionOT demonstrates its potential for spatial transcriptomic analysis (Fig. 6). The inferred trajectories and state transitions align with known spatial organization in the developing embryo, suggesting that when transcriptomic states are strongly correlated with spatial position, as in patterned embryonic tissues, the method can reliably recapitulate spatial dynamics. However, caution remains necessary in contexts where transcriptomic state decouples from location, such as in metastatic or highly migratory cell populations. These applications of DiffusionOT across various biological processes underscore its generality.

To benchmark the trajectory inference capability, we compared DiffusionOT against the static coupling UOT method across simulated and real datasets (fig. S20) (32, 48, 49). For pure trajectory alignment, the static coupling algorithm was computationally superior, completing matching in seconds versus hours for DiffusionOT and exhibited lower variance in high-dimensional latent spaces (e.g., four-dimensional or six-dimensional AE projections of mouse hematopoiesis data). However, for dynamical inference, DiffusionOT provided continuous and smoother trajectories that delineated biologically plausible state transitions, while the static method offered only discrete cell-to-cell assignments between snapshots. Crucially, only DiffusionOT enabled the prediction of unobserved intermediate states and supported downstream analyses such as GRN inference and perturbation simulation, which are beyond the scope of the static coupling framework. Future work may explore hybrid approaches to bridge this efficiency-interpretability gap.

Despite the attempts DiffusionOT has made in dynamics inference, several limitations remain. First, DR is required to handle high-dimensional sequencing data due to computational constraints of OT (24), potentially losing information. Future directions include integrating manifold learning to better preserve high-dimensional structure (73), as well as jointly training the embedding and dynamical model to optimize the latent space for capturing cellular dynamics (74). Second, recent theoretical advances have established that diffusion coefficients are both identifiable and essential for inferring drift fields from temporal snapshots under nonstationary conditions (71, 72). Our DiffusionOT framework is, to our knowledge, the first to enable the joint learning of drift, diffusion, and the score function. However, our current implementation assumes isotropic noise, which may not apply to heterogeneous systems. Extending the framework to handle noise varying with time and system state is a major challenge. In addition, the constraint of stochasticity to remain within a reasonable range and prevent overestimation will be a focus of our future work. Third, DiffusionOT reconstructs the energy landscape through score matching in a data-driven manner. Of note, in the model presented in this study, the effect of growth rate is considered. One could attempt to decompose the potential energy landscape into two components in a data-driven manner: a cell-type landscape and a pluripotency landscape, the latter of which is induced by changes in growth rate (36). Moreover, projecting high-dimensional landscapes into low-dimensional spaces for visualization is challenging. Combination with a model-based idea for dimension reduction of the landscape may offer a solution (47). In addition, with the increasing availability of omics data, integrating multiomics data into a unified framework to reveal cross-modal regulatory mechanisms is becoming increasingly important. For example, incorporating spatial transcriptomic data could provide a more comprehensive picture of cell-cell communication (75), which may be related to the approach of artificial intelligence–generated virtual cells (76).

In summary, DiffusionOT enhances the robustness and accuracy of cellular trajectory inference through improved computational approaches, provides an interpretable visualization of cellular transitions, and offers comprehensive tools for downstream driver genes and perturbation prediction analysis. In addition, the algorithmic framework proposed in this study for connecting snapshot data across multiple time points is not only applicable to biological systems but can also be extended to other fields, such as image generation in image-to-image translation (77), reinforcement learning where policy optimization minimizes differences between state distributions (78), and cross-lingual alignment for aligning word vector spaces across different languages (79).

To efficiently apply DiffusionOT to real-world sequenced datasets, we first project the data into a low-dimensional space. For the EMT dataset, we used PCA to reduce the dimensionality to two PCs. For the mouse hematopoietic datasets and spatial Stereo-seq mouse embryo data, we used an AE to project the data into two dimensions. For the zebrafish sensory HC regeneration data, we first used an AE to reduce the dimensionality to a six-dimensional AE space and trained the DiffusionOT model. Subsequently, UMAP was used to project the results into two dimensions for visualization. Specifically, the AE was implemented using PyTorch packages (80), while the other methods were used from Seurat packages (81). After obtaining the data in the low-dimensional space, each axis of the reduced space was scaled to the range [−2, 2]. It has been demonstrated that the dimensionless solver in DiffusionOT is capable of directly solving the OT problem in high-dimensional spaces (24).

Different DR methods are suited to different data characteristics. For downstream analyses such as GRN inference and identification of key genes, reversible and differentiable projections such as PCA or AE are required. PCA is typically preferred for linear projections, especially when the leading PCs capture a substantial portion of the variance (e.g., in the EMT data, the first two PCs explain 46.92% of the cumulative variance) and offer better interpretability. In contrast, AEs are more suitable for high-dimensional, nonlinear data due to the strong representational capacity of neural networks. For tasks not requiring detailed gene-level analysis, UMAP and t-SNE are valuable alternatives. UMAP excels at preserving global structure and is faster for large datasets, making it suitable for studies such as developmental trajectory inference. In contrast, t-SNE offers superior resolution for distinguishing local neighborhoods and is often preferred for sample clustering and classification. We recommend the DR method that best preserves biologically relevant information and dynamics.

We conducted preprocessing before DR of the time-series data. For the EMT data, we first removed cell types with a small number of samples to avoid interference with training. We then log transformed the data and normalized it, followed by PCA DR to extract the first two PCs, which accounted for a cumulative contribution rate of 46.92% (fig. S7). Cells were subjected to transforming growth factor–β (TGFβ) induction during the initial four time points (0d, 2d, 6d, and 10d), with withdrawal of TGFβ at 10d enabling observation of MET in subsequent stages (fig. S7D). This experimental design creates a biphasic trajectory: forced EMT progression followed by spontaneous MET regression. To enhance the resolution of dynamic cellular reprogramming, we established distinct analytical frameworks for EMT and MET processes, allowing systematic interrogation of state transitions in both directions.

For the mouse hematopoietic data, which includes three time points (2d, 4d, and 6d), we first removed genes with expression levels below 1% in all cells and further eliminated cell types with a small number of samples. The remaining cell types included Er, Mk, Ba, Ma, eosinophils (Eos), Neu, Mo, dendritic cells [plasmocytoid DC (pDC); Ccr7 + migratory cDC], and undifferentiated cells (Ud). We then log transformed the gene expression data and retained the top 3000 highly variable genes for subsequent analysis, resulting in a dataset size of 43,968 cells × 3000 genes.

For the zebrafish sensory HC regeneration data, we first selected data from four time points (0 min, 30 min, 3 hours, and 5 hours) and log transformed the gene expression. Because there were no labeled cells, we performed Leiden clustering and then selected corresponding categories based on marker genes for further analysis, resulting in a dataset size of 7122 cells × 2191 genes.

For the spatial Stereo-seq mouse embryo transcriptomic data, given the large number of sequenced cells and multiple time points in the original data, we selected gene expression data from five time points (E9.5, E11.5, E13.5, E15.5, and E16.5) based on the embryonic development patterns summarized in the original paper (66). We filtered the data for specific cell types for further analysis. We first removed cells with fewer than 200 expressed genes and genes expressed in fewer than three cells and then selected the top 3000 highly variable genes for subsequent analysis. The remaining cell types included brain, branchial arch, connective tissue, mesenchyme, GI tract, muscle, Neural crest, and spinal cord. Because of the large differences in the number of cells at each time point, we performed resampling to set the number of cells at E9.5, E11.5, E13.5, E15.5, and E16.5 to 2500, 5000, 7500, 8000, and 6000, respectively.

Here, λ1 and λ2 are hyperparameters, which are set to 1 in this study. The velocity, growth, and score function are all parameterized using neural networks and solved using neural ODE (42, 43). The ODE solver DOPRI5, a type of Runge-Kutta method, is used for the temporal integration. The Adam optimizer is used to update the gradients (88). To streamline the training process and enhance model convergence, we implement a two-stage training strategy (25) that involves pretraining and unified training phases.

In the score function training step, we fix the parameters related to velocity V and growth g. We then focus on training the score function ∇logp using Monte Carlo sampling and the score matching method. This pretraining phase allows the score function to be optimized independently, setting a solid foundation for subsequent steps. Next, in the velocity and growth training step, with the score function ∇logp fixed, we proceed to train the parameters of the velocity V and growth g. We use the flow matching approach to roughly align these parameters, which helps the model to converge more rapidly and prevents divergence that might occur because of the selection of initial values.

The total loss function is integrated for unified training. The training process for the model involves a structured approach to minimize the total loss over a specified number of epochs. Initially, the diffusion coefficient d is set to its initialized value d0. For each epoch, data xt is sampled independently and identically distributed from the distribution Dt at the time point t. The model then predicts the subsequent state Dˆt+1 using the velocity function V. The total loss is calculated by combining three components: the OT loss (lossOT), the score matching loss (lossscore), and the reconstruction error (R), each weighted by their coefficients (λ1 and λ2). After computing the loss, the estimated diffusion coefficient d∼ is updated. Last, the neural networks, NN1, NN2, and NN3, are updated using the Adam optimizer to minimize the total loss, thereby enhancing the model’s performance over successive epochs. For more details on the specific algorithm and pseudocode, please refer to text S5. This structured approach ensures that each component of the model is optimized effectively, leading to a robust and accurate model. We performed a comprehensive hyperparameter sensitivity analysis on the MISA model, focusing on the reconstruction loss coefficient (λ2) with values of 0.1, 1, and 10 (fig. S22). Across these settings, all model configurations successfully recovered the core biological dynamics, including the correct gene regulatory interactions and growth rate contributions. While the choice of λ2 influenced the initial convergence speed, DiffusionOT demonstrated strong final convergence and result stability once training was complete, indicating robustness across a reasonable hyperparameter range. Furthermore, the computational costs of training DiffusionOT across datasets are summarized in table S4.

After training the model, we output its velocity to visualize the evolutionary trajectory of cells. Furthermore, by taking the partial derivatives of the velocity with respect to the variables, one can obtain the Jacobian matrix J=[∂vi∂xj]∈ℝd×d, which can be used to identify the interactions between genes. In addition, the cell-specific Jacobians or gradients are indeed aggregated by averaging across all cells to derive gene-level conclusions. This approach allows us to summarize the population-level trends and obtain robust estimates of gene regulatory relationships and growth-related contributions. Here, ∂vi∂xj can be interpreted as the regulatory effect of the jth gene on the ith gene. If it is greater than 0, then it is considered that the jth gene activates the ith gene, whereas if it is less than 0, then it indicates an inhibitory effect, with the magnitude representing the strength of the regulation. The growth rate shows changes in cell abundance, and the gradient of the growth rate ∇g=[∂g∂x1,…,∂g∂xd]T∈ℝd indicates the contribution of genes to growth. Genes ranked at the top and bottom are considered as those related to growth promotion and inhibition, respectively. When the DR method is reversible and differentiable, such as in the case of PCA or AE with a differentiable decoder, we can rigorously bridge the latent and gene spaces. Specifically, we leverage the inverse mapping and its Jacobian to project quantities such as the growth rate gradient and the velocity field back to the original gene expression space. This allows us to compute derivatives with respect to individual genes and reconstruct the gene-level analysis (text S6).

The algorithm, based on SDEs, allows STA. The forward process is given bydxt=v(xt,t)dt+εdt(23)

Here, ε∼N(0,2dˆ) and dˆ is the estimated diffusion coefficient. Given a starting point, the forward process predicts the cellular state at the terminal time by integrating the SDE, thereby predicting its future cell fate. For the backward processdxt=[v(xt,t)−d∇logp(xt,t)]dt+εdt(24)

Given the terminal point, one can infer which initial cells the cell could have originated from and estimate the probabilities of different ancestral cells. This process injects Gaussian noise, governed by the estimated diffusion coefficient dˆ, at each step, thereby producing a stochastic trajectory. We repeat this process multiple times to obtain an ensemble of trajectories representing probabilistic development. In this context, we use cell labels from the original data to train a corresponding random forest classifier (89). For the terminal cells obtained by SDE trajectories, we use the trained random forest classifier to predict their cell types and ultimately calculate the fate distribution by tallying the frequencies of each category.

The critical genes are identified along the differentiation path for gene perturbation analysis. Specifically, for a given initial value point s0, we simulate a single ODE trajectory (s0,s1,…,sn) leading to the terminal point, where n is the number of points along the trajectory, sn represents the terminal time location, and si represents the intermediate cell position. By applying the inverse mapping of reversible DR, the trajectory is projected back into the gene expression space to obtain (sˆ0,sˆ1,…,sˆn), where sˆi=(sˆi,0,sˆi,1,…,sˆi,m) is the gene expression of the ith cell and m is the number of genes. For each specific gene gk, we calculate the Pearson correlation coefficient PCCk=PCC(Sk,T) between its gene expression along the ODE trajectory and time changes. Here, Sk=[sˆ0,k,sˆ1,k,…,sˆn,k] represents the gene expression along the unique ODE trajectory, and T=[t0,t1,…,tn] is the time for the trajectory. By ordering the correlation coefficients, genes related to the trajectory are identified. Genes with correlation coefficients close to 1 are considered to be more involved in the differentiation process, while those close to −1 are thought to suppress it.

We use critical genes for gene perturbation analysis to simulate gene overexpression and knockout. Specifically, for gene gk, whose expression at the initial time point is sˆ0,k, we multiply it by corresponding factors {10−3,10−2,10−1,100,101,102,103} to perturb the initial value, where 100=1 indicates no treatment also called wild type, {10−3,10−2,10−1} indicates simulated gene knockout, and {101,102,103} represents simulated gene overexpression. We use a trained random forest classifier to predict cell type and then statistically analyze the changes in fate distribution of STA before and after perturbation.

One advantage of the trajectory-based correlation approach is its focus on expression dynamics along biologically relevant paths, thereby avoiding the need for high-dimensional Jacobian estimation, such as in the identification of GRNs. Another advantage is its ability to identify genes whose expression changes monotonically along the progression of a specific biological process (e.g., EMT), making it particularly suitable for selecting candidate genes in perturbation experiments designed to modulate that process.

The MISA model is used to verify the performance of DiffusionOT (Fig. 2A). It consists of two genes that mutually inhibit each other and are self-activated. The regulatory relationship between them is modeled by Hill function as follow (47)dx1dt=ax1nsn+x1n+bsnsn+x2n−kx1+εtdx2dt=ax2nsn+x2n+bsnsn+x1n−kx2+εt(25)

Here, S represents the threshold of the sigmoidal function, a denotes the magnitude of self-activation, b denotes the magnitude of mutual inhibition between x1 and x2, k denotes the degradation rate of gene, and εt is a Gaussian white noise whose autocorrelation function is <εt,ε0>=2Dδ(t), with δ(t) as Dirac function and D as the diffusion coefficient. Cell growth is designed to contain division and apoptosis as follow (90)g=gdivision−gapoptosis=x11+x121+0.5t−x21+x2(26)

With the time step Δt, we chose γ∼U[0,1]. If g(x)≥0 and γ<g(x)Δt, then a cell divides into two, and we add a white noise N(0,0.01) to gene expression. If g(x)≤0 and γ<−g(x)Δt, then the cell is apoptosis, and we delete the data point. In other situations, the cell remains unchanged.

In this study, we chose a = 0.7; b = 0.8; D = 0.01, 0.02, 0.04 and 0.1; S = 0.5; n = 4, and k = 1. We generated one group of initial cells (500 data points), which are independent and identically distributed from normal distributions satisfying N([2,2],0.15), with the time step Δt=0.05. At each time step, we corrected the negative expression to be 0. Under this parameter setting, the system corresponds to two attractors. With the growth rate configuration, when cells differentiate toward attractor 1, they undergo division, leading to a gradual increase in cell count. Conversely, differentiation toward attractor 2 is accompanied by apoptosis, resulting in a progressive decrease in cell numbers (Fig. 2B).
