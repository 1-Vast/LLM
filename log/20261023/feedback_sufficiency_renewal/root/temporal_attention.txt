Abstract Understanding non-genetic determinants of cell fate is critical for developing and improving cancer therapies, as genetically identical cells can exhibit divergent outcomes under the same treatment conditions. In this work, we present a deep learning approach for cell fate prediction from raw long-term live-cell recordings of cancer cell populations under chemotherapeutic treatment. Our Transformer model is trained to predict cell fate directly from raw image sequences, without relying on predefined morphological or molecular features. Beyond classification, we introduce a comprehensive explainability framework for interpreting the temporal and morphological cues guiding the model’s predictions. We demonstrate that prediction of cell outcomes is possible based on the video only, our model achieves balanced accuracy of 0.94 and an F1-score of 0.93. Attention and masking experiments further indicate that the signal predictive of the cell fate is not uniquely located in the final frames of a cell trajectory, as reliable predictions are possible up to 8 h before the event. Our analysis reveals distinct temporal distribution of predictive information in the mitotic and apoptotic sequences, as well as the role of cell morphology and p53 signaling in determining cell outcomes. Together, these findings demonstrate that attention-based temporal models enable accurate cell fate prediction while providing biologically interpretable insights into non-genetic determinants of cellular decision-making.

Author summary Cancer treatment often fails because not all cells respond in the same way. Even when exposed to the same drug, some cells die while others survive and continue to grow, which can lead to relapse. Understanding why genetically identical cells make different decisions is therefore an important challenge. In this study, we investigate whether it is possible to predict the fate of individual cells directly from time-lapse microscopy videos. We develop a computational approach that learns from raw image sequences without relying on manually defined features. This allows us to capture subtle changes in cell behavior over time. We find that cell fate can be predicted with high accuracy based on the video data alone, and that informative signals appear well before the final outcome is visible. By analyzing the model, we identify when during a cell’s lifetime predictive information emerges and which aspects of cell behavior are most relevant. Our results show that dynamic patterns in cell behavior contain early indicators of fate decisions, providing new opportunities to better understand variability in treatment response.

1 Introduction A major challenge in cancer treatment is the partial response of tumors to chemotherapeutic drugs. Even when initially efficacious, such treatments often leave behind residual tumor cells, which can subsequently give rise to resistant clones and drive further disease progression. Understanding the mechanisms underlying cellular non-responsiveness to therapy is therefore of critical importance. Individual cells within identical population can exhibit markedly different outcomes under the same therapeutic conditions. While some cells undergo programmed cell death in response to treatment, others survive and continue proliferating, ultimately contributing to therapy resistance and relapse. Genetic factors play a key role in treatment response, however, a broad range of cell-intrinsic factors can lead to heterogeneous responses even among genetically identical cells [ 1 – 3 ]. These cells may differ in their epigenetic, transcriptomic, and proteomic states [ 4 ]. Understanding the non-genetic factors that drive divergent outcomes is essential for improving treatment strategies and for gaining insight into fundamental cellular decision-making processes. Previous studies [ 4 – 7 ] have identified multiple molecular factors influencing cell fate following treatment. In addition to markers such as p53, these studies highlight the importance of dynamic cellular properties, including cell-cycle phase and proliferative state, in determining drug susceptibility. Although these studies inspected the link of individual factors to cell fate, a systematic investigation of the predictability of cell fate under chemotherapeutic treatment given various dynamical factors is still lacking. Deep learning methods provide a powerful means to investigate not only molecular determinants of cell fate, but also morphological and spatial aspects of the cellular environment. Rather than relying on the manual selection and quantification of predefined features [ 8 ], deep learning approaches enable direct assessment of cell fate predictability using raw time-lapse microscopy image data. This allows simultaneous capture of dynamic, morphological, and contextual features of cells within a culture. Recent studies have adopted representation learning techniques to capture cellular dynamics directly from raw data. Several self-supervised approaches leverage autoencoder-based architectures to learn latent representations of cell morphology and dynamics, which are subsequently analyzed in search for their biological relevance. For example [ 9 ], learned representations from time-lapse sequences and related principal components of the latent space to handcrafted morphological features, enabling post-hoc interpretation. Similarly [ 10 ], employed vector-quantized autoencoders to learn discrete representations for cell-cycle classification. While these methods provide useful representations of cellular dynamics, they primarily focus on cell-cycle stage rather than direct prediction of divergent outcomes of cells under treatment. [ 11 ] explored cell fate prediction in multicellular contexts. The study proposed an interpretable graph neural network framework to model cell fate decisions in tissues, explicitly incorporating cell–cell interactions through graph structures derived from live imaging data. Their work highlights the importance of spatial context and collective dynamics but does not consider intracellular signaling dynamics in the prediction. [ 12 , 13 ] introduced autoencoder-based cell representations to predict cell fate. Latent representations were analyzed for their link to the morphological and intensity-based features and predictability. Among these studies, a systematic investigation of when predictive information emerges over time and how temporally localized cues relate to interpretable cellular features remains limited. In particular, none of the studies explored the predictive power of Transformer models and their interpretability via the attention mechanism. Transformers represent powerful architectures to process sequential data, such as time-lapse recordings. Their use in the study of cellular dynamics, particularly in the context of chemotherapeutic treatment and dynamic molecular reporters, has not been demonstrated yet. Here, we develop a deep learning model to predict the fate of individual cancer cells following exposure to chemotherapeutic treatment. We propose a Transformer-based architecture that operates on temporal sequences of center-cropped image patches extracted from long-term time-lapse microscopy data. The input captures not only cellular morphology but also the dynamic activity of multiple transiently active molecular markers, including p53, circadian, and cell-cycle reporters. Importantly, the model predicts whether a cell undergoes apoptosis or mitosis directly from raw image sequences, without relying on predefined morphological features, allowing it to learn task-relevant representations in a data-driven manner. We investigate not only the extent to which cell fate can be predicted from video-encoded cellular trajectories but also develop model explainability strategies to address two key questions: (1) when during a cell’s lifetime fate decisions become predictable, and (2) which signals are most informative for those decisions. Our work demonstrates that the combination of rich time-lapse microscopy data, temporal modeling, and attention-based analysis enables the extraction of meaningful biological signals and facilitates the discovery of determinants of cell fate following chemotherapeutic treatment. The contributions of this work can be summarized as follows: We introduce a Transformer-based model for predicting cell fate from long-term single-cell raw image sequences. We demonstrate prediction accuracy on a challenging, imbalanced live-cell imaging dataset and show that prediction of cell fate is indeed possible from videos of cell trajectories. We perform temporal truncation experiments to assess how predictive information is distributed across time. We propose an attention-based explainability framework that links temporally important frames to interpretable morphological and intensity-based features. 2 Methods 2.1 Dataset We use a publicly available live-cell imaging dataset [ 14 ], comprising 274 time-lapse fluorescence microscopy videos. Each video consists of 186 frames with a spatial size of  1024 × 1024  pixels, acquired every 30 minutes over a continuous period of 93 hours. Out of the 274 videos, 118 capture cell cultures under treatment with the chemotherapeutic agent cisplatin. Cisplatin induces DNA damage and was administered 48 hours after the start of imaging at three different dosage levels (high, medium, and low). Three fluorescent reporters were used to monitor key cellular pathways: CFP-hGeminin, which reports on cell-cycle progression by indicating S/G2/M phases; NR1D1::VNP, a fluorescent circadian clock and metabolism reporter reflecting cellular stress and circadian rhythm activity; and a p53 transcriptional activity reporter, capturing stress- and DNA-damage–related signaling dynamics. Together, these channels provide a rich temporal record of molecular signaling and cellular state transitions. In each video, cells are tracked and assigned unique cell identifiers. All annotations were performed manually. Each cell was tracked by marking its centroid across consecutive frames, with mitotic events recorded at the frame in which a parent cell separated into two daughter cells and apoptotic events recorded at the first frame exhibiting clear signs of cell death, such as morphological collapse. In total, the dataset contains approximately 51,000 individual cell trajectories (averaging  ~ 187 cells per video), including about 18,000 mitotic events and 3,500 apoptotic events. The remaining sequences do not carry a cell fate label, either because the cell left the field of view before a fate could be determined or because the recording ended before the fate occurred. To predict fate of individual cells, we extracted temporal image sequences by center-cropping each annotated cell at a spatial resolution of  112 × 112  pixels around its centroid, ensuring that the entire cell body was captured throughout the sequence. Each sequence spans from the first appearance of the cell up to the frame preceding the cell event. If  t  denotes the event frame, i.e.,  t he first frame showing clear apoptotic collapse, or the first frame in which two separate daughter cells are visible, we include only frames 0 to  t − 1  as model input. Thereby, the visible collapse or completed division are never used in prediction. Each sequence was paired with its corresponding cell fate label - mitotic vs. apoptotic outcome. For our approach, we exclusively included cells with annotated mitotic or apoptotic fates. Furthermore, we included only sequences longer than 10 frames. When a crop extended beyond the image boundaries, zero-padding was applied to preserve a fixed patch size. The sequence-length distributions differ across both fate and dose ( S1 Fig ). Apoptotic sequences are considerably longer than mitotic ones (median 73 vs. 45 frames; mean 74.0 vs. 44.2,  S1 Figa ). Control and cisplatin-treated cells have a similar median sequence length (57 vs. 58 frames,  S1 Figc ), but the treated cells show a broader distribution with a longer tail toward high sequence lengths (mean 64.4 vs. 56.7,  S1 Figb ). Across the three cisplatin doses the length distributions are broadly comparable, with a slight decrease at lower doses (median 65, 57, and 51 frames for high, medium, and low dose, respectively). The composition of the dataset with respect to fate and dose in summarized in  Table 1 . The proportion of apoptotic events increases with dose, rising from 0.9% of events in the control condition to 27.4% at the highest dose, while the proportion of mitotic events decreases correspondingly. Since the control group contains only 41 apoptotic sequences, precludes a robust characterization of apoptotic dynamics under control condition. Our interpretability analyses therefore focus on the cisplatin-treated sequences. A large subset of sequences originates from videos for which the information on the administered dose is not available (denoted as “Unknown”). Fig 1 Overview of the proposed cell fate prediction framework and the explainability using temporal attention analysis. 1)  From time-lapse microscopy recordings, we extract single-cell trajectories. Each trajectory starts at the first appearance of a cell and ends at the last frame prior to the fate event (division or death).  2)  For each cell patch, frame-level embeddings are computed using a ResNet-50 backbone. We randomly mask a portion of frames at the end of each trajectory during training.  3)  The sequence of frame embeddings is processed by a Transformer encoder, which integrates temporal information to predict the final cell fate.  4)  To enable interpretability, we extract the attention weights assigned to individual frames and categorize them into high-attention and low-attention frames.  5)  In parallel, we compute handcrafted cell features for each patch (e.g., area, eccentricity, p53 concentration).  6)  Finally, we compare the feature distributions of high- and low-attention frames to identify biologically meaningful patterns associated with the model’s decision-making process. Each input consists of a time-lapse sequence of cropped single-cell images. Individual cell images are first processed independently by a ResNet50 backbone to extract frame-wise (2048-dimensional) feature embeddings. These embeddings represent input tokens to our Transformer model.  [cls]  token is prepended to the sequence of image tokens to serve as a global representation of the entire trajectory. To encode temporal order, we add sinusoidal positional embeddings [ 15 ] to all elements of the sequence, computed on absolute frame indices starting at the first appearance of the cell. The resulting representation is then input into a Transformer encoder composed of 8 identical layers, each employing multi-head self-attention with 4 attention heads. This encoder models long-range temporal dependencies and interactions between time points across the full cell trajectory. On top of the encoder output, we apply an additional attention layer to aggregate temporal information and capture the relative relevance of individual frames for the final prediction. Layer normalization is applied both before and after this attention layer to stabilize optimization and improve representational consistency. Finally, the transformed  [cls]  token is passed through a linear classification head to predict the cell fate based on the full temporal sequence. 2.2.1 Morphology-feature baseline. As a baseline that isolates the contribution of the learned image embeddings, we consider a variant in which the ResNet-50 backbone is replaced by a segmentation-based feature extractor, while the rest of the architecture is left unchanged. For each frame, cells are segmented with Cellpose [ 16 ] and the same morphological features are extracted as described in  Sect 2.3 . The size-related features are log-transformed and all features are standardized using training-set statistics. The resulting per-frame feature vectors are embedded by a small MLP and passed to the same Transformer encoder in place of the ResNet-50 embeddings. The only change to the architecture is the Transformer’s input embedding dimension, set to match the feature-embedding output rather than the ResNet-50 output. All other components and training settings are kept identical, so that the comparison isolates the effect of the representation. 2.3 Explainability In addition to predicting cell fate, we investigate which frames within a temporal sequence are the most influential for the model’s decision-making process and how these frames differ from the less informative ones. To this end, we leverage the attention mechanism of the model as a proxy for temporal importance. During inference, we extract attention weights from the final attention layer of the network. Given a sequence  s  of length  N , we denote the attention weights assigned to individual frames  { f s , i } i = 1 N  by  { a s , i } i = 1 N . To identify the most relevant frames within a sequence, we define a sequence-specific threshold  τ s  as the 90th percentile of the attention weights of  s . Using this threshold, we partition the set of frames into two disjoint subsets: (1) ℋ s : = { f s , i ∣ a s , i ≥ τ s } (2) ℒ s : = { f s , i ∣ a s , i < τ s } Thus,  ℋ s  contains the 10% of frames within sequence  s  receiving the highest attention weights, while  ℒ s  comprises the remaining frames. Before analyzing the content of the high-attention frames, we first verify that these frames are indeed the most influential for the model’s prediction. To this end, we perform a masking experiment on the test set that measures the impact of masked frames on the prediction. For each sequence of length  N , we rank all frames by their attention weight and mask a fraction  ρ ∈ { 0.1 , 0.2 , . . . , 0.9 }  of the sequence, corresponding to  n ρ = r o u n d ( ρ N )  frames. We compare three selection strategies each removing  n ρ  frames out of the: (i) the highest-attention frames, (ii) the lowest-attention frames, and (iii) randomly chosen frames. We emphasize that the masked frames are selected directly from the ranked attention weights of the entire sequence at each fraction  ρ , independently of the fixed high-/low-attention partition defined above. To search for distinguishing features between high- and low-attention frames, we quantify morphological and intensity-based characteristics of cells in all video frames. Individual cells are segmented using Cellpose [ 16 ], and visual features are computed from the resulting segmentation masks using  scikit-image  [ 17 ]. To avoid confounding effects from neighboring cells appearing in the cropped patches, only the centrally located cell is considered for feature computation. The extracted features include cell area, perimeter, equivalent diameter, eccentricity, solidity, circularity, and the mean intensity of each fluorescence channel. For each sequence and feature, we conduct permutation tests with 50,000 iterations to assess whether feature distributions differed significantly between  ℋ s  and  ℒ s . This yields one p-value per feature and sequence. In addition to statistical significance, we quantify the magnitude and direction of differences using Cliff’s delta [ 18 ]. Unlike Cohen’s  d , Cliff’s delta does not assume normality or equal variances and is robust to skewed distributions and unequal sample sizes [ 19 ]. Each sequence therefore contributes a single p-value and a single Cliff’s delta per feature. Aggregating over all  n  analyzed sequences (where  n  denotes the number of sequences in the respective dataset) thus yields  n  p-values and  n  Cliff’s delta values per feature. To assess global consistency of within-sequence effects, we additionally compute the median Cliff’s delta and its 95% confidence interval. A median effect size whose confidence interval is shifted away from zero is interpreted as evidence of a consistent effect, with the magnitude indicating the strength and direction of the association. 2.4 Training We randomly split our cell trajectories into training, validation, and test sets using an 0.8/0.1/0.1 ratio, while approximately preserving the distribution of mitotic and apoptotic events across all splits. During training, we used a batch size of 4 and to address the pronounced class imbalance, we employed a weighted random sampler to approximately balance mitotic and apoptotic samples. As cell trajectories vary in temporal length, sequences were padded at the end of the sequence with empty tokens to match the maximum sequence length within each batch. During training, padded positions were masked within the Transformer encoder to ensure that they do not contribute to the attention computation. At evaluation time, sequences are processed individually and no padding is applied. To improve generalization, we applied a range of data augmentations independently and stochastically during training. The augmentation pipeline includes random geometric transformations, such as horizontal and vertical flips, as well as appearance-based augmentations including color jittering and Gaussian blurring. In addition, we applied a sequence-level augmentation by randomly masking a contiguous segment at the end of each sequence, with the masked portion ranging between 10% and 50% of the total sequence length. Each augmentation is applied with a fixed probability, increasing the diversity of the training data and improving model robustness. The ResNet backbone, Transformer encoder, final attention layer, and classification head were trained from scratch using binary cross-entropy loss. Optimization was performed using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of  1 × 10 − 5  and a weight decay of  1 × 10 − 3 . Training was conducted for up to 86 epochs with early stopping based on the validation loss, and the model parameters corresponding to the best validation performance were retained. All experiments were conducted on a system equipped with an AMD EPYC 7662 CPU, 150 GB of RAM, and an NVIDIA Tesla A100-SXM4 GPU with 40 GB of memory. Reproducibility was ensured through fully deterministic training and evaluation. All experiments were run with fixed random seeds across Python, NumPy, and PyTorch, and deterministic computation settings were enforced to guarantee identical results across runs. 3 Experiments and results In this section, we present experimental results evaluating both the predictive performance and explainability of our model for single-cell fate prediction. We first report classification performance, compare our model against a morphology-based baseline, and extend it to a three-class prediction. We then use temporal truncation experiments to characterize how predictive information is distributed across time, and attention-based explainability to identify the temporal signals and cell characteristics that drive the model’s predictions 3.1 Classification Classification performance was evaluated on a held-out test set of cell trajectories. Our model achieved a balanced accuracy (BAcc) of 0.94 and an F1-score of 0.9326 on the test set. For many of cell in our data the fate is undetermined - these cells do not divide or die within the timespan of our recordings or leave the field of view. We next trained the model to distinguish three classes of sequences - mitotic, apoptotic, and “neither”. Cells labeled as “neither” have not yet undergone a fate transition, their eventual fate is unobserved rather than absent. Therefore, “neither” class inevitably overlaps with the two cell fate classes in feature space. With this caveat, the model achieved a balanced accuracy of 0.8434 and a macro F1-score of 0.7946 ( Fig 2c ). The most errors stem from the confusion between the “neither” and the apoptosis class. We therefore regard three-class classification as feasible but inherently limited by the ambiguity of the “neither” label, and retain the binary fate prediction as the focus of this work. 3.1.1 Temporal truncation. Of the 1,593 mitotic sequences we analyzed, 1,312 ( ~ 80 % ) exhibited a trigger event with crossings most frequently occurring 20–35 frames prior to division (see  S2 Fig ) corresponding roughly to the beginning of the S-phase. Of the 401 apoptotic sequences, only 212 ( ~ 48 % ) exhibited a trigger event, and these crossings were distributed across the entire observed window without a prevalent temporal position. Again, this suggests that signals predictive of cell division appear shortly prior to the event while signals predictive of cell death can be distributed over longer time spans. 3.2 Explainability Beyond predictive performance, understanding how and when the model arrives at its decisions is critical for model interpretation. Unless stated otherwise, the following analyses are restricted to sequences originating from videos in which cisplatin was administered. This lets us specifically study the temporal cues predictive of divergent cell fate decisions under chemotherapeutic treatment. We first verify that the attention weights faithfully reflect the frames the model relies on for its predictions. We then use the attention mechanism to investigate (1) when during a cell’s trajectory predictive information appears and (2) which cell features contribute most to fate discrimination. Finally, we examine the full temporal trajectories of attention and of the interpretable features. 3.2.1 Attention validation. A prerequisite for interpreting attention weights as a measure of temporal importance is that the high-attention frames are indeed the frames the model relies on for its prediction. To confirm this, we progressively masked an increasing fraction of frames in a sequence that are either the highest-attention frames, the lowest-attention frames, or are randomly selected. The results are shown in  Fig 5 . Fig 5 Attention Validation. Balanced accuracy as a function of the masked frames, for three masking strategies: highest-attention, lowest-attention, and random frames. Across all masking fractions, removing the highest-attention frames degraded performance the most, more than removing random or low-attention frames. At a masking fraction of 0.1, BAcc dropped from 0.939 to 0.907 when masking high-attention frames, compared to only 0.934 and 0.937 for random and low-attention masking, respectively. This gap widened as more frames were removed. At a fraction of 0.5, masking high-attention frames reduced BAcc to 0.836, whereas random and low-attention masking retained 0.912 and 0.931. Notably, masking low-attention frames consistently degraded performance the least, even less than random masking, indicating that the frames receiving the lowest attention were also the least informative for the prediction. Together, these result confirm that the attention weights are faithful to the model’s decision process. Frames identified as important based on the attention mechanism are those whose removal most strongly impairs prediction, justifying their use as a basis for the subsequent feature analysis. 3.2.2 Attention focus. To corroborate the findings based on sequence truncation and to identify what are the signals predictive of particular cell fate, we next performed attention-based explainability analysis. Following the procedure described in  Sect 2.3 , we identified the high-attention and low-attention frames  ℋ s  and  ℒ s  in all sequences  s ∈ S . In  Fig 6 , we aggregated attention weights across all sequences by aligning them to the final frame of each sequence. In sequences of dividing cells, the aggregated attention profile shows a pronounced peak at the final frame, indicating that the model’s predictions relied predominantly on information from the frames shortly prior to the event. The average normalized attention weight was markedly lower in the earlier frames, at 0.62 in the second-to-last frame and 0.22 at the 10th frame before the event. Attention weights decreased more markedly only beyond this range, reaching 0.07 at the 45th frame prior to the event. These findings indicate that, for apoptotic sequences, informative signals were distributed across a wider temporal context rather than being confined to the immediate vicinity of the final event. 3.2.3 Predictive features. Having identified frames important for prediction, we next investigated which morphological and intensity-based features distinguish high-attention frames from less informative ones. Feature quantification and statistical testing followed the procedure outlined in  Sect 2.3 . Not all cells were correctly segmented. The frames with missing segmentation were discarded from the feature analysis. We used permutation test and Cliff’s delta to identify significantly different features between the two groups of frames. We repeated this for all sequences and calculated the median Cliff’s delta and its 95% confidence interval to determine the direction of the feature change. The results are shown in  Fig 7 . Positive values indicate higher feature values in high-attention frames compared to low-attention frames, whereas negative values indicate the opposite. The background shading denotes commonly used effect size categories (negligible, small, medium, and large effects), facilitating interpretation of the magnitude and direction of the observed differences. For mitotic sequences, several features exhibited consistent differences between high- and low-attention frames. Most notably, the p53 channel showed a pronounced positive effect, indicating higher p53 intensity in frames that received high attention. Area-related features, including cell area and equivalent diameter, exhibited medium positive effects, while perimeter showed a smaller positive effect. Circularity displayed a small to medium positive effect, solidity a small positive effect, and eccentricity a medium negative effect. This pattern suggests that frames predictive of division were characterized by cells that appear larger and more rounded. The sequence depicts progressive cell growth followed by characteristic mitotic rounding in the frames immediately preceding division. Frames assigned high attention by the Transformer model are highlighted in green, whereas representative low-attention frames are marked in red. Attention weights were normalized across the sequence. For apoptotic sequences, small positive effects were observed for equivalent diameter area, cell area, perimeter, and circadian features, indicating modest but consistent differences between high- and low-attention frames across these properties. In contrast to mitosis, no pronounced difference between high- and low-attention frames were observed for circularity and eccentricity in apoptotic sequences. This suggests that while high-attention frames in apoptotic sequences were also characterized by larger cells, they were not necessarily round, but instead retain their original shape. Notably, p53 again exhibited a large positive Cliff’s delta median, highlighting elevated p53 intensity as a dominant characteristic of frames important for apoptosis prediction. Interestingly, high-attention frames were also characterized by a modest increase in the intensity of the circadian reporter channel. This is in line with previous studies establishing a relationship between circadian signals and apoptosis at the single-cell level [ 20 , 21 ]. An example of an apoptotic sequence is shown in  S8 Fig . Finally, for both mitotic and apoptotic sequences, no significant differences were observed in the cell-cycle channel expression, which marks the progression through the specific cell-cycle phases. This suggests that cell-cycle progression was not strongly associated with the features emphasized by the model in this analysis. To assess whether the aforementioned features depend on the experimental condition, we repeated the same analysis separately for the control condition and each of the three cisplatin doses ( S4 Fig ). Since untreated cells undergo apoptosis more rarely, the control condition contains only 41 apoptotic sequences (see  Table 1 ) and the corresponding estimates are very noisy. We therefore do not interpret the control apoptotic estimates and restrict the following observations to the cisplatin-treated conditions and, for mitosis, to the control condition. Across the three cisplatin doses, all features show the same trends of change in the high-attention frames. Several features additionally exhibit a consistent gradient with dose. For mitotic sequences, the size-related effects weaken with increasing dose (area, equivalent diameter, and perimeter), whereas the shape-related effects strengthen (circularity and eccentricity). The p53 effect increases slightly and remains the largest effect throughout. The high-attention signature of division shifts gradually from size-based toward shape-based cues as the dose increases, while its overall structure remains unchanged. For apoptotic sequences, the three treated doses show a comparable mild gradient, with p53, perimeter, area, and circadian effects decreasing slightly from low to high dose. 3.2.4 Feature trajectories. The preceding analyses summarize the attention distribution ( Sect 3.2.2 ) and the high- vs. low-attention feature differences within sequences ( Sect 3.2.3 ) as aggregate quantities. To complement these, we additionally present the full temporal trajectories of attention weights and of each feature, aligned to the cell event ( S5 Fig ). This trace-level view shows how each cell feature evolves over the cell’s lifetime and how these dynamics relate to the frames the model attends to for prediction. The fate-resolved trajectories are consistent with the effect-size analysis. For mitotic sequences, attention is sharply concentrated in the final frames, coinciding with a late increase in cell area and equivalent diameter, a rise in circularity, and a pronounced drop in eccentricity, together reflecting possible mitotic rounding. The p53 channel rises steeply toward the event, mirroring its large positive effect size. For apoptotic sequences, the trajectories change more gradually and over a broader temporal window. Area and perimeter increase while circularity decreases, consistent with cell stretching rather than rounding, and p53 intensity is elevated over an extended period preceding the event rather than only in the final frames. The cell-cycle channel remains comparatively flat over time for both fates. Comparing the two fates at matched positions relative to the cell event also reveals which features are unique for each of them. The p53 and cell-cycle reporters are markedly higher in mitotic than in apoptotic sequences throughout the observed window, whereas the circadian reporter reaches comparable levels in both fates near the event. The shape features separate the fates in the opposite direction, as apoptotic cells retain a more eccentric, less circular morphology, while mitotic cells become rounder towards division. We further examined whether these patterns depend on treatment or dose, complementing the condition-resolved effect-size analysis above with the corresponding trajectory view. While the analyses above are restricted to cisplatin-treated cells, here we additionally include the untreated control condition for comparison. The attention profiles and morphological features are largely consistent between control and cisplatin-treated cells (see  S6 Fig ), indicating that the shape-based cues underlying the model’s predictions are not specific to the treatment. The reporter channels, however, differ between conditions. Circadian intensity in high-attention frames is markedly higher under cisplatin than in control for both fates, and the cell-cycle reporter in apoptotic cells remains elevated in the control condition but is low in treated cells. These differences are consistent with cisplatin perturbing the underlying signaling dynamics, while leaving the morphological progression toward each fate largely unchanged. We note, that the control curves are considerably noisier because of the low number of apoptotic events (see  Table 1 ), and we therefore refrain from drawing strong quantitative conclusions from this comparison. Across the three cisplatin doses ( S7 Fig ), the attention and feature trajectories are largely indistinguishable, with only a minor reduction in circadian intensity at the lowest dose. 4 Discussion The ability to predict cell fate from microscopy videos transforms time-lapse imaging from a descriptive tool into a quantitative framework for identifying early determinants of drug response, stratifying heterogeneous populations, and guiding mechanistically informed therapeutic strategies. Here we present our computational approach for cell fate prediction from raw video data of cell trajectories. Our Transformer model exploits not only static visual information but also its temporal dynamics in the prediction. Together with the model we design a thorough explainability pipeline pointing to the video frames where the predictive information is located and to the statistically significant properties of cells in these video frames. Our first finding is that cell fate can be reliably predicted even  ~ 8  h prior to the cell’s death or division. This prediction is performed on raw video data that encodes cell morphology, presence of three dynamically expressed molecular markers and their change in time. The fact that such input allows us for cell fate prediction with high accuracy suggests that this input provides the right information for this task. Importantly, the model does not depend exclusively on the final pre-event frame but integrates signals from preceding frames throughout the sequence. Secondly, through a systematic explainability analysis of the model, we point to the video frames and cellular features that are exploited by the model in prediction. By combining aggregated attention distributions, temporal truncation analyses, and attention-guided feature comparisons, we observed systematic differences in how the model processed mitotic and apoptotic sequences. In the following, we discuss these observations in relation to cell fate dynamics and model explainability. 4.1 Attention focus Differences in the temporal distribution of attention weights suggest that the model relied on distinct temporal cues when predicting mitotic versus apoptotic outcomes. In mitotic sequences, attention was almost exclusively concentrated towards the end of the sequence. This indicates that the model primarily leveraged late-stage visual cues to identify mitosis, potentially corresponding to abrupt and short-lasting changes in cell morphology or changes in expression of the imaged proteins that occurred just before division. These changes might involve rounding of the cell and fluctuation of nuclear fluorescence signals during rounding and after nuclear envelope breakdown [ 22 – 24 ]. This is also supported by our results shown in  Fig 3d , that indicate that the model already achieved a high recall in mitosis classification based on the last 10 frames only (recall: 0.933). In contrast, apoptotic sequences exhibited a broader distribution of attention over time. Our analysis suggests that apoptotic cell death is preceded by cellular processes that emerge substantially earlier in time. Rather than concentrated near the end of the sequence, attention was distributed across a wider temporal range, with many frames showing high relevance well before cell death. We hypothesize that predictive information for apoptosis emerges gradually, such that early morphological or intensity changes already contribute to the model’s decision. This interpretation is further supported by the truncation experiment in  Fig 3d , which showed that restricting the model to only the final frames was insufficient to achieve strong performance for apoptotic sequences. 4.2 Attention-guided morphological features Using our explainability analysis we point to visual features that allow the model to make a correct prediction. Our systematic, statistical analysis has pointed to mostly morphological features as well as p53 expression that are indicative of the cell outcome. Our method applied to more comprehensive cancer cell imaging data can allow to identify early determinants of drug resistance and sensitivity. Based on the identified features resistant cells can be isolated and used to guide prospective intervention — e.g., by applying secondary drugs, or perturbing signaling pathways at time points when cells are most vulnerable. Such cells can be additionally characterized using, e.g., single cell transcriptomics in the particular time points when their fate is determined. The features identified here could be validated causally by ablating them individually from a features-only model and testing whether performance changes match our importance rankings. Mitosis  For cells undergoing mitosis, high-attention frames contained cells with increased size, larger area, equivalent diameter, and perimeter measurements. This growth was accompanied by a decrease in eccentricity together with an increased circularity, indicating that cells progressively expanded, becoming larger and rounder. This transition from elongated to more circular shapes is consistent with known mitotic morphology, where cells undergo rounding prior to division, and aligns with previous observations in live-cell imaging studies ([ 23 , 24 ]). To predict mitosis, therefore, the Transformer primarily relies on morphological cues associated with cell rounding that occur shortly before cell division. Interestingly, intensity changes in p53 were also highlighted by the model as predictive of mitosis. p53 is frequently located in centrosomes during cell division and acts as a sensor for the mitotic surveillance pathway [ 25 ]. Furthermore, p53 plays a role in the detection of mitotic extension, preventing daughter cells resulting from abnormally long mitosis from progressing further in the cell cycle [ 26 ]. Among the myriad of functions p53 has, it is plausible that high-attention patterns in mitotic sequences relate to the role of p53 in genomic integrity. Increasing evidence also suggests that p53 signaling dynamics, such as signal duration, amplitude, or accumulation rate, encode biologically meaningful information that influences cellular outcomes [ 27 ]. Cells that resume proliferation after DNA damage do this via escape from p53-mediated arrest through characteristic transient changes in p53 activity shortly before division [ 28 ]. These findings underline the advantage of our approach, by processing the entire video sequence, our model captures not only snapshots of p53 activity but its dynamics across the cell cycle. The cell-cycle channel, by contrast, contributed little to identifying mitosis, which may reflect the reporter itself. CFP-hGeminin is expressed throughout S/G2/M rather than being mitosis-specific, so it remains at near-constant levels over much of the cycle. As the last analyzed frame precedes division, and the reporter signal drops only during the metaphase-to-anaphase transition once the APC complex becomes active, the characteristic cell-cycle change associated with mitosis falls outside our analysis window. Apoptosis  In contrast to mitosis, the attention patterns associated with apoptotic outcomes were more broadly distributed in time. Rather than pinpointing a consistent temporal ordering of predictive events, attention in apoptotic sequences suggests the presence of informative frames across the cell’s lifespan. Frames receiving high attention were characterized by increased cell size relative to low-attention frames within the same sequence. In contrast to mitosis, however, this increase in size was not necessarily confined to the final frames preceding the event and involved a decrease in cell circularity. These morphological changes involve cell stretching rather than rounding and can occur earlier in a cell’s trajectory. In addition, high-attention frames in apoptotic sequences exhibited elevated p53 intensity, highlighting p53-related signals as an important contributor to the model’s predictions. It is well established that following DNA damage, p53 signaling is activated and plays a central role in coordinating cell fate decisions, including cell-cycle arrest, DNA repair, and the initiation of apoptosis [ 29 – 32 ]. Sustained p53 activation can occur several hours before an apoptotic event [ 33 , 34 ]. We therefore speculate that prolonged p53-related intensity changes in apoptotic sequences may constitute one of the early cues captured by the model and reflected in the broader temporal distribution of attention. In summary, by using truncation experiments and explainability analysis we quantify how predictive information accumulates over time with longer observation window. The ability to predict cell death early in a cell’s lifetime reflects the extent to which this fate is pre-determined versus progressively acquired. Our approach enables identification of critical temporal windows during which fate becomes predictable. 5 Conclusion The combination of Transformer models with attention-based interpretability provides a powerful framework for the analysis of complex video data. By operating on full temporal sequences and leveraging the attention mechanism, the proposed framework is particularly well suited to capturing dynamic cellular processes and temporally evolving molecular signals present in time-lapse microscopy data. Our results demonstrate that this approach not only enables prediction of biological phenomena but also allows the extraction and interpretation of the signals underlying these predictions. Particularly in biological systems that remain incompletely understood, the methodology presented here offers a valuable strategy for identifying informative patterns and gaining insight into the mechanistic determinants that drive specific phenotypic outcomes. For example, cells in densely populated area are more likely to undergo apoptosis/mitosis. - Are distributions of sequence lengths different between mitosis and apoptosis? For example, does high p53 mean higher chance of apoptosis? - Since each sequence contains longitudinal data points, it is expected that frame t might contain lots of similar information as frame (t-1) and (t+1). The strategy of using attention weights as feature importance might not be able to natively capture this longitudinal continuity, may authors comment on their choice here? - It is also widely recognized that attention weights could be arbitrary. In addition to this attention-based importance scores, would there be alternative measures to get frame importance? A naive idea would be to mask certain frames (just like what authors did in Fig. 3) and see how such masking lead to changes in predictions. - Since the key topic of this manuscript is about cell fate, I think a key type of discovery is some kind of trigger events detected by the sequence transformer that heavily affect the predictions of cell fate. Authors have only discussed these qualitatively ("rounding of the cell and fluctuation of nuclear fluorescence signals"), but I would recommend extending the masking experiments in Fig. 3 to further reveal this type of information. For example, given a sequence, can we show the curve of predicted mitosis/apoptosis along the sequence, where each point's prediction is only based on the history observed until this point. A sharp change in this curve could be indicative of a trigger event. - Would there be any differences between control / cisplatin-treated cases in terms of their interpretability results? Given the effect of cisplatin as a chemotherapy reagent to trigger apoptosis, it can be imagined that the important frames might show different distributions as compared to control cases. Discussion on such differences is missing. - In practice, it would be nearly impossible to get continuous measurements out of a real cancer patient's tumor. In cases where only snapshots can be acquired (high-dimensional spatial omics, for example), may authors discuss how this proposed modeling approach/concept can be applied or adapted? Minor: - Fig. 4 is a bit hard to read, maybe use a scatter/line plot instead. - Line 170-174: "Across all sequences, this results in n p-values and n corresponding Cliff’s delta values ..." What is `n` here? Do you mean "across ONE sequence" or actually across all sequences of the dataset? I find this paragraph a bit hard to understand. Reviewer #2: Overall assessment: Bürger et al., has developed a Transformer-based model that predicts single-cell fate directly from raw time-lapse microscopy sequences of cisplatin-treated cancer cells, reporting a balanced accuracy of 0.94. They introduce an explainability pipeline that finds high attentionframes and by using a Cliff's-delta comparison they investigate which morphological or intensity-based features leads to the high attention frames. Here they find that the p53 channel showed a pronounced positive effect. In my opinion, the problem is well-motivated and investigates a very deep question. The main results and conclusions are very interesting though vital aspects remain unclear. The dataset applied for this analysis is small compared to normal ML approaches (which is not a bad thing) but good enough to draw conclusions. The writing of the paper is very direct (which again is not a bad thing) for the most part clear. Therefore I do see that this work can be published in Plos comp. Biol, however I have however a series of questions and points where I believe the authors do not support their main conclusions and to publish this paper would therefore require a major revision. General problem: The authors report very profound results but I believe the evidence of these are often thin. First of all, the two central claims of the paper: 10 h predictability and importance of p53 channel is not supported directly by a figure. I really need the authors to make this more clear. Major points: A) Figures: I would very much like more figures showing the dynamical traces of many of these observables - in particular p53 and circadian information. Also I need figures (histograms at least) that outline what makes the high attention weights dominating (see my point E on this also). Also, there should be a qualitative apoptotic example analogous to mitosis in Fig. 6. Furthermore, Fig. 4 is really hard to read quantitatively; the numbers quoted in text (0.62, 0.22, 0.88, 0.42) deserve a line plot with confidence bands, not a grayscale strip. B) Regarding the 10 h claim, then I cannot trace it to a specific quantitative result. In Fig 3a, the authors show BAcc stays above approximately 0.9 up to 20 frames removed (which means 10 h) - but this definition should be more clearly stated. Also, this is overall BAcc on a set dominated by mitosis, and Fig. 3c shows mitotic recall already drops at 5 frames. I therefore understand this as the 10 h statement holds mainly for apoptosis, not as a general claim? Please describe this result in greater detail and state the claim per-class, define it against a stated baseline, and mark the relevant threshold explicitly on Fig. 3. C) I find the p53 claim very interesting but was disappointed with the (lack of) evidence revealed in the paper. In the discussion, the authors discuss how: “the distinct temporal attention profiles for mitotic versus apoptotic sequences mirror the distinct p53 dynamical states that mechanistic studies have identified as fate-determining”, citing work of the Lahav lab. However, the authors show no traces of neither p53 or circadian information and in the main text they write that high-attention frames have elevated p53 intensity on average. There is no analysis of p53 dynamics despite the authors explicitly invoking these as the mechanism. I therefore see two options: either add an analysis that directly tests a dynamical hypothesis based on p53 trajectories and ask whether a dynamical feature (pulse count, slope before event) separates fates and aligns with high-attention frames or substantially downtune the claims of mechanisms and explainability claims throughout the text. As written, the interpretation is largely overstated compared to the evidence revealed. D) A main claim of the paper is that temporal attention matters and to validate this the authors should perform a non-temporal baseline. In particular Fig. 3d shows mitosis can be estimated approximately 0.94 recall from the last 5 frames alone - this could suggest that a static last-frame classifier might already do well for mitosis? This needs to be thoroughly discussed. E) The second half of the paper (Sec. 2.2 and the p53/circadian/morphology interpretation in the Discussion) rests on a critical assumption: that the frames receiving high attention weights are the frames the model actually relies on for its prediction. I am no expert in the current developments in ML, but it is my impression that based on papers by (Serrano, S., & Smith, N. A. (2019). Is attention interpretable? and Jain, S., & Wallace, B. C. (2019). Attention is not explanation - and counter argument Wiegreffe, S., & Pinter, Y. (2019). Attention is not not explanation) it seems to me that this could be validated even further. My simple suggestion for the authors is to show a figure where you take the correctly classified sequences and measure how much prediction degrades when you remove frames, comparing three conditions: 1) masking the high-attention frames, 2) masking the same number of randomly chosen frames and 3) masking the same number of low-attention frames. If attention is faithful, masking H_s should hurt prediction substantially more than masking random or low-attention frames. If the three are comparable, the attention weights are not tracking importance. In my opinion this would strengthen the central claim of this paper. Minor points: a) The reported embedding dimension is "2024-dimensional" (line 129). Do you mean 2048 for ResNet-50? b) BAcc on the cisplatin-only test set is 0.958 (line 243) vs. 0.94 on the full test set (line 215). Please clarify which is the main number and why they differ. c) Cisplatin was given at three dose levels (line 101). Are temporal/attention patterns dose-dependent? Pooling across doses may mask heterogeneity relevant to the overall fate claim. At minimum, report the dose composition of the apoptotic vs. mitotic sets. Reviewer #3: In this paper, the authors analyze an existing live-cell imaging dataset obtained upon treatment with cisplatin, a common chemotherapeutic agent, in which treated cells, followed for hours, were annotated as mitotic or apoptotic. In this dataset, nuclei, p53 levels, cell-cycle progression, and circadian progression can be monitored through the use of appropriate reporters. By applying a transformer model, the authors are able to accurately classify mitotic and apoptotic cells. Furthermore, attention and masking allow them to identify the variables that contribute most to the classification, with the potential to provide insights into the biological determinants of cell fate decisions. Using machine-learning methods able to extract features and potentially biological insights into what determines cellular outcomes from live-cell imaging data can certainly be very useful. At the same time, a careful evaluation of the precise advantages and disadvantages of each methodology, and of the extent to which the systems used can really provide biological insight, is necessary. At this stage, this paper does not address these two important issues in a convincing way. To do so, several points must be addressed so that the contribution and value of the present work become evident. 1 The authors declare that their dataset comes from a system developed by some of them called “TransientTrack” ( https://arxiv.org/html/2512.01885v1 ). Inspecting this work, it is evident that it is a machine-learning-based system that is also able to track and classify cells. At this point, it is not clear to me whether the annotations used in the present study were generated with this method. Furthermore, it would be important to understand how the classification performed here compares to that performed by TransientTrack (which is only possible if both are assessed against an independent manually annotated dataset). More details on how the annotations were generated would be necessary to properly assess the biological potential of the method and to ensure that there are no experimental biases. 2. A method able to automatically classify cells undergoing apoptosis, mitosis, or neither of these two outcomes would be of great interest, given the growing availability of live-cell imaging experiments and datasets. It would therefore have been worthwhile to test whether the proposed method was able to classify cells into these three classes: apoptotic, mitotic, or neither. Such a classification should also be compared against TransientTrack. 3. A major hurdle in convincing the reader of the power of the method is that it is validated on only one experimental condition: cells treated with cisplatin. Applying it at least to untreated versus treated cells would have provided more convincing evidence of both the validity of the method and the biological insights it can provide. For example, some evidence points to a role of p53 accumulation in cell death (PMID: 27062928) and to p53 bursts during the cell cycle (PMID: 20598361). I would expect these behaviors to change and produce different patterns in treated cells compared to untreated cells. This aspect is unfortunately unexplored. 4. The classification results shown in Figure 4 are striking and deserve further comment. From the results, it appears that cells treated with cisplatin are predicted to be apoptotic very accurately even when the last 50 frames are removed. This roughly corresponds to 25 hours, which is typically the time required for cells to die upon cisplatin treatment according to PMID: 27062928. A point that the authors need to clarify is the exact moment at which a track is stopped because the cell is considered dead (since dead cells can also be tracked). If the tracks are interrupted at the moment when a cell can be visually declared dead, then it raises the question of which cells are actually being considered: perhaps only the fraction of cells that have not yet died but are about to die, making them particularly distinguishable. This might also affect the interpretation of the attention results in the case of apoptosis, where attention appears broadly distributed but, surprisingly (given that discarding 50 frames does not substantially affect classification), tends to have very small values during the last 50 frames. To me, this appears somewhat contradictory and, together with the methodological questions raised above, should be clarified by the authors. I think that using cells with tracks that are comparable (similar starting and ending points, etc.) would provide more clarity. 5. A clarification of when the cells are declared as dividing would also be important: there the error of keeping on tracking a cell that is not alive is not present, so the risk of bias is smaller; however, the selection of the endpoint might affect the changes in p53 levels (since nuclear rupture would typically be preceded by a spike in nuclear accumulation). 6. From a biological point of view, there are two aspects that require additional consideration. First, I found it very surprising that mitosis is identified with almost no relation to cell-cycle progression. Could this be related to the fact that, upon chemotherapy, the cell-cycle machinery is perturbed and this might interfere with the reporter? The authors also claim a role for circadian progression, although the circadian signal does not appear to be strongly enriched in high-attention frames according to Figure 5. 7. As an overall comment, the graphical style adopted by the authors is quite parsimonious. To improve the readability of the manuscript, a larger number of illustrative panels would be beneficial. In particular, illustrating the results with representative frames from time-lapse videos would make the findings much more convincing. **********
