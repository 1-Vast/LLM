To explore common markers for the less differentiated cancer cells across the dataset, we ranked genes on the basis of their correlation with CytoTRACE.  KRT14  was identified as one of the top three genes associated with less differentiated cells, along with other basal genes, such as  KRT16  and  POSTN , ranking at the top of the list ( Fig. 1C  and table S1). While  KRT14  is typically considered a marker of basal cells, single-cell transcriptomic analyses of the normal human breast have identified an intermediate cell population that coexpresses  KRT14  and luminal keratins ( 27 ,  28 ). This intermediate cell state lacks canonical myocontractile genes associated with mature myoepithelial cells ( 29 ), a feature indicative of a plastic developmental state ( 27 ,  28 ). Here in breast cancer, the  KRT14 +  cell population also exhibited markedly low levels of myocontractile genes (fig. S1B), indicating an immature basal-like cell state distinct from the differentiated phenotype. Upon closer examination of the  KRT14 +  populations, we observed that there were subsets of cells with low CytoTRACE scores (fig. S1C). To further investigate the more immature subset of  KRT14 +  cells, we paired each of the top 1000 genes identified by CytoTRACE with  KRT14  and examined the mean CytoTRACE scores associated with each gene and gene pair. This analysis yielded a list of candidate genes that not only predict less differentiated cells independently in the whole dataset, but also mark subsets of  KRT14 +  cells in the more immature cell state (fig. S1D).  BCL11B , a marker gene for a minority stem cell population that we previously identified in the mouse mammary epithelium ( 19 ), emerged as one of the top 30 candidate genes (fig. S1D). Many of the other top candidate genes have been previously linked to the increased tumorigenic ability and invasiveness in breast cancer, such as  PLA2G7 ,  MSLN ,  MCAM , and  CREB5  (fig. S1D) ( 30 – 33 ), validating the effectiveness of our approach.
HER2 has been identified as an important biomarker and treatment target for a subset of aggressive breast cancers, resulting in the development of highly successful targeted therapy ( 35 ). This prompted us to investigate the therapeutic significance of targeting BCL11B in addressing chemoresistance. We first experimentally examined whether the BCL11B-expressing cancer cells have the ability to resist chemotherapeutic drugs. To this end, we used the widely used MMTV-PyMT (PyMT) transgenic mouse model, which has been shown to recapitulate many aspects of human breast cancer ( 36 ). Despite being regarded as a luminal type of breast cancer, the primary PyMT tumor harbored a specific subset of KRT14 +  basal-like cells, predominantly located at the tumor-stromal border and surrounding KRT8 +  luminal tumor cells ( Fig. 2A ). This finding aligns with observations reported in recent studies ( 37 ,  38 ). Notably, BCL11B +  cells were found to be a notable subset of the KRT14 +  population in the tumor ( Fig. 2, B and C ). These results showed that BCL11B marks a distinctive tumor subpopulation in the PyMT tumor model, exhibiting basal-like features and resembling human BCL11B +  cancer cells.

To investigate how  Bcl11b +  cells are primed to adopt a drug-resistant state, we first performed bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis on  Bcl11b +  and  Bcl11b −  cells in treatment-naïve tumors. As expected, basal genes such as  Col17a1 ,  Krt14 , and  Krt5  were highly expressed by  Bcl11b +  cells, while luminal markers were predominantly observed in  Bcl11b −  cells ( Fig. 3A  and table S2). However, many of the top enriched genes in  Bcl11b +  cells, such as  Dsc3 ,  Dsg3 , and  Tgfbi , have not been previously linked to mammary basal cells. Conversely, markers associated with a more mature myoepithelial phenotype, including  Actg2 ,  Myh11 ,  Myl9 , and  Mylk , were found to be expressed at low levels in  Bcl11b +  cells ( Fig. 3B ), consistent with our observations in human  BCL11B +  cells. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis further uncovered an enrichment of genes involved in xenobiotic metabolism and glutathione (GSH) metabolism in the treatment-naïve  Bcl11b +  cells, such as  Aldh3a1 ,  Cyp26a1 , and  Mgst2  ( Fig. 3, C and D ). Both pathways have been closely associated with detoxifying chemotherapeutic drugs in cancer ( 42 ,  43 ). Notably, one of the representative genes, the aldehyde dehydrogenase enzyme  Aldh3a1 , has been demonstrated in breast cancer to protect cells against various chemotherapeutic drugs including Doxo, paclitaxel, and cyclophosphamide ( 44 ,  45 ). By interrogating a recent single-cell transcriptome atlas spanning mouse mammary development stages ( 46 ), we also unexpectedly identified that  Bcl11b  and its associated gene program in cancer were specifically enriched in the rudimentary basal-like cells at the early postnatal stage (day 5) ( Fig. 3E  and fig. S5, A to C). Most of basal-like cells at this early postnatal stage lacked myoepithelial markers ( Fig. 3E ) and represented a precursor population in an intermediate developmental stage ( 14 ,  47 ). Many of the xenobiotic detoxification genes also showed a specific enrichment in this early basal cell state ( Fig. 3E  and fig. S5C). Therefore, these results suggest that  Bcl11b +  tumor cells mimic a precursor-like developmental state in cancer, which is inherently linked to a xenobiotic inactivation program that may play a physiological role in responding to external stimuli.
Given that the deficiency of  Bcl11b  compromised the drug resistance ability, we questioned whether  Bcl11b  is functionally required for maintaining the immature basal-like cell state and its associated drug resistance program. The loss of  Bcl11b  resulted in a substantial decrease in the number of KRT14 +  tumor cells, both in vitro and in vivo, while leaving the KRT8 +  luminal cell lineage unaffected ( Fig. 3, F and G , and fig. S5, D and E). The mRNA levels of  Krt14  and the drug resistance genes, including  Aldh3a1 ,  Cyp26a1 , and  Mgst2 , were also significantly down-regulated in tumor organoids after the KO of  Bcl11b  ( Fig. 3H ). Transcriptomic analysis of WT and  Bcl11b  KO tumor organoids further confirmed that both the basal gene program and drug resistance program were impaired upon the deletion of  Bcl11b  (fig. S5F and table S3). Moreover, we used a recently published BCL11B binding dataset generated in the same PyMT tumor model ( 48 ) and identified BCL11B binding at the  Krt14  promoter, as well as at the promoter regions of other downstream genes such as  Aldh3a1  and  Mgst2 , suggesting that these genes are direct downstream targets of BCL11B (fig. S5G). To validate these findings, we performed a CUT&RUN (cleavage under targets and release using nuclease) assay in our PyMT tumor organoids and confirmed BCL11B binding at the  Aldh3a1  promoter in control organoids, with loss of this binding upon  Bcl11b  deletion (fig. S5H). We also detected BCL11B binding at the promoter of  Snai2  (fig. S5G), a key regulator in the mammary epithelium that controls basal/stem cell maintenance.  Snai2  expression was also markedly down-regulated upon  Bcl11b  deletion (table S3), indicating that BCL11B may also regulate the basal-like cell state indirectly through  Snai2 . Together, these results suggest that  Bcl11b  functionally controls an immature basal-like cell state that is inherently linked to a preexisting drug detoxification gene program and exhibits selective advantages upon chemotherapy treatment.
The expression of the induced drug resistance gene program was predominantly dependent on  Bcl11b , as drug treatment was unable to induce most of these genes in the  Bcl11b- deficient tumor cells ( Fig. 4I  and table S4). Among the various drug resistance pathways present or activated in  Bcl11b +  cells, we specifically examined the drug detoxification program that involves both preexisting and adaptive mechanisms, such as the drug metabolism gene  Aldh3a1  and the drug transporter  Abcb1b . Consistent with our hypothesis, there was an obvious clearance of intracellular Doxo in BCL11B +  cells as opposed to BCL11B −  cells within 24 hours of administration (fig. S8F). The uptake of Doxo in the cells was similar in both populations, supporting a drug elimination mechanism (fig. S8G). Consistently, impairment of  Aldh3a1  or  Abcb1b  can significantly decrease the number of drug-resistant organoids following drug treatment (fig. S9, A to C). Moreover, the intracellular elimination of Doxo by BCL11B +  cells was effectively blocked when either  Aldh3a1  or  Abcb1b  was inhibited (fig. S9, D and E). Although we did not detect BCL11B binding at the  Abcb1b  promoter, suggesting that  Abcb1b  is an indirect target,  Abcb1b  is strongly induced in  Bcl11b +  cells upon treatment (fig. S8C). These results indicate that the two genes are likely involved in the same drug detoxification pathway in  Bcl11b +  cells. This is in line with the general concept that xenobiotics can undergo chemical modification and conjugation through phase 1/2 drug-metabolizing enzymes (e.g.,  Aldh3a1 ) to increase their water solubility, thus facilitating their excretion through phase 3 transporters (e.g.,  Abcb1b ) ( 64 ,  65 ). Collectively, these results suggest that  Bcl11b  controls multiple preexisting and adaptive drug resistance pathways, including a drug detoxification mechanism, to offer a comprehensive defense against chemotherapy.
Mouse primary mammary tumors were obtained from 12- to 16-week-old virgin female MMTV-PyMT mice. Mouse or human xenograft samples were obtained from NSG recipient mice that had been transplanted with tumor cells. For human samples, informed consent was obtained, and human normal breast and breast tumor specimens were collected according to guidelines from Stanford University’s Institutional Review Board (4344). Tumor samples were dissected and processed according to the published protocol ( 77 ), with minor modifications. Briefly, samples were mechanically dissociated into small pieces (1 to 2 mm 3 ) with a razor blade and then digested at 37°C with 1500 U collagenase and 500 U hyaluronidase (Stem Cell Technology) in Advanced DMEM/F-12 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with 2% FBS (Hyclone) and 1% PSA (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 3 hours with hourly pipetting. After digestion, the reaction was neutralized by adding FACS staining buffer (Hanks’ balanced salt solution + 2% FBS + 1% PSA) and spun down at 1500 rpm for 5 min. The pellet was then treated with 5 ml of ACK lysis buffer (Lonza) on ice for 5 min to deplete red blood cells. The sample was spun down, and the cells were further digested in 4 ml of 0.25% trypsin-EDTA (Gibco) supplemented with 1000 U deoxyribonuclease I for 10 min at 37°C. Dissociated cells were filtered by a 40-μm strainer and washed with FACS staining buffer. For FACS analysis and sorting, single cells were stained with CD45 (BioLegend), CD31 (BioLegend), Ter119 (BioLegend), CD49f (BioLegend), EpCAM (BioLegend), and CD24 (BioLegend) with appropriate conjugated fluorophores for 20 min on ice. The cells were then washed and resuspended in FACS staining buffer containing DAPI (1 μg/ml). Stained cells were analyzed and sorted on a FACSAria II (BD Bioscience) instrument with a 100-μm nozzle. A complete list of antibodies is provided in table S5.
Dissociated single cells from tumors were resuspended in 100 μl of media (organoid culture media + 50% of Matrigel) and injected into the nipple of female NSG mice at the fourth mammary fat pad. For lentivirus-mediated  BCL11B  knockdown transplant assay, dissociated xenograft cells were transduced overnight in organoid culture media with 20 multiplicities of infection of lentivirus. The next day, PDX cells were transferred to Matrigel and cultured in organoid media + Neuregulin 1 (5 nM; PeproTech) + A83-01 (500 nM; Tocris) + SB202190 (500 nM; Sigma-Aldrich). Seventy-two hours after transduction, organoids were selected with puromycin (1 μg/ml). Transduced organoid cells were grown in the selection media for 4 to 5 days and then transplanted to NSG recipient mice, as described above. A summary of the PDX models used in this study is described in table S6.

Downstream qPCR analysis was subsequently performed according to the kit protocol using primers targeting the  Aldh3a1  promoter region (table S7).
Normal mammary tissues or tumor samples were fixed in 4% formaldehyde solution and subjected to either paraffin or optimal cutting temperature compound embedding (Tissue-Tek). Paraffin sections were deparaffinized, dehydrated, and microwaved for 15 min at 95°C in sodium citrate buffer (10 mM sodium citrate and 0.05% Tween 20, pH 6.0) for antigen retrieval. Frozen sections were first hydrated with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) for 10 min at room temperature and then microwaved for 15 min at 95°C in sodium citrate buffer for antigen retrieval. Tissue sections were blocked with staining buffer (PBS + 5% bovine serum albumin + 0.1% Triton X-100) for 1 hour at room temperature and then incubated overnight at 4°C with the primary antibodies (table S5) diluted in the staining buffer. Samples were subsequently washed with PBS and incubated with conjugated secondary antibodies (Invitrogen) at 1:300 in staining buffer + DAPI (1 μg/ml) for 1 hour at room temperature (antibodies are listed in table S5). After PBS washing, tissue sections were mounted with Fluoromount Aqueous Mounting Medium (Sigma-Aldrich). Images were acquired by a Carl Zeiss LSM 710 Meta confocal microscope or a Keyence fluorescence microscope.
Cells were lysed in ice-cold radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer containing 1× Halt Protease and Phosphatase inhibitor cocktails (Thermo Fisher Scientific), followed by addition of Laemmli Sample Buffer (Bio-Rad) and boiling on a heat block at 100°C for 10 min. Samples were loaded on a 4 to 20% gradient SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis gel (Bio-Rad), run at 100 V for 90 min, and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride membranes. Membranes were blocked with tris-buffered saline with Tween 20 + 5% nonfat dry milk for 1 hour at room temperature and then incubated with primary antibodies (table S5) at 4°C overnight. Membranes were subsequently washed with tris-buffered saline with Tween 20 and stained with horseradish peroxidase–conjugated secondary antibodies. Membranes were then washed and visualized using the SuperSignal West Dura Extended Duration Substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific).
