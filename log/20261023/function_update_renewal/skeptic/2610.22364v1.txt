 Abstract 
    
 Predicting how the world responds to an action requires more than what can be seen: robots must account for environmental factors that are neither directly observable nor explicitly specified, from physical properties such as friction and mass to human-defined environmental rules.
We introduce  T2-z  for test-time optimization of environment latents, which augments a robotic world model with a learnable environment latent to condition world predictions. This compact latent captures underlying environmental variation without supervision of what environmental factors exist or what values they take.
During training,  T2-z  alternates between two stages: a world-model optimization stage that fixes the latents and learns general action-conditioned dynamics across environments; and an environment-latent optimization stage that freezes the world model and optimizes the conditioning latents, allowing latent structure to emerge purely through world prediction.
At test time, the world model remains frozen and a newly initialized environment latent is optimized from one or more test-time interactions, adapting the model to previously unseen environmental configurations.
Across 7 simulated settings and 4 real-world robotic tasks,  T2-z  reduces object prediction error metric from  15.20 15.20  to  7.00 7.00  and raises action success from  33.73 % 33.73\%  to  72.96 % 72.96\% .
Its emergent latents capture structured
representations of individual and compounded factors across diverse visual scenes.
Ablations further show that  T2-z  can efficiently infer environment latents from as little as a single test-time interaction trajectory.

 1  Introduction 

 
 What a robot observes is insufficient to determine how the world will respond. Beneath visually similar scene configurations reside hidden environmental factors, such as friction, mass distribution, and unobserved human-defined interaction rules, that can fundamentally alter the consequences of the same action ( fig.   1  (a)). Such factors are often revealed only through interaction with the target environments, as their influence becomes observable through the outcomes they induce. Explicitly representing these factors  ( Kumar et al., 2021 ) , however, requires specifying in advance what factors may vary, their exact values, and how they should be parameterized, which is difficult to scale to unknown or compounded environmental variation. On the contrary, humans reason through “analysis by synthesis”   ( Yuille and Kersten, 2006 ) , using internal generative models not only to anticipate future outcomes, but also in reverse to infer latent structure that best accounts for what they observe. This raises a natural question: can hidden environmental factors be similarly captured in a latent representation that is directly optimized from test-time interactions? 
 
 
 We introduce  T2-z  for test-time optimization of environmental latents, which augments an action-conditioned robotic world model with a learnable environment latent  𝐳 e \mathbf{z}_{e} .  𝐳 e \mathbf{z}_{e}  is a randomly-initialized compact representation that is expected to encode the unobserved factors governing how an environment responds to interaction, with no supervision about what environmental factors it should represent.
The world model predicts future visual observations conditioned jointly on the action and  𝐳 e \mathbf{z}_{e} . We iteratively optimize the world model and  𝐳 e \mathbf{z}_{e}  in an alternating two-stage procedure: 1) world-model optimization: with  𝐳 e \mathbf{z}_{e}  fixed, we train the world model across all trajectories to learn shared action-conditioned dynamics across environments; 2) environment-latent optimization: with the world model frozen, we optimize the conditioned latent  𝐳 e \mathbf{z}_{e}  through prediction error, so that it captures underlying environment-specific variation not explained by the general world model alone. Both stages are optimized with the same flow-matching loss and alternated for multiple rounds until convergence, allowing the environment representation to emerge directly through prediction without predefined physical-factor supervision or a separate environment encoder. 
 
 
 At test time, the world model is frozen and  T2-z  turns environment inference into a direct
optimization problem. For a previously unseen environmental configuration,
we initialize a new  𝐳 e \mathbf{z}_{e}  and optimize  𝐳 e \mathbf{z}_{e}  to explain the outcomes of one or more observed test-time interactions.
Specifically, we update  𝐳 e \mathbf{z}_{e}  for multiple optimization steps through the same flow-matching objective used
during stage-two training, minimizing prediction error on the observed interactions. Because inference is optimization-based rather than amortized through a learned history encoder,  T2-z  is not tied to the trajectory count used for training and can flexibly incorporate any number of test-time trajectories.
Once optimized, the latent turns the frozen world model into an
environment-adapted test-time simulator, enabling prediction and comparison
of candidate action outcomes before execution. 
 
 
 We evaluate  T2-z  across 7 simulated settings and 4 real-world robotic tasks. Quantitatively and qualitatively,  T2-z  improves world-model prediction across visual quality, physical prediction metrics ( e.g. , relevant object locations), and downstream action selection.  T2-z  reduces physical prediction error from  12.86 12.86  to  4.80 4.80  in simulation and tracked-state error from  19.30 19.30  to  10.85 10.85  pixels in the real world, while improving action success from  37.9 % 37.9\%  to  74.2 % 74.2\%  and from  26.5 % 26.5\%  to  70.8 % 70.8\% , respectively. PCA of the learned latents reveals structured representations that reflect the underlying environmental factors, including a multi-dimensional organization when more than one factors vary jointly, despite the model never knowing how many factors are present or what they represent ( fig.   1 (b)). In our multi-background setting, a unified latent structure emerges across diverse scene setups, revealing that the learned representation captures environmental dynamics without becoming entangled with scene appearance ( fig.   1 (d)). Ablations further show that  T2-z  can infer useful environment representations from as few as a single test-time interaction trajectory. 
 
 
 
 2  Related Works 

 
 Figure 2:  Comparison with existing approaches. 
 (a)  Vanilla world models do not explicitly represent unobserved
environmental variation, producing the same prediction under different
environmental conditions.
 (b)  System-identification approaches such as RMA learn environment
representations from predefined factors and their ground-truth values, then
infer them from interaction history through a learned encoder.
 (c)   T2-z  requires neither predefined environmental factors nor a
learned environment encoder, instead directly optimizing an emergent
environment latent from test-time interaction through prediction error.
 
 
 
 Two distinct strands of work are particularly related to our problem. 
 
 
 Robotic world models  have made rapid progress in predicting
action-conditioned futures  ( Zhou et al., 2025 ;  Zhou et al., 2024 ;  Zhu et al., 2024 ;  Zhen et al., 2025 ;  Zhu et al., 2025 ;  Ye et al., 2026 ;  Huang et al., 2026 ;  Gao et al., 2026 ) . More recent work explores test-time
adaptation to improve long-horizon memory or incorporate new
behavioral supervision  ( Wan et al., 2026 ;  Wang et al., 2026 ;  Feng et al., 2026 ;  Yoo et al., 2025 ;  Jang et al., 2026 ) . These approaches predict future outcomes from visual observations alone, without accounting for underlying environmental variation that is not directly observable. ( fig.   2  (a)).
In contrast,  T2-z 
disentangles unobserved environmental variation from the world model’s
observable scene representation, through environment latent conditioning. 
 
 
 Environment representations and system identification  have
demonstrated the value of environment-specific representations for system
identification and rapid behavioral adaptation
  ( Yu et al., 2017 ;  Nagabandi et al., 2019 ;  Rakelly et al., 2019 ;  Lee et al., 2020 ;  Kumar et al., 2021 ;  Miki et al., 2022 ;  Nahrendra et al., 2023 ;  Wang et al., 2023 ;  He et al., 2024 ;  Luo et al., 2024 ) . The Rapid Motor Adaptation (RMA) family and related
methods typically rely on predefined privileged parameters
  ( Kumar et al., 2021 ;  Miki et al., 2022 ;  Nahrendra et al., 2023 ;  He et al., 2024 ;  Luo et al., 2024 ) . For example, RMA restricts
adaptation to 5 factors and uses their ground-truth
values to learn an embedding that an adaptation encoder later
recovers from interaction history  ( Kumar et al., 2021 ) . This prespecifies the
environment representation and ties inference to a learned
history-to-embedding mapping, limiting flexibility under unknown, compounded,
or out-of-distribution variation ( fig.   2  (b)). These methods also generally rely on a learned history encoder  ( Chen et al., 2024 ;  Xing et al., 2026 ) ,  limiting generalization when the amount of test-time interaction differs from that seen during training . In contrast,  T2-z  learns representations of environmental factors
implicitly through world prediction error, without predefined ground-truth physical parameters or a learned
history encoder. 
 
 
 Our method is also related to the literature
on  Test-Time Adaptation  (TTA), which
has enabled robots
to improve after deployment by adapting policy, prompts, or memory rather than optimizing a dedicated
environment representation. Existing approaches include test-time adaptation of
diffusion and flow policies  ( Li et al., 2025 ;  Li et al., 2026 ) ,
online policy adaptation  ( Liu et al., 2026 ;  Jin and Zhang, 2026 ;  Zhu et al., 2026 ) , and adaptation through latent prompts, fast weights,
memory, or retrieval  ( Zhang et al., 2026 ;  Jiang et al., 2026 ;  Feng et al., 2026 ;  Zhao et al., 2026 ;  Park et al., 2026 ) . TTA has also been
applied directly to predictive models through video replanning, world-model
training, and closed-loop latent adaptation  ( Ko et al., 2025 ;  Wan et al., 2026 ;  Wang et al., 2026 ) . 
 
 
 
 3  Method 

 
 Figure 3:  T2-z  framework. 
During training,  T2-z  alternates between two stages:
(1) world-model optimization, which learns shared action-conditioned
dynamics with environment latents fixed, and
(2) environment-latent optimization, which optimizes the latents
through prediction error with the world model frozen.
(3) At test time, the world model remains frozen while a new
environment latent is directly optimized from interaction and then reused
to predict the outcomes of different actions.
 
 
 
 We introduce  T2-z  ( fig.   2  &  algorithm   1 ), a framework for adapting robotic world models to hidden environmental variation through direct optimization of an environment latent. Our key idea is to separate the general visual predictive capability of a world model from unobserved environment-specific information. During training, we learn these two components through alternating optimization under a shared flow-matching objective. At test time, we freeze the world model and optimize only a newly initialized
environment latent from observed test-time interaction trajectories. The optimized latent subsequently conditions the frozen world model to predict the outcomes of diverse actions. 
 
 
 3.1  Problem Setup 

 
 We consider a robotic world model that predicts future visual observations  𝐲 \mathbf{y}  from the observation  𝐱 \mathbf{x}  and action  𝐚 \mathbf{a} .
We augment the world model with an environment latent  𝐳 e \mathbf{z}_{e}  and formulate prediction as: 
 

 
 
 p θ ​ ( 𝐲 ∣ 𝐱 , 𝐚 , 𝐳 e ) , p_{\theta}\left(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x},\mathbf{a},\mathbf{z}_{e}\right), 
 
 (1) 
 
 where  θ \theta  denotes the shared world-model parameters and  𝐳 e \mathbf{z}_{e}  is a latent representation of the environment.
Importantly,  𝐳 e \mathbf{z}_{e}  receives no supervision of which environmental factors it should represent.

at flow timestep  t t , and  𝐮 t \mathbf{u}_{t}  its corresponding target flow

 For each training environment  e e , we maintain an environment latent
 𝐳 e \mathbf{z}_{e} , randomly initialized by sampling each dimension from a
uniform distribution: 
 

 
 
 𝐳 e ( 0 ) ∼ 𝒰 ⁡ ( 0 , 1 ) . \mathbf{z}_{e}^{(0)}\sim\mathcal{U}(0,1). 
 
 (3) 
 
 We alternate between two stages throughout training: world-model optimization and environment-latent optimization, with  k k  indexing the outer optimization cycles. The world model is updated with low learning rate to learn general dynamics

 Stage I: World-Model Optimization. 

 
 In the first stage, we fix all  𝐳 e \mathbf{z}_{e}  and optimize only the world-model parameters. Let  𝒟 e \mathcal{D}_{e}  denote the interaction trajectories collected under environment  e e . 
 

 
 
 θ ( k + 1 ) = arg ⁡ min θ ​ 𝔼 e , ( 𝐱 , 𝐚 , 𝐲 ) ∼ 𝒟 e ​ [ ℒ FM ​ ( θ , 𝐳 e ( k ) ) ] . \theta^{(k+1)}=\arg\min_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{e,\,(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{a},\mathbf{y})\sim\mathcal{D}_{e}}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{FM}}\left(\theta,\mathbf{z}_{e}^{(k)}\right)\right]. 
 
 (4) 
 
 During this stage,  𝐳 e \mathbf{z}_{e}  is held constant, while the same world model is optimized over trajectories spanning different environments and actions. This encourages  θ \theta  to capture predictive structure that is shared across the training distribution, including how visual states evolve to different robot actions. 
 
 
 
 Stage II: Environment-Latent Optimization. 

 
 In the second stage, we reverse the optimization: the world model is frozen, and only the environment latents are updated. For each environment  e e , 
 

 
 
 𝐳 e ( k + 1 ) = arg ⁡ min 𝐳 e ​ 𝔼 ( 𝐱 , 𝐚 , 𝐲 ) ∼ 𝒟 e ​ [ ℒ FM ​ ( θ ( k + 1 ) , 𝐳 e ) ] . \mathbf{z}_{e}^{(k+1)}=\arg\min_{\mathbf{z}_{e}}\mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{a},\mathbf{y})\sim\mathcal{D}_{e}}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{FM}}\left(\theta^{(k+1)},\mathbf{z}_{e}\right)\right]. 
 
 (5) 
 
 Because  θ \theta  is fixed, the only way to reduce prediction error is to change  𝐳 e \mathbf{z}_{e}  to better match the interaction outcomes observed in environment  e e .
As a result, environments that induce systematically different outcomes drive their latents toward different regions of the latent space. 
 
 
 
 Alternating Optimization 

 
 Training starts with Stage I, then alternates between Stage II and Stage I, each optimized for a fixed number of steps while keeping the other component fixed. As the
world model improves, latents are optimized against an increasingly
capable predictive model; conversely, the refined latents provide
more informative conditioning for subsequent world-model optimization. 
 
 
 
 
 3.1.2  Test-Time Optimization 

 
 At test time, we adapt to a previously unseen environment by optimizing only
its environment latent while keeping the trained world model fixed. We
initialize a new latent  𝐳 test ( 0 ) \mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{test}}^{(0)}  and observe one or
more interaction trajectories on randomly-sampled actions. We denote the resulting test-time interaction
set as 
 

 
 
 𝒟 test = { ( 𝐱 i , 𝐚 i , 𝐲 i ) } i = 1 N , \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{test}}=\left\{(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{a}_{i},\mathbf{y}_{i})\right\}_{i=1}^{N}, 
 
 (6) 
 
 where each tuple consists of an observation  𝐱 i \mathbf{x}_{i} , an
action  𝐚 i \mathbf{a}_{i} , and the resulting observations
 𝐲 i \mathbf{y}_{i} . 
 
 
 We then optimize  𝐳 test \mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{test}}  using the same flow-matching
objective employed during training: 
 

 
 
 𝐳 test ( s + 1 ) = 𝐳 test ( s ) − η z ( s ) ​ ∇ 𝐳 test ( s ) 1 | 𝒟 test | ​ ∑ ( 𝐱 , 𝐚 , 𝐲 ) ∈ 𝒟 test ℒ FM ​ ( θ ∗ , 𝐳 test ( s ) , 𝐱 , 𝐚 , 𝐲 ) . \mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{test}}^{(s+1)}=\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{test}}^{(s)}-\eta_{z}^{(s)}\nabla_{\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{test}}^{(s)}}\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{test}}|}\sum_{(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{a},\mathbf{y})\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{test}}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{FM}}\left(\theta^{*},\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{test}}^{(s)};\mathbf{x},\mathbf{a},\mathbf{y}\right). 
 
 (7) 
 
 where  θ ∗ \theta^{*}  denotes the frozen world-model parameters and
 η z ( s ) \eta_{z}^{(s)}  follows a coarse-to-fine learning-rate strategy, with larger learning rates in early optimization steps and
smaller learning rates in later steps.
Because the world model is fixed, prediction errors over the observed
interactions can only be reduced by updating  𝐳 test \mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{test}} .
The latent is therefore optimized toward a representation under which the
frozen world model reproduces the interaction outcomes observed in the
current environment. 
 
 
 Once optimized,  𝐳 test ∗ \mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{test}}^{*}  is reused to condition the world model for actions that were not observed during latent optimization. Given a different action  𝐚 ′ \mathbf{a}^{\prime} , we predict its outcome as 
 

 
 
 𝐲 ^ ′ ∼ p θ ∗ ​ ( 𝐲 ∣ 𝐱 , 𝐚 ′ , 𝐳 test ∗ ) . \hat{\mathbf{y}}^{\prime}\sim p_{\theta^{*}}\left(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x},\mathbf{a}^{\prime},\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{test}}^{*}\right). 
 
 (8) 
 
 The resulting conditioned world model serves as an environment-adapted test-time simulator, allowing it to predict the consequences of different actions under the inferred environment. 
 
 
 Unlike amortized history-encoder approaches,  T2-z  uses direct optimization. This avoids committing to a fixed history mapping and searches for the representation that best explains the current environment. The formulation can naturally incorporate any number of test-time trajectories that differs from training, by optimizing a latent against all available evidence. Moreover,  T2-z  is not restricted to the range of factor values during training, providing greater flexibility under out-of-distribution configurations.
Ablation in  fig.   5  shows this optimization remains stable across varying test-time trajectory numbers, and is interaction-efficient with a single trajectory sufficient in most settings.

 Evaluation Metrics. 

 
 (1)  Physical Error  measures task-relevant state prediction:
object-location error in pixels for Friction, Gravity, Mass Collision, and Diverse Backgrounds; lamp activation error for Light Switch; and bar-tilt
angle error in degrees for Mass Balance. For real-world tasks, we measure
tracked-state error in pixels. We report  Physical Error (Mean) ,
averaged over predicted frames, and  Physical Error (Final) , measured
at the final predicted frame.
(2)  Video Generation Quality  is measured by LPIPS and PSNR between predicted and ground-truth videos. LPIPS measures perceptual discrepancy in a learned feature space, capturing differences in visual structure and appearance, and serves as our primary measure of generation quality. In contrast, PSNR primarily reflects pixel-level reconstruction fidelity and is less informative about semantically meaningful differences or whether object motion is physically correct.
(3)  Action Success  treats the world model as a simulator: given candidate actions, we predict their outcomes and select the action most likely to achieve the task goal, such as pushing a ball to a target, balancing a stick, or closing a door. We execute the chose action in real world and observe if meets the success criterions in Supp  appendix   C . 
 
 
 
 Baselines. 

 
 We compare  T2-z  with four baselines using the same world-model backbone.  Standard World Model  uses no environment latent or test-time adaptation;  LoRA TTA  adapts attention LoRA parameters from test-time interactions;  DINOv2 Context  amortizes a 32-D environment context from interaction trajectories; and  TTT-KQV  adapts fast weights within the world model. All adaptive baselines receive the same test-time interaction trajectories as  T2-z . 
 
 
 Please refer to the Supplementary for more details about architecture ( appendix   A ), task definitions ( appendix   C ), baseline implementation ( appendix   D ), and compute efficiency comparison ( section   E.4 ).

 Single-Factor Environmental Variation. 

 
 We first evaluate  T2-z  under individual unobserved environmental factors, spanning
continuous physical properties, mass-dependent interactions, and discrete
human-defined rules. As shown in
 table   1 ,  T2-z  achieves the highest action success on all five
tasks, increasing the five-task average from  36.9 % 36.9\%  for the Standard World
Model to  76.6 % 76.6\% , while reducing average LPIPS from  0.0469 0.0469  to  0.0325 0.0325 .
It also substantially improves task-relevant prediction, reducing mean error
from  11.06 11.06  to  3.85 3.85  pixels on Friction,  21.93 21.93  to  6.45 6.45  pixels on Mass
Collision, and  1.18 ∘ 1.18^{\circ}  to  0.64 ∘ 0.64^{\circ}  on Mass Balance. These gains arise because  T2-z  directly optimizes an environment latent from observed interactions, capturing environment-specific variation more flexibly than non-adaptive or amortized baselines.

different backgrounds organize consistently rather than separating according to scene
appearance. This indicates that environment-latent can isolate
environment-specific information that is shared across visually different
observations, enabling a unified latent structure to emerge across diverse
scenes.

 5  Conclusion 

 
 We introduce  T2-z  which augments robotic world models with enviornment latent conditioning.
 T2-z  alternates between world-model optimization that learns action-conditioned dyanmics, and environment-latent optimization that captures hidden environment factors. For a new environment, the world model is frozen and only the latent is optimized.
Across 7 simulation and 4 real-world tasks,  T2-z  outperforms baselines a lot in both video prediction and action selection.

 A.1  Architecture and latent conditioning 

 
 Inputs and inherited backbone. 

 
 For an episode in environment  e e , the model receives one initial RGB frame
 I 0 I_{0}  and an aligned sequence  a = ( a t ) t a=(a_{t})_{t}  of 14-D robot actions, and predicts

initial frame into the video-latent stream and decodes predicted latents

extra cross-attention token.
In short,  I 0 I_{0}  enters the video-latent stream,  a a  follows BWM’s two

 A.2  Training and test-time adaptation 

 
 Training schedule. 

 
 Training uses the inherited flow-matching video-prediction loss, excluding
the initial conditioning frame. A latent block freezes the shared model and
updates the environment table; a model block freezes the table and updates the
DiT, action encoder, and two projectors. The basic cycle alternates a
200-update latent block and a 200-update model block. An initial 300-update model block precedes
the curriculum. The shared-model optimizer is AdamW with learning rate
 10 − 5 10^{-5} , weight decay  0.01 0.01 , 100 warmup updates, gradient clipping  0.5 0.5 ,
and bfloat16 training. The environment-latent optimizer uses learning
rate  0.03 0.03  and zero weight decay. Latents are independently initialized from
 𝒰 ⁡ ( 0 , 1 ) \mathcal{U}(0,1)  and clipped to  [ 0 , 1 ] [0,1]  after training updates. 
 
 
 
 Test-time adaptation. 

 
 For a held-out environment, a 32-D latent is optimized from its
test-time interaction trajectories; all shared weights stay frozen. Query outcomes never
enter this update. Both simulation and real-world main evaluations use
40 latent updates with learning rates  3.0 , 1.5 , 0.5 , 0.15 3.0,1.5,0.5,0.15 , each for ten
updates. The resulting latent is reused across query actions from that
environment. Exact task-specific execution configurations are released with
the code.

the number of steel beads inside the ball (not visible in the video frame),
and the Door task uses a spring-loaded latch. 
 
 
 Ball Friction. 

 
 We vary the mass of steel beads placed inside the ball, changing its total
mass and the sliding-friction force under a robot swing
(Figure  8 a). The ball variants in the latent-space
visualizations correspond to these different steel-bead loads.
We track the ball’s visible center for native-pixel ADE/FDE. Success means
that the selected swing moves the ball to the right-hand blue-marker target. 
 
 
 
 Door Close. 

 
 The door has a spring-loaded latch; we replace the spring to change how much
closing action is needed to engage it (Figure  8 b).
The tracked outer door edge, rather than a centroid or hinge angle, defines
native-pixel ADE/FDE. Success means that the selected action fully closes
and latches the door. 
 
 
 
 Stick Balance. 

 
 We change the weights attached to the stick’s ends, altering its mass
distribution and thus the
angle at which it settles after lifting (Figure  8 c).
We track the two material markers at its ends and average their
positional errors for ADE/FDE. We sample a set of candidate actions and use
the model to predict which actions will maintain balance. One action is
selected uniformly at random from the predicted balancing set and executed
on the real robot. A trial is successful if the stick actually maintains
balance. Each sampled candidate set contains at least one action that
achieves balance. If the model predicts no balancing action, the trial is
counted as a failure. We report the fraction of successful trials. 
 
 
 
 Soft Pull. 

 
 The task uses soft tubes with different stiffness coefficients. The robot
may grasp from the left or right, and the tubes also have different natural
curvatures (Figure  8 d). The central prediction
question is approximately how far the grasp must rotate before the tube
starts dragging the object. The visible object center defines native-pixel
ADE/FDE. Given a candidate action, the model predicts the rotation angle
 θ ^ \hat{\theta}  at which the object starts sliding. We then conduct two
real-robot tests, rotating to  θ ^ − 6 ∘ \hat{\theta}-6^{\circ}  and
 θ ^ + 6 ∘ \hat{\theta}+6^{\circ} , respectively. A trial is successful only if the
object does not slide in the first test and slides in the second; otherwise,
it is counted as a failure. We report the fraction of successful trials. 
 
 
 
 
 
 Appendix D  Baseline Implementation Details 

 
 All four baselines use the task-level training data and per-method GPU
budgets in Tables  5 – 6 .
They use the same query videos and, for action-selection tasks, the same
candidate actions and success criteria as T2-Z. The three adaptive
baselines receive exactly the same test-time interaction trajectories as our latent
optimizer.
The tables give the common task-level batch specification; DINOv2 Context
uses a subset of sampled trajectories for conditioning and all sampled trajectories
as queries during training, whereas TTT-KQV
processes them as ordered within-environment streams. 
 
 
 D.1  Standard World Model 

 
 This baseline fine-tunes the BWM-initialized DiT and action encoder on
pooled task trajectories without an environment latent or test-time
adaptation. It uses AdamW with model learning rate  10 − 5 10^{-5} , weight decay
 0.01 0.01 , 100 warmup updates, gradient clipping  0.5 0.5 , and bfloat16. 
 
 
 
 D.2  LoRA TTA 

 
 Starting from the Standard World Model, rank-8,  α = 8 \alpha=8  LoRA
adapters  ( Hu et al., 2022 ) 
are placed on the DiT attention Q/K/V/output projections. The backbone
stays frozen during test-time adaptation. Adapters are reset for each
environment and optimized on its test-time interaction trajectories, then held fixed
across that environment’s query actions. The Friction configuration uses 50
AdamW adaptation updates at learning rate  10 − 4 10^{-4} , zero weight decay, and
gradient clipping  1 1 ; task-specific adaptation configurations are
released with the code. 
 
 
 
 D.3  DINOv2 Context 

 
 A frozen DINOv2-B/14 encoder  ( Oquab et al., 2024 )  extracts conditioning-frame features, while a
learned 128-D action GRU summarizes the intervening 14-D actions. For each
of  N N  task-configured sampled frames, the CLS and mean patch features are
projected to 256 dimensions. The  N − 1 N-1  adjacent visual–action transition
features (640-D each) are concatenated; a  640 ​ ( N − 1 ) → → 1024 640(N-1)\!\to\!1024\!\to\!1024 
MLP and final projection produce the 32-D context. Multiple conditioning-trajectory
summaries are averaged before that final projection.
At each training update, we sample a set of trajectories  Q e Q_{e}  from a training
environment and select a subset  C e ⊆ Q e C_{e}\subseteq Q_{e}  for conditioning. All
trajectories in  Q e Q_{e}  serve as training queries. The number of conditioning trajectories is sampled from the
task-configured choices (typically one or two; one to four for Soft Pull),
so the predictor learns to use different conditioning-set sizes. Every query
in  Q e Q_{e}  is predicted using only the context inferred from  C e C_{e} ; training
minimizes the mean query-video flow-matching loss. The context
head, BWM DiT, action encoder, and two context projectors are trained
jointly, while DINOv2 remains frozen. The model and context-head learning
rates are  10 − 5 10^{-5}  and  10 − 4 10^{-4} , respectively. At test time, context
comes from a forward pass over the observed test-time interaction trajectories, with no gradient
update. 
 
 
 
 D.4  TTT-KQV 

 
 We adapt a video test-time-training fast-weight module  ( Dalal et al., 2025 ) 
to the BWM backbone. Gated two-layer GELU MLP modules with expansion
factor four are inserted after eight approximately evenly spaced
attention blocks. Training randomly shuffles the chunks from each sampled
environment in a batch. Within each training stream, all chunks use the same
sampled flow-matching timestep (but independent noise), matching the
same-timestep processing of observed interactions and query videos at inference.
At position  i i , the preceding chunks form the observed prefix and the current chunk is the query. Fast weights
reset to a learned shared initialization at the start of each stream.
For each chunk, the module causally writes its key–value tokens before
reading its output. Let  x t x_{t}  denote an input token,  h h  an attention head,

 E.4  Computational efficiency 

 
 We profile a representative Friction setting on one H200 GPU.
For training, each method is warmed up for two optimizer steps and timed for
ten further steps. Figure  10  reports the
median compute-only optimizer time per update, excluding dataset reads, and
peak allocated GPU memory rather than full-run training time. Each measured
update has six loss-bearing video chunks; method-specific conditioning
computation remains included. The world-model and latent updates of our method
are separate phases and are therefore shown separately. LoRA TTA starts from
the Standard World Model and has no distinct world-model training row. 
 
 
 For inference, all methods receive the same test-time interaction trajectory and five
disjoint query video chunks. We use 25 denoising steps and exclude model
loading, dataset reads, and MP4 writing from timing; query generation includes
pipeline sampling and VAE decoding. Interaction processing is performed once
and reused across queries.
DINOv2 Context encodes the interaction trajectory without gradient updates, our method runs
40 latent updates, and LoRA TTA runs 50 adapter updates. TTT-KQV precomputes
one post-interaction fast state at each denoising timestep and reuses those
states for all five queries. Figure  11 
separates interaction processing from five-query generation and shows how processing
time changes with  K ∈ { 1 , 2 , 4 } K\in\{1,2,4\} ; the five-method total-time comparison
uses  K = 1 K=1  throughout. At  K = 1 K=1 , our method takes 18.09 s including latent
adaptation and five query generations, versus 32.73 s for LoRA TTA and
37.57 s for cached TTT-KQV.
