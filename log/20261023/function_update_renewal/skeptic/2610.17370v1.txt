 Abstract 
    
 Scientific discovery with language models requires finding the best valid solution within a finite computational budget. We study how feedback from this search can improve the procedure used to conduct it.
We introduce  HarnessTTT , a test-time learning framework that trains a harness proposer while keeping the executor frozen. The proposer generates executable harnesses comprising system prompts, reusable skills, tools, middleware, and execution controls. Each harness guides a budgeted discovery rollout, whose outcome provides feedback for updating the proposer. A discovery-oriented policy-gradient objective combines gap-normalized rewards, leave-one-out advantages (RLOO), and max-sharpening to favor high-performing proposals. Generated components are validated before execution, and the search retains the best valid solution and selected harnesses across rounds. Across fourteen discovery tasks in mathematics,
systems, GPU kernels, heuristic programming, astrodynamics, and biology,  HarnessTTT  with a
frozen 9B executor improves on OpenEvolve with the same executor on all
eleven benchmark tasks (e.g., from 1.17 to 2.64 on circle packing), improves
on the discovery-fine-tuned Finch-9B by up to  92 % 92\% , holds the best
open-weight sub-10B result on nine, and reaches  0.380919 0.380919  on Erdős
minimum overlap, better than the best human reference of  0.380927 0.380927 .
Scaling the proposer and frozen executor to 27B improves on eleven of fourteen tasks,
and transferring the learned harnesses to GPT-5.5 matches or exceeds
the strongest reported system of any backbone on nine of fourteen tasks.
Together, these results show that harness
search can itself be learned at test time: a trained proposer lets a small
frozen executor search effectively, and the harnesses it finds carry over to
stronger executors.

 1  Introduction 

 
 Scientific discovery advances knowledge and technology by finding solutions beyond what established methods can achieve  [ 52 ,  42 ] . Recent work  [ 77 ,  72 ,  65 ]  on AI for scientific discovery formulates each task as an exploration for the best valid solution to a single challenging scientific problem within a finite computational budget, with the hope of surpassing the best-known results from the best human expert  [ 14 ] . Success is therefore measured by the best solution found during the exploration, rather than the average quality of all attempts. 
 
 
 Large language models have tackled these tasks through two representative routes as shown in Figure  3 .
TTT-Discover  [ 77 ]  performs reinforcement learning at test
time  [ 3 ,  87 ] , directly updating the executor—the model that solves the
task—from its search outcomes guided by the reward. This adapts the model to solve the problem directly, but requires repeated sampling and training, with 25,600 rollouts per problem
in its reported configuration (50 rounds  × \times  512 rollouts). Moreover, the learned behavioral adaptation remains encoded in the executor’s parameters, making individual changes difficult to isolate and limiting their direct transfer to a different or stronger executor. Artifact
evolution methods  [ 52 ,  42 ,  58 ,  28 ,  72 ]  instead keep the executor and harness fixed—adaptation occurs through the feedback artifacts supplied in context. Artifacts are inspectable and reusable, allowing subsequent attempts to build on previous discoveries. However, adaptation within the search occurs through the artifacts supplied in context: the model must infer useful revisions based on its in-context capabilities  [ 59 ,  38 ] ,
without parameter updates from these outcomes.
In long-horizon discovery, successive rounds produce increasingly extensive histories. When more of this history is incorporated into the input, accumulating more experience does not reliably translate into more effective exploration  [ 32 ,  18 ] . 
 
 
 To address the above limitations, we introduce  HarnessTTT , which updates the proposer’s parameters to generate and refine task-specific harnesses while keeping the executor frozen (as shown in Figure  2 ).
Our approach takes inspiration from human researchers, who improve not only the solutions they explore, but also the harness through which they explore them. During the exploration, humans may develop reusable problem-solving skills, revise construction methods, build specialized tools, or reorganize the experimental workflow to improve exploration efficiency. On a circle-packing problem, for example, a
researcher might adopt a boundary-first construction strategy, write tools
that check pairwise overlaps and boundary violations or compute the largest
radii a fixed set of centers admits, and organize geometric diagnostics to guide subsequent refinements. In an agentic system, these changes correspond to adapting the  complete harness  that governs the executor’s behavior  [ 71 ,  67 ,  29 ] . These harness-level adaptations shape an entire subsequent sequence of attempts, rather than only the next candidate solution. 
 
 
 HarnessTTT  makes such harness adaptation a learned process as shown in Figure  4 . At each round, unlike existing methods that adapt only part of the harness—a prompt or context playbook  [ 70 ,  24 ,  1 ,  83 ] , a code
patch installed at predefined lifecycle hooks of a fixed
runtime  [ 56 ] , or a fixed four-module protocol (memory, planning,
action, and tool/skill orchestration) that draws on a given tool and skill
registry  [ 79 ] —our proposer model generates  complete harnesses  comprising system prompts, skills, tools, middleware, and execution-control parameters.
Each generated harness guides the frozen executor through a budgeted discovery rollout from a shared incumbent solution. Improvements in the best valid solution provide rewards, which update the proposer’s parameters via our carefully designed reinforcement learning methods, while only the selected best solution, the incumbent harness, and a compact experience digest are retained for the next round.
Across rounds, discovery experience changes the proposer’s policy itself, enabling reliable improvement via learning. Meanwhile, the resulting harness remains explicit and separate from the executor’s weights: it can be inspected, revised, and deployed with a stronger executor, providing a mechanism for weak-to-strong transfer  [ 7 ] . 
 
 
 Implementing  HarnessTTT  presents
two challenges.
First,  harness-level credit assignment is indirect and noisy . The proposer generates a harness rather than a final solution, so its reward is observed only through a separate, stochastic executor rollout.
Even when a fixed solution has a deterministic score, the same harness can
produce different trajectories and yield different best solutions. The observed reward therefore reflects both harness quality and executor sampling variability.
We develop a discovery-oriented RLOO-based update  [ 25 ,  2 ] 
with gap-normalized rewards, leave-one-out advantages without
variance normalization  [ 34 ] , max-sharpening  [ 54 ,  6 ] , and filtering
of uninformative groups  [ 74 ] , designed to limit noise amplification
while accommodating heterogeneous reward scales and
best-of-group selection.
Second,  harness optimization must guard against reward
hacking and information leakage . Higher evaluator scores do
not necessarily reflect better solutions: an agent may exploit
flaws in the evaluation process  [ 26 ,  39 ]  or use information unavailable
under the intended task protocol  [ 23 ] . We therefore design a new critic component to address this challenge.

 We evaluate  HarnessTTT  on fourteen discovery problems spanning mathematical
constructions  [ 42 ,  14 ] , systems
benchmarks  [ 9 ] , GPU kernels  [ 77 ] , heuristic programming
contests  [ 4 ,  22 ] , astrodynamics, and biology. With a frozen
Qwen3.5-9B executor  [ 49 ] ,  HarnessTTT  improves on OpenEvolve with the
same executor on all eleven benchmark tasks and holds the best open-weight
sub-10B result on nine, ahead of the discovery-fine-tuned
Finch-9B  [ 30 ]  on every task by up to  91.7 % 91.7\% . Scaling the proposer and the frozen
executor to Qwen3.8-27B  [ 50 ]  improves on  eleven  of fourteen tasks,
and transferring the learned harnesses to GPT-5.5 matches or exceeds
the strongest reported system of any backbone on  nine  of fourteen tasks and
comes within  1 % 1\%  on four more. Our ablations show where the gains come
from: at matched rollouts, routing reward into the proposer’s weights reaches
better results earlier than updating the executor or the proposer’s context;
wider search per round yields late breakthroughs; and our RLOO objective with
max-sharpening outperforms standard GRPO and a frozen proposer. Together,
these results show that the search procedure itself can be learned at test
time with a small frozen model and carried over to stronger executors. 
 
 
 
 2  Related Work 

 \para 
 Self-evolution by adapting the model. One family improves an agent
by changing model weights. Test-time training and test-time RL adapt weights
per instance or per problem  [ 3 ,  87 ] , while test-time scaling spends more inference compute  [ 40 ] ; for discovery,
TTT-Discover  [ 77 ]  and ThetaEvolve  [ 65 ]  fine-tune
the executor on its own search rollouts and hold strong published  ≤ \leq 10B
records, while discovery fine-tuning amortizes the skill across tasks by
training on evolution-trajectory corpora, as in the Finch models we use as a
parameter-matched comparator  [ 30 ] . Closest to us, HASE  [ 36 ]  and SIA  [ 17 ]  co-evolve weights
 and  harness, which couples the improver to the improved and requires
update access to the executor;  HarnessTTT  freezes the executor and trains a
separate proposer policy. 
 
 \para 
 Self-evolution by editing the harness. A second family keeps the
model frozen and revises an external artifact. Evolutionary program search
around frozen LLMs produces state-of-the-art discovery programs:
FunSearch  [ 52 ] , AlphaEvolve  [ 42 ,  14 ] ,
open reimplementations and successors  [ 58 ,  28 ] ,
heuristic evolution  [ 31 ] , equation discovery  [ 60 ] , reward
design  [ 37 ] , and multi-agent and meta-evolution
systems  [ 48 ,  33 ] , evaluated on algorithm-engineering
suites  [ 22 ]  and the systems problems of  Cheng et al. [9] ;
research agents automate the surrounding pipeline  [ 35 ] . A
parallel line lets a frozen model rewrite its own code or scaffold:
STOP  [ 78 ] , the Darwin Gödel Machine  [ 81 ] , self-improving
coding agents  [ 51 ,  73 ] , capability-synthesizing
generalists  [ 47 ] , and a survey of the space  [ 13 ] .
A third optimizes the text or structure around the model: prompt
search  [ 86 ,  70 ,  12 ,  16 ,  62 ] , prompt
compilation  [ 24 ,  45 ] , textual gradients  [ 76 ,  10 ] ,
reflective evolution  [ 1 ] , automated agent and workflow
design  [ 20 ,  82 ,  55 ,  85 ] , whole-harness
editing  [ 29 ,  80 ] , and in-context
self-correction  [ 64 ,  59 ,  38 ] . Across this family the proposer’s  weights are fixed , so design
knowledge is re-derived per task rather than accumulated. We train the
proposer across rounds, and its action space includes executable
tool code: unlike tool creation that abstracts functions from solution
traces  [ 8 ,  46 ,  75 ,  66 ] , the tools here are emitted by a
 learned  policy and admitted through a leak-limiting review chain
(§ 3.2 ). 
 
 
 
 3  HarnessTTT 

 
 HarnessTTT  learns the discovery procedure by training a harness
proposer while keeping the executor frozen. The proposer specifies how the
executor searches through proposed harnesses; the executor conducts budgeted rollouts to generate candidate solutions and interacts with evaluators to obtain scores for them.
Feedback from these evaluators updates the proposer and the search state
retained between rounds. We first formulate the discovery objective, then
describe the harness representation, proposer update, iterative search, and
validation protocol.

 \para 
 Executable harness representation.
What the proposer can learn is bounded by what a harness can express.
Existing systems represent it narrowly: prompt optimizers rewrite
instructions  [ 12 ,  24 ,  1 ] , reflective agents append notes
to the context  [ 59 ,  83 ] , and Harness-R1 patches a
fixed runtime without adding executable tools  [ 56 ] . These change
what the executor is told but not what it can do, such as adding an analysis
tool or restarting a stalled search. We instead represent the harness as an
executable program built on NexAU  [ 41 ]  with five editable components
(Figure  8 ; Appendix  C ): a  system prompt 
for strategy and workflow,  skills  for reusable procedures,
 tools  including proposer-written Python functions,  middleware 
hooks that run between executor steps (e.g., budget reminders, restarts after
stalls), and  control parameters  for sampling and iteration limits. The
proposer edits a private copy of  H ⋆ H^{\star}  and cannot touch the task program,
evaluator, executor weights, or shared runtime; unchanged fields are
inherited, so a partial specification still yields a complete harness. Each
episode ends with  validate_harness , which checks schemas, mounts,
and generated code (§ 3.2 ), and
 submit_harness ; accepted specifications are compiled and hashed to
remove duplicates within the group. The result is a standalone artifact that
can be inspected or run with another frozen executor (§ 4.5 ). 
 
 
 3.1  Learning the harness proposer 

 
 A harness is judged only through executor rollouts.
Even with a deterministic evaluator, the same harness can lead a stochastic
executor to different solutions. An observed score therefore mixes the
quality of a proposed procedure with downstream sampling variability.
Our RLOO-based update  [ 2 ,  25 ]  must assign credit under this
indirect feedback while accounting for shrinking absolute gains and an
objective that retains the best discovery rather than the average attempt.

 3.2  Experience retention and harness validation 

 \para 
 Incumbent and bounded context.
At the end of each round, the best valid program in the group and its harness
become the new incumbent if they strictly improve on its score
(Figure  4 ; Appendix  E ), and the next round starts
from them rather than from the seed. Solution quality therefore accumulates,
and rewards measure progress beyond the current starting point. Under a fixed
evaluator the incumbent score never decreases, but this follows from retention
and does not by itself show that the proposer improved. Unlike reflective
methods  [ 59 ,  1 ] , we do not feed the proposer the history of
earlier candidates. Such a history grows every round, raises cost, and buries
informative outcomes in a long context that models use
poorly  [ 32 ] . Feedback that drifts across rounds would also make
within-group reward differences reflect context rather than proposer quality.
The proposer instead reads a fixed-size  experience digest , rebuilt each
round: the incumbent harness and program (truncated), the seed and current best
scores, and a stall flag. Experience across rounds is thus carried by the
digest and the proposer’s weights. Proposals from the same parent share this
digest and differ only in sampling seed. 
 
 \para 
 Harness validation.

Evaluator scores cannot tell real improvements from exploits of the evaluation
interface  [ 61 ,  26 ,  11 ,  5 ,  84 ] ,
so every generated tool is validated before rollouts. The  critic  in
Figure  4  is this validation pipeline, not a learned value
function. Tools access the task only through a restricted interface that
withholds the evaluator implementation, ground truth, and network access, and
they must pass static checks and a sandboxed self-test. A failing tool gets at
most two repair attempts, screened for evaluator or target-score content, and
is dropped if it still fails. These controls
describe the evaluated protocol, not an independently verified guarantee.

 \para 
 Evaluation protocol. Following the default setting of Finch  [ 30 ] , every task provides a description, a
seed program with a delimited editable region, a black-box evaluator, and a
hard per-rollout evaluator-call budget. Tables report native metrics and mark
their optimization direction. The configured executor interface exposes
neither evaluator code nor ground truth (§ 3.2 ). 
 
 \para 
 Implementation and reporting unit. The principal executor is
frozen Qwen3.5-9B  [ 49 ]  served with vLLM  [ 27 ] ; the scale
comparison uses frozen Qwen3.8-27B  [ 50 ] . The standard configuration runs 20 outer rounds and samples  K = 32 K{=}32  (Qwen3.5-9B) or  K = 8 K{=}8  (Qwen3.8-27B) harnesses per round; for each
harness, the executor runs a single solution trajectory and may call the
evaluator at most  B = 20 B{=}20  (Qwen3.5-9B) or  B = 5 B{=}5  (Qwen3.8-27B) times within it; the proposer is a persistent LoRA adapter  [ 19 ]  trained with
our proposed advantage (Eq.  3 ) for three epochs per round at
batch size 8 under a KL anchor. Tables report the selected valid outcome of
each single search campaign, not an across-campaign mean; reruns of a selected
program characterize execution noise only.

 4.3  Ablation Study 

 \para 
 Comparison of reward-update locations.
A rollout reward can update the executor’s weights, the proposer’s context, or
the proposer’s weights. We compare the three routes on two tasks with the same
base checkpoint, evaluator, initial harness, seed program, and 20 evaluator
calls per executor trajectory. The executor arm is a budget-scaled
TTT-Discover-style reference that keeps the initial harness and updates the
executor with a three-epoch rank-64 LoRA on  K = 16 K{=}16  final-edit trajectories
per batch. The proposer arm trains a persistent rank-64 LoRA for three epochs
on  K = 8 K{=}8  proposals. The context arm makes no gradient update and adds two
frozen-model analyst calls after the cold round. Because each harness candidate
also costs a proposer rollout, Figure  5 a plots every arm
against total rollouts. A harness round spends 8 proposer and 8 executor
rollouts; the executor arm has no proposer, so it runs 16 executor
trajectories per round, and every arm reaches 320 rollouts after 20 rounds. The proposer
arm reaches the highest endpoint on both tasks and gets there sooner. By 128
rollouts it has reached 1.00 on Erdős and 0.90 on autocorrelation II
(normalized), while the context arm is at 0.34 and 0.41, and the executor arm
is still below the plotted range on Erdős and at 0.26 on
autocorrelation II. Updating the proposer thus turns rollout rewards into
better search procedures early in the run. 
 
 \para 
 Test-time scaling.
We study how discovery performance changes with search width
and depth by varying the number of harness proposals per round,
 K ∈ { 2 , 4 , 8 , 16 , 32 } K\in\{2,4,8,16,32\} , and running each configuration for
15 outer rounds on CP26 and Hadamard
(Figure  5 b).
The widest configuration,  K = 32 K=32 , achieves the highest final
score on both tasks: 2.515 on CP26 and 0.812 on Hadamard,
compared with 2.074 and 0.573 for the best narrower configurations,
respectively. It also makes substantial late-stage improvements on both tasks, while the narrower configurations largely plateau. 
 
 \para 
 Reward design.
Figure  5 c varies only the proposer objective, with the same
frozen executor, initial harness, seed program, budget, and  K = 32 K{=}32  over
15 rounds; scores are normalized as in Figure  5 a. Updating
the proposer helps under either objective, and ours helps most: with the
RLOO endpoint at 1.00, GRPO reaches 0.38 on CP26 and 0.26 on Hadamard, and
the frozen proposer 0.14 and 0.21. GRPO’s curve is nearly flat after

 5  Conclusion 

 
 We introduced  HarnessTTT , which casts test-time discovery as learning the
harness rather than the model: a proposer is trained online to write
executable harnesses for a frozen executor. With a frozen 9B executor,
 HarnessTTT  improves on OpenEvolve and Finch-9B on all eleven benchmark tasks
and holds the best open-weight ( ≤ \leq 10B) value on nine; a 27B model
improves further on eleven of fourteen tasks; and with transfer to GPT-5.5
it matches or exceeds the reported frontier on nine of fourteen
tasks and comes within  1 % 1\%  on four more. Ablations show that, at matched
rollouts, updating the proposer’s weights reaches better results earlier than
updating the executor or the context, and that RLOO with max-sharpening
outperforms GRPO and a frozen proposer. Together, these results show that
harness search can itself be learned at test time, complementary to scaling
the model that executes it.

 Appendix B  HarnessTTT  on one page 

 
 Figure  2  summarizes  HarnessTTT  on a single page. Panel (a)
contrasts two test-time evolution loops that differ only in what the reward
updates. In executor RL, as in TTT-Discover  [ 77 ] , the executor
 θ \theta  samples  N N  rollouts, each covering one strategy; the reward updates
 θ \theta , and the final weights  θ ⋆ \theta^{\star}  cannot be transferred. In
 HarnessTTT , the proposer  ϕ \phi  writes  K K  harnesses—named, readable files for
the prompt, skills, tools, and middleware—that the frozen executor  M 0 M_{0} 
rolls out. A single harness-guided rollout can move through several strategies
(A → \to B → \to C). Candidates are scored and gated, the reward updates only
 ϕ \phi , and the best harness  H ⋆ H^{\star}  is a transferable package. Panel (b)
lists the four properties that follow. Exploration happens within a rollout.
Every edit is traceable as a named file. Generated code is kept safe by
schema, scope, and sandbox checks. The harness transfers to other frozen
executors. 
 
 
 Panel (c) marks the tasks on which the learned harness, with or without

of the frontier: Erdős and AC1 (Hyra), circle packing (CORAL;

 \para 
 Overriding existing components.
The upper block rewrites what the executor reads and how it is run. Three text
fields carry the natural-language part of the harness. The system prompt sets
the executor’s workflow. The skill package is split into a short card that
advertises the skill in the executor’s tool list and a playbook body that holds
the procedure itself.  tool_descriptions  rewrites how the fixed tools
are described without touching their code, so the proposer can steer when and
how the executor calls them.  sampling  and  agent  set the
decoding parameters and the iteration cap.  middleware  tunes the
built-in controls: when the budget reminder starts, when a stalled run is
restarted and how many restarts are allowed, and the output-truncation limit. 
 
 \para 
 Changing the component set.
The lower block adds or removes components. A candidate may add up to three
tools, each with a name, a description, a JSON schema for its arguments, and a
Python implementation. It may also add up to two skill packages, and up to two
middlewares, each attached to a named hook with its own Python implementation.
Tools can be removed only if they are marked optional. These caps bound how much
new code a single candidate can introduce. 
 
 \para 
 Constraints on generated code.
Generated tool names must match  ^[a-z][a-z0-9_]{2,31}$ : they start with
a lowercase letter, contain 3–32 lowercase letters, digits, or underscores, and
may not shadow a reserved name. Each generated implementation is limited to
6,000 characters. A generated tool exposes the single  run(ctx, args) 
entry point and reaches the task only through  ctx 
(Appendix  D ). Before it is mounted, it must pass the static gates
and sandboxed self-test of § 3.2 . A tool that still fails
after repair is dropped rather than shipped. 
 
 \para 
 Validation, compilation, and hashing.
Validation is fail-closed: anything that does not pass is excluded rather than
let through. The proposer calls  validate_harness , which checks
component mounts, field schemas, and generated code, and finishes with
 submit_harness  (§ 3 ). A deterministic
materializer then merges the candidate over its parent, compiles the result
into a runnable package bound by  agent.yaml , and writes a canonical
spec of the effective harness. Within one materializer revision, identical
effective specs yield identical canonical-spec bytes and therefore the same
hash. Provenance metadata stored with the package may still differ, so the hash
identifies the effective harness rather than any particular package. Because
the hash is taken after merging, two candidates in a group that reach the same
effective harness through different partial documents share a hash. The hash
is used to de-duplicate them.

 \para 
 Scoring and budget.
The two scoring calls differ in cost and accounting.  evaluate()  runs
the full evaluation and uses one of the  B B  evaluator calls.  probe() 
scores the program on a subsample at roughly one-eighth the cost. It does not
count toward  B B , but it has its own cap. A tool can therefore screen edits
cheaply and save budgeted calls for promising ones, but the cap stops it from
using probes as unlimited free evaluations. 
 
 \para 
 Isolation.
Path resolution refuses evaluator and ground-truth locations. The configured
sandbox gives generated tools no network, general-filesystem, or subprocess
access. 
 
 \para 
 Static gate and self-test.
The static gate admits imports only from  math ,  re ,
 json ,  itertools ,  functools ,  collections ,
 heapq ,  bisect ,  random ,  statistics ,
 string ,  typing ,  dataclasses ,  numpy , and
 pandas . It also applies the forbidden-access checks of
§ 3.2 . A tool that passes the gate then runs a self-test
against a mock context under  RLIMIT_CPU  20 s and  RLIMIT_AS 
6 GB. 
 
 \para 
 Verifiability.
The public artifact does not currently include the trusted runtime, sandbox,
gate, scrubber, localization guard, or served-model request logs. These
settings therefore describe the evaluated protocol and cannot yet be verified
independently.

 Round structure. 

 
 Each task runs for 20 outer rounds. In each round the proposer samples  K K 
harnesses, and each candidate harness is rolled out once by the frozen
executor and compared against the shared incumbent score. A round therefore
launches  2 ​ K 2K  agent trajectories (one proposer episode and one candidate
rollout per candidate): 16 in the 27B campaign and 64 in the 9B campaigns.
To limit the cost of the larger executor, the 27B campaign samples  K = 8 K=8 
proposals rather than 32. It also gives each rollout  B = 5 B=5  evaluator calls
of up to 420 s, where the 9B campaigns give  B = 20 B=20  calls of up to 600 s. Every rollout is capped at 3 h of wall-clock time. A
round is accepted only if at least six of its proposals validate (the
 min6  gate). The same frozen model may repair a generated tool that
fails validation at most twice. 
 
 
 
 Parent selection and grouping. 

 
 Programs and harnesses are promoted separately. The program registry is a
strict single-program ratchet: a rollout’s program replaces the incumbent only
if it strictly improves the score. The incumbent  harness  is promoted
only when the round’s best candidate exceeds the historical best; displaced
parents are archived. From the second round on, the  K K  proposal slots are dealt round-robin to
a beam of two parents: the promoted or retained incumbent, and the best
candidate within 2% of the top score that adds a new component (or else
the second-highest scorer). An advantage group
contains the proposals that share one sampling condition (parent spec, prompt,
and budget hash). Hence  K g = K K_{g}=K  in the first round and  K / 2 K/2  afterwards, which is 8
and then 4 for the 27B campaign, and 32 and then 16 for the 9B campaigns.

 Proposer update. 

 
 Training runs on slime with the FSDP backend on 8 GPUs, for three epochs per
round over all trainable episodes of the round. Each episode is converted into
a replay of its full multi-turn trajectory, and the loss mask covers assistant
turns that are closed by a later user or tool message. Episodes are truncated
to 18,000 tokens in a terminal-preserving way: tool observations are clipped
first, and then the longest fitting suffix that starts at an assistant action
is kept. The rewarded  submit_harness  action therefore always survives
truncation. The loss is a clipped policy-gradient objective with the
precomputed advantage  A k A_{k}  broadcast over the masked tokens. slime’s own
reward normalization is disabled, consistent with the absence of variance
scaling in Eq.  3 . A KL penalty of 0.1
( low_var_kl ) anchors the policy to the frozen base. The adapter is
cumulative: each round loads only the weights of the previous round’s adapter
( --load-weights-only ). Every round’s checkpoint is retained. The
merged checkpoint is served as the next round’s proposer, while the executor
stays at the base checkpoint. 
 
 
 
 
 Appendix F  Published reference scores 

 
 The reward of § 3.1  divides a candidate’s gain over its
baseline (the incumbent score  b τ b_{\tau} ; Appendix  E ) by the baseline’s remaining gap to a
task reference  u τ u_{\tau} . The reference therefore sets the scale of credit on
each task; it is not a target that the proposer or the executor ever sees. 
 
 
 In the 27B campaign,  u τ u_{\tau}  is the largest of four fixed values: the seed
score, the best human value, the published  ≤ \leq 10B SOTA, and the Finch-9B
score. All four are expressed in the evaluator’s combined score.

the published  ≤ \leq 10B SOTA alone (Table  1 ), which
changes the reference on the four tasks where the best human value is

exceed the baseline, the remaining gap is not positive. The reward then falls
back to the relative gain over  | b τ | |b_{\tau}| , clipped to  [ − 1 , 5 ] [-1,5] . The reference
enters the reward only. It is never written into a harness, a prompt, or a
