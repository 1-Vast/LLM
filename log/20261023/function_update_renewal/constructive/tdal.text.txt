Affiliation: Center for Machine Learning Research, Peking University, Beijing, China Affiliation: AI for Science Institute (AISI), Beijing, China Affiliation: Center for Quantitative Biology, Peking University, Beijing, China Abstract Genetic and drug perturbation screens are important tools for elucidating disease mechanisms and validating therapeutic targets, but experimental costs limit the number of candidates that can be tested. Existing perturbation screening methods typically update the surrogate model with feedback while keeping the acquisition function fixed, limiting their ability to adapt selection to different response landscapes. Experimental feedback reveals current hits and informs subsequent selection by updating candidate predictions, providing a training signal for learning how to select experiments. We formulate budgeted perturbation discovery as a sequential decision problem and propose Temporal Difference Active Learning (TDAL), which learns an acquisition function online through temporal difference learning from a single screening trajectory, accounting for current hits and future discoveries. TDAL uses the Hellinger distance between hit probabilities for unmeasured candidates before and after an experiment to weight successor value, and reuses measured outcomes through leave-batch-out (LBO) to construct virtual transitions and alleviate training data scarcity. Across eight public screens spanning single, double, and triple perturbations under varied experimental configurations, TDAL achieves an average rank of 1.63 among eleven methods and ranks first on four tasks, demonstrating its adaptability across response landscapes. 1 Introduction Large-scale perturbational screens deepen the understanding of disease mechanisms and help validate therapeutic targets ( Schmidt et al., 2022 ; Zhuang et al., 2019 ; Sanchez et al., 2021 ) . The human genome comprises nearly 20,000 single-gene candidates, roughly 200 million pairs, and one trillion triples in terms of perturbation combinations. The costs of testing each perturbation and measuring its phenotype make exhaustive screening infeasible ( Mehrjou et al., 2022 ; Pacchiano et al., 2023 ) . Closed-loop systems have shown that model-guided experiment selection can improve biological discovery under limited resources ( Xu et al., 2026 ; DeMeo et al., 2025 ) . It is thus important to develop strategies that maximize cumulative discoveries within a fixed experimental budget. Active learning addresses this challenge by iteratively fitting a surrogate model to measured outcomes and using an acquisition function to select subsequent experiments (Figure 1 a). GeneDisco evaluates active learning methods for perturbation screening and reports substantial variation in their performance across experimental settings ( Mehrjou et al., 2022 ) . Existing methods employ different selection criteria to guide perturbation discovery. OAE ( Pacchiano et al., 2023 ) seeks near-optimal perturbations, DiscoBAX ( Lyle et al., 2023 ) considers response strength and mechanistic diversity, and Probability-of-Hit (PoH) ( Rubbi et al., 2026 ) prioritizes perturbations that exceed a predefined phenotypic threshold. Recent approaches also incorporate language model agents or biological priors into experiment selection ( Roohani et al., 2025 ; Hao et al., 2025 ; Li et al., 2026a ) . Indeed, different response landscapes favor different selection strategies (Figure 1 b). In conventional active learning, experimental feedback updates the surrogate model and changes subsequent selections, while the acquisition rule itself typically remains predefined. The same feedback can also inform how experiments should be selected: each experiment yields immediate discoveries and updates predictions for the remaining candidates, influencing future discovery opportunities. This motivates learning an acquisition function that accounts for both current and future discoveries from experimental feedback. We therefore formulate screening as a sequential decision problem and use experimental transitions to learn a dynamic acquisition function online (Figure 1 a). However, unlike reinforcement learning with repeated episodes, a screening campaign follows a single trajectory that cannot be reset, with each candidate queried at most once and only one real transition generated per round. Learning an acquisition function from such limited feedback presents a particular challenge. Here we introduce Temporal Difference Active Learning (TDAL), which learns a dynamic acquisition function online from experimental feedback to maximize cumulative discoveries. Building on surrogate hit probabilities, TDAL uses temporal difference learning to incorporate observed discoveries and feedback-weighted successor value into experiment selection. It weights successor value according to changes in predicted hit probabilities for the remaining candidates, reflecting how experimental feedback updates their predicted outcomes. To alleviate the scarcity of real transitions, TDAL constructs additional training transitions from previously measured outcomes through leave-batch-out (LBO). Our main contributions are threefold. • We formulate perturbation discovery as a sequential decision problem and learn a dynamic acquisition function from experimental feedback to adapt to different response landscapes. • We introduce TDAL, which learns online from a single screening trajectory through temporal difference learning. It uses the Hellinger distance to weight successor value and LBO to construct additional training transitions, alleviating training data scarcity. • We evaluate TDAL across eight screens spanning single, double, and triple perturbations. TDAL ranks first on four tasks and achieves an average rank of 1.63 among eleven methods. 2 Related Work 2.1 Active Learning and Acquisition Function Learning Active learning alternates between sample selection and model updates to improve predictions with fewer labels. Methods include BALD, which uses information gain ( Houlsby et al., 2011 ) , CoreSet, which emphasizes geometric coverage ( Sener and Savarese, 2018 ) , and BADGE, which combines uncertainty and diversity ( Ash et al., 2020 ) . For discovery, active search ( Garnett et al., 2012 ) , ENS ( Jiang et al., 2017 ) , and its batch extension ( Jiang et al., 2018 ) evaluate future hits within the budget. IDS ( Xu et al., 2022 ) balances instantaneous regret and information gain to increase cumulative response. These methods adapt sampling to observations but retain predefined acquisition functions. Other approaches learn acquisition functions from data. Learning Active Learning predicts expected error reduction ( Konyushkova et al., 2017 ) . MetaBO trains acquisition functions through reinforcement learning across tasks ( Volpp et al., 2020 ) . Amortized active search trains a neural network to imitate ENS selections ( Nguyen et al., 2024 ) . In molecular discovery, GLARE updates its selection policy online using measured activity labels ( Chen et al., 2025 ) , while Zhang et al. (2026) learn a policy to identify the optimal molecule. SOFisher uses Double DQN to select spatial-omics fields of view ( Li et al., 2026b ) . Our work focuses on perturbation screens, where TDAL learns a dynamic acquisition function online from experimental feedback. 2.2 Active Learning for Perturbation Discovery Active learning guides the discovery of perturbations with target phenotypes under a limited experimental budget. GeneDisco provides a unified benchmark for gene perturbations ( Mehrjou et al., 2022 ) . OAE seeks near-optimal perturbations with high responses ( Pacchiano et al., 2023 ) , DiscoBAX balances response strength and mechanistic diversity ( Lyle et al., 2023 ) , and PoH selects candidates by their posterior probability of exceeding a predefined phenotypic threshold ( Rubbi et al., 2026 ) . RECOVER and NAIAD target drug and genetic combination discovery, respectively ( Bertin et al., 2023 ; Qin et al., 2025 ) , while BATCHIE uses information-driven batch design to improve predictions of drug combination responses ( Tosh et al., 2025 ) . These methods update surrogate models from observations while typically keeping acquisition functions fixed. Biological priors and agents guide selection. IterPert improves predictions with multimodal priors ( Huang et al., 2024 ) , while BioBO combines gene representations and pathway information ( Li et al., 2026a ) . Soper et al. (2025) and DeepAL ( Zhu et al., 2026 ) combine knowledge graphs with Bayesian matrix factorization and representation learning, respectively, for double gene perturbation discovery. BioDiscoveryAgent and PerTurboAgent design perturbations using language models, experimental history, and tools ( Roohani et al., 2025 ; Hao et al., 2025 ) . AIDO.Builder updates models from feedback and reuses experience through memory ( Guo et al., 2026 ) . Gupta et al. (2025) found the tested agents insensitive to permuted feedback, whereas Wainrib et al. (2026) reported discovery gains from feedback beyond prior knowledge. Then we examine how local landscape structure affects active discovery, assess individual components through ablations, and analyze candidate rankings over selection rounds. 5.1 Experimental Setup Data and budgets. We evaluate TDAL on eight public datasets recording phenotypic responses to genetic perturbations, aiming to identify perturbations producing desired phenotypic changes (Appendix C.1 ). These comprise four single perturbation tasks ( Schmidt et al., 2022 ; Zhuang et al., 2019 ; Carnevale et al., 2022 ) , three double perturbation tasks ( Fong et al., 2025 ; Simpson et al., 2023 ; Horlbeck et al., 2018 ) , and one triple perturbation task ( Kuzmin et al., 2020 ) , with candidate pools ranging from 11,067 to 214,642. Initial sample sizes, batch sizes, and budgets vary across tasks (Table C2 ). Experimental protocol. We conduct retrospective sequential evaluation using measured phenotypes from these genetic perturbation datasets. Candidate identities and fixed representations are available at the start. Each method initially receives the phenotypes of n 0 n_{0} candidates, and the remaining outcomes are revealed as candidates are selected in subsequent rounds. We fix the hit threshold τ \tau before screening to target the top 5% of candidates in the predefined effect direction ( Mehrjou et al., 2022 ) . Selection aims to maximize final cumulative hits C T C_{T} within the budget, and recall Recall T = C T / ∑ x ∈ 𝒳 z ⁡ ( x ) \operatorname{Recall}_{T}=C_{T}/\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}z(x) measures discovery coverage. Within each of five seeds (0–4), methods share the candidate pool, initial data, budget, and hit threshold (Appendix C.2 ). Baselines. We compare diverse baselines with publicly available implementations (Appendix C.3 ). Perturbation discovery baselines include PoH ( Rubbi et al., 2026 ) , OAE ( Pacchiano et al., 2023 ) , and DiscoBAX ( Lyle et al., 2023 ) . General acquisition functions include UCB ( Srinivas et al., 2010 ) , Expected Improvement ( Jones et al., 1998 ) , and Thompson Sampling ( Thompson, 1933 ) . Active learning baselines include BADGE ( Ash et al., 2020 ) , CoreSet ( Sener and Savarese, 2018 ) , and TopUncertain and KMeans-Embedding from GeneDisco ( Mehrjou et al., 2022 ) . Random serves as a control. For computational scalability, we evaluate DiscoBAX on four single perturbation tasks. Across the 85 candidates, their mean ranks at selection are 16.6 under TDAL and 9,780 under concurrent surrogate predictions, showing how accumulated feedback helps prioritize hits ranked poorly by the surrogate alone (Appendix Figure F1 ). 6 Conclusion We introduce TDAL to learn a dynamic acquisition function online from a single perturbation screening trajectory. Building on surrogate-estimated hit probabilities, TDAL uses temporal difference learning to incorporate observed discoveries and feedback-weighted successor value, while LBO reconstructs additional training transitions from measured outcomes. By learning how to select experiments from feedback accumulated within the ongoing screen, TDAL adapts candidate selection to both immediate discoveries and subsequent discovery opportunities under limited experimental feedback. Across eight public screens spanning single, double, and triple perturbations, TDAL achieves an average rank of 1.63 among eleven methods, demonstrating consistent discovery performance across diverse response landscapes. Limitations. Our evaluation is retrospective, and the additive acquisition function does not explicitly model interactions among candidates within a batch. Feedback weighting also relies on surrogate-estimated hit probabilities, motivating further investigation of its robustness to prediction errors. Future work will explore batch-aware selection and alternative feedback weighting strategies, alongside prospective validation in closed-loop biological experiments. Biological knowledge guides selection through pathway information, literature, or knowledge encoded in language models. BioBO and PerTurboAgent combine such knowledge with response modeling, whereas BioDiscoveryAgent incorporates experimental observations into its language model context without fitting a response surrogate. SOFisher uses observed spatial features as inputs to its sampling policy rather than fitting a phenotype prediction model. Online acquisition updates use feedback from the current task to update the acquisition function’s parameters. Value bootstrapping constructs training targets from current rewards and estimated successor values. GLARE updates its selection policy during screening but does not explicitly bootstrap a successor value. SOFisher trains its sampling policy through value bootstrapping before application and uses fixed policy parameters on new tissue sections, while incorporating newly sampled observations into the state. TDAL performs both acquisition updates and successor value bootstrapping within the current screening task. Experience augmentation reconstructs additional transitions from measured outcomes to train the acquisition function. In budgeted experimental design without replacement, the measured dataset grows while the unmeasured pool and remaining budget shrink. A screen therefore provides a single executed trajectory and cannot return to an earlier state to test alternative experimental sequences within the same budget. LBO reconstructs predecessor states in which a measured batch has not yet been observed, then restores that batch’s real outcomes to form additional transitions to a known successor state. The same experimental outcomes can thus support batch value learning under different information states, expanding online training experience without additional experiments. Starting from the initial observations D 0 D_{0} , TDAL fits a surrogate model, estimates hit probabilities for candidates in the initial unmeasured pool U 0 U_{0} , and forms the initial selection state. It then constructs initial LBO transitions from D 0 D_{0} to initialize the acquisition network through TD training before the first query. At each round, the acquisition function scores candidates in U t U_{t} , and TDAL queries the B B candidates with the highest scores. The observed outcomes are added to D t D_{t} , and the queried candidates are removed from U t U_{t} . TDAL refits the surrogate model on the updated data. Before the next round, it updates predictions over U t + 1 U_{t+1} to construct the successor state. Comparing hit probabilities before and after the experiment over this pool gives the Hellinger weight. The selected batch, observed discovery count, feedback weight, and current and successor states form a real transition. Real and online LBO transitions enter the replay buffer, from which TD updates train the acquisition network for subsequent selection. After T T rounds, TDAL returns all verified hit candidates. A.4 Candidate Representations The mapping ϕ ⁡ ( x ) \phi(x) encodes candidate perturbation x x as a vector of fixed dimension, providing gene functional information to the surrogate and acquisition network. Human tasks use genome-wide CRISPR–Cas9 knockout screening data from the Broad Institute’s Project Achilles. The CERES gene dependency profiles in DepMap 20Q4 provide 808-dimensional gene representations. Each dimension corresponds to a cancer cell line and describes the effect of knocking out the gene on cell growth. CERES corrects for copy number effects ( Meyers et al., 2017 ) . The Kuzmin yeast task uses the 500-dimensional STRING network embeddings released with Mashup ( Cho et al., 2016 ) . These base gene representations remain fixed during screening. For a single gene perturbation, ϕ ⁡ ( x ) \phi(x) is the corresponding gene embedding. A.7 TD Learning and Experience Replay LBO transition construction. Initial LBO hides batches from D 0 D_{0} to train the acquisition network before the first query. Online LBO draws hidden batches from candidates measured after initialization, so a batch can combine observations from different rounds. Algorithm 2 gives the construction for both stages. The current measured dataset D + D^{+} and unmeasured pool U + U^{+} define the successor state s + s^{+} . Each hidden batch produces a different predecessor state, but restoring its outcomes returns to this same successor state. For a nonterminal successor, let p ^ + ​ ( x ) \hat{p}^{+}(x) denote the MC dropout hit probability estimated by the surrogate trained on D + D^{+} , for x ∈ U + x\in U^{+} . For initial LBO, the successor dataset is D + = D 0 D^{+}=D_{0} , giving p ^ + ​ ( x ) = p ^ 0 ​ ( x ) \hat{p}^{+}(x)=\hat{p}_{0}(x) . For online LBO after round t t , it is D + = D t + 1 D^{+}=D_{t+1} , giving p ^ + ​ ( x ) = p ^ t + 1 ​ ( x ) \hat{p}^{+}(x)=\hat{p}_{t+1}(x) . For each hidden batch A A , removing its outcomes from D + D^{+} gives the predecessor dataset D − D^{-} , and returning its candidates to the unmeasured pool gives U − U^{-} . We fit the predecessor surrogate g β − g_{\beta^{-}} to D − D^{-} with validation early stopping. Its MC dropout predictions give predecessor hit probabilities p ^ − ​ ( x ) \hat{p}^{-}(x) for x ∈ U − x\in U^{-} . UCB, EI, TS, TopUncertain, and OAE use the GeneDisco Bayesian MLP and retrain their surrogate after each round of new observations ( Mehrjou et al., 2022 ) . PoH. Many Needles selects the B B unmeasured candidates with the highest estimated probability of exceeding the hit threshold ( Rubbi et al., 2026 ) . At each round, we refit a Bayesian MLP with two hidden layers of widths 128 and 64 on the measured data. For each candidate x ∈ U t x\in U_{t} , we obtain 50 MC dropout predictions and use their mean and standard deviation to form a Gaussian predictive approximation. The probability of exceeding τ \tau then serves as the acquisition score. OAE. OAE uses optimistic estimates for batch discovery of candidates with high responses. We use its released Bayesian variant, which combines predictive means and uncertainty to select each batch after aligning predictions with the effect direction ( Pacchiano et al., 2023 ) . DiscoBAX. DiscoBAX targets a diverse subset of candidates with high responses and uses fantasized observations to estimate information gain about that subset. We use the authors’ surrogate training and acquisition implementation, with two hidden layers of 128 and 64 units and dropout 0.2 to match TDAL and PoH in network capacity. We evaluate this implementation on the four single gene perturbation tasks ( Lyle et al., 2023 ) . UCB. Upper Confidence Bound combines the predictive mean with a weighted uncertainty term. It favors candidates with high predicted effects or high uncertainty to balance exploitation and exploration ( Srinivas et al., 2010 ) . Expected Improvement (EI). EI estimates the expected improvement over the best effect observed so far and prioritizes candidates with larger expected gains ( Jones et al., 1998 ) . Thompson Sampling (TS). TS draws an effect function from the surrogate posterior and selects a batch by ranking unmeasured candidates under that draw, allowing model uncertainty to influence selection ( Thompson, 1933 ) . TopUncertain. TopUncertain ranks candidates by predictive standard deviation and allocates experiments to regions where the surrogate is most uncertain ( Mehrjou et al., 2022 ) . BADGE. BADGE selects diverse batches using k-means++ in a gradient representation space. We use the GeneDisco regression implementation and its proxy gradient representations ( Ash et al., 2020 ; Mehrjou et al., 2022 ) . CoreSet. CoreSet greedily expands geometric coverage in the candidate representation space extracted by the surrogate. It successively selects the candidate farthest from the existing selection centers ( Sener and Savarese, 2018 ) . KMeans-Embedding. This method uses k-means to cluster candidate representations extracted by the surrogate and selects candidates near cluster centers to represent different regions of the candidate space ( Mehrjou et al., 2022 ) . Random. Random samples uniformly without replacement from the current unmeasured pool, providing a reference for random screening. We construct landscapes in which hits are dispersed or concentrated in six clusters. The 17,660 candidates have fixed two-dimensional coordinates that are defined without phenotype information. KMeans divides these coordinates into six regions, each with a response peak at its center. Within each region, candidates closer to the center receive higher response scores. We sort candidates by distance, negate their ranks, and divide by the region’s hit quota. The quotas are 148 for one region and 147 for each of the other five, giving 883 hits in total. This scaling produces clusters of similar hit counts when no noise is added. We standardize the scores across candidates and mix them with independent standardized Gaussian noise. Increasing the noise contribution disperses hits away from the cluster centers. To keep phenotype values comparable, we rank the mixed scores and assign them the same fixed set of standard normal quantiles. The top 5% are designated as hits. All panels share axis ranges. Appendix F Ablation Studies and Selection Dynamics F.1 Component Ablations We compare five ablations with full TDAL on IFN- γ \gamma , IL-2, Kuzmin, and Horlbeck Jurkat. All methods share the surrogate configuration, fixed candidate representations, initial sets, experimental budgets, and five random seeds. Final cumulative hit counts are reported in Table 1 . Surrogate only. This ablation refits the same surrogate as TDAL after each round of observations but selects candidates with the highest p ^ t ​ ( x ) \hat{p}_{t}(x) without training an acquisition network, breaking exact probability ties at random. Its mean final recall is 0.54–13.01 percentage points lower than full TDAL across the four tasks. Updating response predictions alone therefore does not recover the full model’s discovery gains. Learning a selection function from batch feedback further improves screening. w/o adaptive w t w_{t} . This ablation retains successor value bootstrapping but replaces the adaptive Hellinger weight with 1, applying the same successor weight in every round. Mean final recall decreases by 3.88–9.03 percentage points across the four tasks. Giving successor value full weight at every round therefore performs less well than adjusting its contribution to the observed prediction changes. w/o LBO. This ablation disables LBO transitions constructed from the initial set, the associated initial TD training, and online LBO. The acquisition network learns only after receiving real batch feedback. Mean final recall is 0.42–5.54 percentage points lower than full TDAL across the four tasks. LBO reuses measured outcomes to provide training experience at initialization and supplement the single real transition obtained per online round. w/o successor. This ablation sets the training target to Y = R Y=R , retaining the hit probability term, acquisition network, and both real and LBO transitions. With surrogate predictions held fixed, the network fits the difference between the observed batch discovery count and its surrogate estimate. Mean final recall exceeds Surrogate only on all four tasks but is 0.15–1.21 percentage points lower than full TDAL. Current feedback can therefore train an effective selection function, while successor value bootstrapping further increases final discoveries. w/o anchor. This ablation uses q θ ​ ( s t , x ) = f θ ​ ( s t , c t ​ ( x ) ) q_{\theta}(s_{t},x)=f_{\theta}(s_{t},c_{t}(x)) . Both current and successor batch values sum network outputs without explicitly adding p ^ t ​ ( x ) \hat{p}_{t}(x) . Surrogate prediction features remain in the inputs. Relative to full TDAL, mean final recall decreases by 0.96 and 2.82 percentage points on IFN- γ \gamma and IL-2, respectively, and increases by 0.31 and 0.16 points on Kuzmin and Horlbeck Jurkat. The two single perturbation tasks each use 60 screening rounds, whereas the two combinatorial tasks each use 200. More rounds provide the acquisition network with more feedback and training opportunities. The explicit hit probability term is more beneficial on the two tasks with 60 rounds. This pattern is consistent with a useful probability reference early in learning and motivates gradually reducing its contribution as feedback accumulates.
