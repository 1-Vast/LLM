A systematic transcriptomic atlas and predictive framework for metabolite-driven T cell state transitions

These authors contributed equally

Lead contact

Summary

Gut microbial and endogenous metabolites are key regulators at the interface of host metabolism and immunity, yet their global effects on human T cell states remain poorly defined. Here, we profile primary human T cell responses to 364 endogenous and microbiota-derived metabolites using high-throughput digital RNA with perturbation of genes sequencing (DRUG-seq), revealing structured transcriptional trajectories spanning baseline, metabolically primed, and highly activated states. We develop DRUG-seq-PerturbFormer, a multi-task deep learning framework that quantitatively captures perturbation magnitude and directionality across these states. This analysis identifies a subset of metabolites that robustly reprogram T cell transcriptional programs. In a dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)-induced colitis model, representative candidates attenuated disease severity and were associated with suppression of inflammatory programs and partial restoration of immune homeostasis. Collectively, these findings show that metabolites act as signals shaping T cell function via transcription, nominating immunomodulatory targets for inflammatory diseases.

Introduction

The gut microbiota exerts a broad influence on host physiology by shaping metabolic, immunological, and inflammatory processes through a chemically diverse repertoire of small molecules.1 Many of these compounds arise from microbial transformation of dietary substrates, whereas others are synthesized endogenously or generated through host-microbe co-metabolism.2,3 These metabolites form a complex signaling interface that mediates bidirectional communication between microbial communities and the immune system.

T cells are central coordinators of adaptive immunity, and their functional states are stringently regulated by the metabolic environments they inhabit.4 Beyond antigen recognition through the T cell receptor (TCR), nutrient availability and metabolite sensing act as additional regulatory inputs that influence T cell activation and differentiation.5,6 The gastrointestinal tract represents a primary interface where T cells interact with a diverse chemical milieu of endogenous and microbiota-derived metabolites.7 Accumulating studies demonstrate that such metabolites modulate multiple aspects of T cell biology, including activation, effector differentiation, cytokine production, and tissue homeostasis.8 For instance, short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) from gut microbiota, including acetate, propionate, and butyrate, promote colonic regulatory T (Treg) cell differentiation through GPR43 signaling and histone deacetylase inhibition.9 SCFAs also modulate effector T cell subsets and attenuate airway inflammation by influencing immunometabolism.10 Tryptophan-derived indole metabolites similarly engage the aryl hydrocarbon receptor to regulate T helper 17 (Th17)/Treg balance and maintain mucosal homeostasis.11 Secondary bile acids generated from microbial modification of host bile acids signal through FXR and TGR5 to modulate epithelial integrity and innate immunity.12,13 Beyond classical metabolite classes, metabolomic studies have linked host-microbe co-metabolites, such as hippurate, to systemic inflammation and metabolic status,14 and polyamine metabolism has been implicated in T cell exhaustion and response to CAR-T (chimeric antigen receptor T-cell immunotherapy) therapy.15,16 Despite these advances, the overarching organizational principles that govern how diverse metabolites reshape T cell transcriptional states at scale, as well as how such information can be systematically leveraged for translational prioritization, remain incompletely defined.

Technical limitations have also contributed to this gap. Although recent advances in high-throughput perturbation profiling have enabled large-scale mapping of ligand-induced transcriptional responses in immune cells,17,18 these approaches have rarely been applied to microbial metabolites. Existing studies typically assess small numbers of metabolites, rely on bulk readouts that obscure heterogeneity, or lack standardized analytical frameworks for broad comparison across conditions. Notably, no unified resource currently exists that systematically compiles immune-related transcriptional signatures elicited by gut microbial metabolites. This absence of an integrated dataset limits the ability to identify convergent or divergent transcriptional programs, hinders cross-study comparability, and constrains efforts to quantitatively prioritize metabolite candidates. In parallel, computational models capable of predicting transcriptional outcomes induced by untested metabolites are still in their infancy. Current machine-learning approaches are either trained on limited perturbation datasets or insufficiently capture the complexity of immune regulatory networks, making it challenging to extrapolate beyond experimentally assayed compounds or to infer latent determinants of metabolite-driven T cell state transitions.

To address these challenges and systematically map the metabolic-transcriptional landscape of T cells, we utilized digital RNA with perturbation of genes sequencing (DRUG-seq), a high-throughput technology developed to sequence total RNA directly from cell lysates in a 384-well format, bypassing cost-prohibitive purification steps.19,20,21 This allowed us to profile the responses of human T cells to a library of 364 metabolites. This large-scale screen revealed that metabolite-induced transcriptional responses are not randomly distributed, but instead organized into a limited number of recurring transcriptional programs, prominently involving coordinated activation of metabolic pathways and TCR-associated signaling modules.

In parallel with advances in high-throughput perturbation profiling, deep learning-based modeling has emerged as a powerful approach for predicting complex transcriptional responses from high-dimensional gene expression data. In particular, neural network architectures based on multi-task transformers have demonstrated a strong capacity to capture gene-gene dependencies and generalize to unseen perturbations.22 In this context, we developed DRUG-seq-PerturbFormer (DPF), a deep learning framework that integrates experimental perturbation data with predictive modeling, to enable quantitative prioritization of metabolite-induced transcriptional effects across T cell states.

Together, this integrated experimental and computational framework generates a comprehensive, high-resolution atlas of metabolite-induced transcriptional responses, providing a foundation for exploring how diverse metabolites reshape human T cell programs and for guiding downstream functional and translational studies.

Results

Systematic perturbation profiling reveals T cell transcriptional programs induced by 364 metabolites

To systematically characterize how endogenous and microbiota-associated metabolites modulate T cell transcriptional states, we established a high-throughput perturbation profiling platform integrating DRUG-seq, transcriptional-state characterization, differential expression analysis, and downstream modeling (Figures 1A and S1 and Table S1). Primary human activated CD3+ T cells were treated with 364 endogenous or gut microbiota-derived metabolites, alongside DMSO controls. Dimensionality reduction by uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) revealed a continuous yet structured landscape of metabolite-induced transcriptional variation (Figure 1B and Table S2). Unsupervised clustering identified eight discrete transcriptional states (C0–C7), representing distinct response modules elicited by metabolite perturbation (Figure 1B, bottom). These clusters captured coordinated expression programs spanning activation, cytokine signaling, metabolic adaptation, and stress responses.

Marker gene analysis highlighted unique transcriptional signatures characterizing each state (Figure 1C). Among these states, C0 showed features of a strongly activated program, whereas C6 was characterized by prominent interferon- and stress-associated signatures. Cluster composition analysis further showed that DMSO controls were relatively enriched in C6, whereas metabolite-treated cells predominated across most other clusters (Figures 1D and S2B). These findings indicate that a substantial fraction of metabolites elicit measurable and state-specific transcriptional remodeling in T cells.

To further interpret the biological relevance of each cluster, we conducted Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis on cluster-defining genes (Figure 1E and Table S3). The resulting landscape revealed heterogeneous pathway engagement. For instance, cluster C0, which displayed a strongly activated transcriptional phenotype, was enriched for pathways involved in TCR signaling, antigen processing and presentation, cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, and Th1/Th2/Th17 differentiation, indicating coordinated activation of T cell effector programs. By contrast, C1 and C6 were enriched for interferon- and stress-associated processes but represented distinct transcriptional clusters. C6 contained most DMSO-treated samples and was therefore designated as the baseline cluster. Although C1 and C4 were not similarly enriched for DMSO, the metabolites in these clusters exhibited transcriptional perturbation patterns similar to those observed in C6. Inspection of individual genes further demonstrated the diversity of T cell states induced by metabolite perturbation (Figure 1F). Key surface markers and signaling molecules, including CD3E, CD247, TRAC, TRBC1, LCK, STAT3, and immune regulators such as HLA-DRB1, TNFSF10, and HSP90AA1, showed distinct expression gradients across the UMAP space, aligning with the inferred transcriptional modules.

Metabolite perturbations converge on three higher order T cell activation states

To dissect how individual metabolites modulate T cell transcriptional programs, we first examined differential gene expression signatures across all treatments. The cluster-specific transcriptional and pathway programs converged into three higher order response states, termed baseline resting, metabolic active, and TCR-high activation (Table S4). Across metabolites, the numbers of upregulated and downregulated genes were strongly correlated (Pearson’s r = 0.98, p < 2.2 × 10−16), with transcriptional perturbation progressively increasing from the baseline resting state to the metabolic active and TCR-high activation states (Figure 2A). Metabolites were then ranked within each state according to total differentially expressed gene (DEG) count, revealing marked variation in perturbation strength among metabolites occupying the same transcriptional state (Figure 2B). Within the TCR-high activation state, top-ranked metabolites such as 2-methylbutanoic acid, tocotrienol, resveratrol, and indole-3-pyruvic acid were associated with strong induction of activation-related genes, including IL2RA, CD5, LCK, and CCR7 (Figures 2C and S3A). By contrast, metabolites assigned to the metabolic active state preferentially enhanced bioenergetic genes, such as NDUFA11 and NDUFB8, consistent with selective engagement of metabolic programs. To define the gene programs underlying these higher order states, we merged significantly DEGs from all pairwise comparisons to construct a perturbation-responsive gene universe (N = 2,541) (Figure S3B). KEGG enrichment analysis of this gene universe revealed that upregulated genes were significantly enriched in T cell activation pathways (e.g., TCR signaling and Th1/Th2 differentiation), while downregulated genes were associated with more basal cellular and metabolic processes (Figure S3C). When mapped back onto the eight transcriptional clusters identified in Figure 1, these genes showed clear state-specific organization: activation-associated genes were most prominently expressed in cluster C0, whereas metabolic genes mapped preferentially to clusters C2 and C3 (Figure 2D). This result links the three-state framework to the finer cluster structure defined in the initial perturbation screen.

To further quantify these states, we computed three signature-based metrics, including a T cell activation score (T score), TCR signaling score (TCR score), and metabolic activation (MA) score (Figures 2E and S3D–S3F). Cluster C0 showed the highest T score and TCR score, whereas clusters C2 and C3 displayed elevated MA scores, highlighting functionally distinct metabolite-driven phenotypes. When projected back onto the transcriptional landscape, these scores organized cells into the three major states—baseline resting, metabolic active, and TCR-high activation, indicating that metabolite perturbations can be interpreted as shifts along a structured activation continuum rather than as unrelated transcriptional outcomes (Figure 2F).

Hierarchical regulatory programs organize metabolite-induced T cell state transitions

To further define the regulatory logic and functional consequences underlying the three metabolite-induced states, we integrated regulatory network inference, cell identity enrichment, and trajectory modeling. Regulon-level analysis revealed a structured hierarchy of transcriptional regulators across the state continuum. The baseline resting state was marked by higher activity of homeostasis-associated regulators, including STAT3 and PURA, whereas the intermediate metabolic active state showed preferential enrichment of KLF2- and MXD3-associated regulons, consistent with a metabolically primed intermediate program. In contrast, the TCR-high activation state was associated with increased activity of stress- and inflammation-related regulators, including ATF4, SREBF2, RELA, and JUND (Figures 3A and 3B). Consistent with this regulatory organization, gene set variation analysis revealed state-dependent remodeling of T cell functional identity. We first quantified the enrichment of 13 T cell subtype signatures across the transcriptional landscape (Figure 3C). When these signatures were compared across the three major states, distinct patterns emerged between CD4+ and CD8+ compartments (Figure 3D). Activated CD8+ T cell and effector memory CD8+ T cell signatures increased stepwise from baseline resting to TCR-high activation (p < 0.0001), whereas activated CD4+ and effector memory CD4+ T cell signatures declined progressively across the same transition (p < 0.0001). These data indicate that metabolite perturbations differentially reshape CD4+ and CD8+ programs rather than uniformly amplifying all activated T cell states. Meanwhile, both CD4+ and CD8+ central memory signatures showed an upward trend across states, suggesting partial preservation or enrichment of memory-associated programs. Among helper T cell signatures, Th1 remained relatively stable, whereas Th17 increased progressively, pointing to selective remodeling of helper-lineage programs along the activation continuum.

To determine whether these relationships reflected a continuous progression rather than discrete and unrelated cell groups, we next reconstructed the state trajectory using two independent analytical frameworks. Both approaches supported a continuous path originating from the baseline-enriched region, traversing an intermediate bridge dominated by metabolically active states, and terminating in the highly activated branch (Figures 3E, 3F, and S4A–S4C). In parallel, differentiation-potential analysis indicated a progressive reduction in transcriptional stemness from resting-like to highly activated states. Notably, metabolites with weaker perturbation strength remained enriched near the trajectory root, whereas stronger perturbagens were associated with cells positioned toward the terminal branch. Together, these analyses support a model in which metabolite-induced T cell states are arranged along a progressive activation continuum governed by hierarchical regulatory programs.

A multi-task transformer model learns, ranks, and generalizes metabolite perturbation strength

Having defined the major metabolite-responsive T cell states and their associated activation continuum, we next asked whether these perturbational features could be learned in a predictive framework. To quantitatively model the effects of endogenous and gut microbial metabolites on T cell transcriptional states, we developed a multi-task transformer encoder, DPF, which integrates gene-level expression profiles with multi-output prediction heads (Figure 4A). To capture both perturbation strength and state progression, we selected three continuous metrics—T cell activation score (T score), differentiation potential (CytoTRACE2), and pseudotime—as regression targets, as these scores collectively reflect the depth of T cell activation and developmental progression. Furthermore, to sharpen the contrast during model training, we merged the metabolic active and TCR-high activation states into a unified perturbed label to compare against the resting state. This multi-task strategy enables a unified latent representation of metabolite-induced transcriptional changes. Training trajectories showed stable optimization of both regression and classification losses, with convergence achieved around epoch 47 (Figure 4B). Visualization of the learned latent space separated perturbed from resting states (Figure 4C). Metabolites with strong predicted effects, including indole-3-pyruvic acid, folic acid, 4-methylcatechol, and 2,3-dihydroxy-3-methylbutanoic acid, localized furthest from the DMSO centroid, whereas weak perturbagens, such as homogentisic acid and methionine sulfoxide, remained near the resting-state cluster. We therefore combined Euclidean distance, reflecting perturbation magnitude, and cosine distance, reflecting direction, into a global perturbation score. Parameter sensitivity analysis identified α = 0.25 as the optimal weighting (Figure S5E). Ranking all 364 metabolites using this score identified 148 high-tier metabolites (Figure 4D and Tables S5 and S6). The DPF ranking was broadly concordant with transcriptional perturbation breadth, with perturbation scores strongly correlated with total DEG counts across metabolites (Spearman’s r = 0.76, p < 2.2 × 10−16; Figure 4E). The remaining rank differences may reflect the distinct information captured by the two approaches, as DEG count measures the breadth of significant transcriptional changes, whereas DPF integrates continuous expression patterns associated with T cell state progression. Integrated gradients further identified model-contributing genes related to cytoskeletal remodeling, apoptosis, and mitochondrial respiration, including COTL1, ACTG1, ADD3, CASP3, and NDUFB4 (Figure S6A). Exploratory enrichment of the signed attribution ranking placed Rho GTPase and innate immune programs toward the positive end, and PD-1 and TNF signaling toward the lower end (Figures S6B–S6E). Because these patterns reflect contributions to model output rather than differential expression, they were interpreted as exploratory attribution trends rather than evidence of pathway activation or inhibition.

To test whether this framework generalized beyond the training data, we performed bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) on T cells treated with 12 representative metabolites selected from the active perturbagen group (Table S7). When applied to this independent dataset, DPF consistently assigned higher perturbation scores to metabolite-treated samples than to DMSO controls (Figure 4F). This external validation was supported by concordant upregulation of key activation-associated genes, including CD3E, LCK, and JUND, across these treatment conditions (Figure S7A). From this validated set, we prioritized four candidates, including indole-3-pyruvic acid (IPA), folic acid (FA), hippuric acid (HA), and 4-methylcatechol (4-MC), for in-depth dose-response characterization (1, 10, and 100 μM), selected for their high perturbation scores and low prediction standard deviation. DPF revealed distinct compound-specific concentration-associated trends rather than a uniform response pattern. Hippuric acid and 4-methylcatechol showed increasing mean predicted perturbation scores across the tested concentrations, whereas folic acid and indole-3-pyruvic acid displayed non-monotonic trends, with lower mean scores at 10 μM than at 1 or 100 μM (Figures 4G and S7B and Table S8). Together, these results support the ability of DPF not only to classify perturbation states, but also to rank metabolite activity, generalize across data modalities, and capture quantitative variation across experimental conditions.

Selected metabolites alleviate DSS-induced acute colitis

Having identified a set of highly active metabolites through perturbation profiling and model-based prioritization, we next asked whether these candidates could also cf. functional benefit in vivo. To this end, we selected four representative metabolites, including indole-3-pyruvic acid, folic acid, hippuric acid, and 4-methylcatechol, for evaluation in a dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)-induced acute colitis model (Table S9).

Across all DSS-treated groups, body weight declined over time, with no significant differences among treatment arms (Figure 5A). Despite these similar weight trajectories, clinical disease measures diverged after metabolite administration. Both 4-methylcatechol and folic acid markedly suppressed DSS-induced increases in bloating scores and disease activity index (DAI) compared with the PBS group (Figures 5B and 5C), suggesting an amelioration of intestinal inflammation. Metabolite treatment shifted the blood transcriptome away from the DSS-associated inflammatory state. In particular, 4-methylcatechol and folic acid moved closer to water controls in principle component space (Figure 6A). Global and metabolite-specific expression profiles showed reduced interferon-stimulated and innate inflammatory programs, accompanied by partial recovery of adaptive immune and chromatin-regulatory programs (Figures 6B and 6C). Pathway analysis further showed attenuation of inflammatory response and myeloid differentiation programs, together with relative restoration of methylation- and T cell differentiation-related pathways (Figures S8C–S8F). Folic acid showed the broadest reversal pattern, whereas hippuric acid exhibited more partial effects, in line with its intermediate phenotypic protection.

Immune deconvolution supported a coordinated shift from myeloid-dominant inflammation toward lymphoid-associated programs (Figure 6D and Table S11). Treatment with 4-methylcatechol and indole-3-pyruvic acid was associated with higher inferred proportions of Th cells, naive CD4+ T cells, and their associated activation score levels, whereas folic acid and hippuric acid were associated with higher inferred proportions of naive and central memory CD8+ T cells. In contrast, DSS+PBS animals showed prominent enrichment of neutrophils, inflammatory monocytes, granulocytes, and macrophage-like inflammatory signatures (Figure 6D). These changes were supported at the gene level: myeloid and interferon-response genes, including S100a8, S100a9, Mmp9, Spi1, Isg15, Ifitm3, Isg20, and Ube2b, were reduced following metabolite treatment, whereas T cell-associated genes (Cd3e, Trac, and Il7r), B cell markers(Cd19, Cd79a, Cd79b, and Ms4a1), and epigenetic regulators (Mettl3, Mettl14, Ezh2, and Prmt5) showed partial recovery (Figure 6E). Together, these findings indicate that metabolite-mediated protection in DSS colitis is associated with suppression of systemic inflammatory programs and restoration of T cell-related and broader adaptive immune features.

Discussion

Our results establish a systematic framework for linking metabolite perturbations to T cell state transitions, predictive modeling, and in vivo functional validation. The gut microbiome and host metabolome together generate a large and chemically diverse pool of small molecules that can shape immune behavior, yet metabolite-immune relationships have been difficult to define systematically with conventional low-throughput approaches. Here we address this gap by combining high-throughput perturbation profiling with predictive modeling to map how metabolites reshape transcriptional programs in human T cells. Using DRUG-seq, we profiled 364 endogenous and microbiota-associated metabolites and organized their effects into a structured transcriptional state space. We then used a multi-task transformer model, DPF, to quantify perturbation strength, prioritize candidate immunomodulators, and guide in vivo validation, which we demonstrated in a DSS-induced acute colitis model. This integrated framework provides both an interpretable atlas of metabolite-induced programs and a scalable route to nominate candidates for downstream functional testing.

A central contribution of the atlas is that it establishes a shared transcriptional reference enabling direct comparison across chemically diverse metabolites. Within this reference, well-studied metabolite classes such as SCFAs, indole derivatives, and bile-acid-related metabolites aligned with activation-associated or cytokine-responsive transcriptional programs, supporting the biological fidelity of the platform. Equally informative, we identified high-impact perturbagens outside these canonical families, including 2-methylbutanoic acid, tocotrienol, and resveratrol, together with indole-3-pyruvic acid, indicating that immunometabolic regulation is not restricted to the metabolite categories most frequently studied. Notably, these metabolites converged on a coherent activation-like output, exemplified by induction of IL2RA and proximal signaling components such as LCK, suggesting that diverse metabolites can funnel into shared T cell activation circuitry rather than generating unrelated signatures. In this way, our dataset complements earlier high-throughput perturbation efforts such as DRUG-seq,19,20 while extending deep perturbation learning concepts into the metabolite domain.23,24

Beyond cataloging responses, the screen revealed that metabolite effects are recurrent and modular. Rather than producing diffuse and idiosyncratic signatures, metabolites concentrated into discrete transcriptional programs that could be interpreted as coordinated activation of metabolic pathways and TCR-associated signaling modules.25 These programs were anchored by interpretable molecular features, with the TCR-high activation state tracking upregulation of core T cell signaling and activation genes such as CD3E, CD247, LCK, and IL2RA, while the metabolic active state preferentially engaged mitochondrial and bioenergetic genes including NDUFA11 and NDUFB8. This anchoring makes the state space actionable by directly linking metabolite ranking to testable pathway hypotheses, rather than to abstract clustering labels. These programs define a continuum of states spanning baseline-like profiles through metabolically activated intermediates to highly responsive endpoints, suggesting that metabolites can shift T cells along an activation trajectory in a graded manner. Along this continuum, STAT3 and KLF2 mark baseline-like programs, whereas ATF4 and JUND track progression toward metabolically activated and highly responsive states, providing practical molecular landmarks for mechanistic follow-up. This framing provides a practical way to compare metabolites not only by the number of responsive genes, but by the directionality and state identity of the programs they induce.

Trajectory inference and regulon analysis provided additional context for these state transitions and highlighted regulatory nodes associated with different portions of the continuum. The inferred hierarchy highlights concrete candidate control points for mechanistic testing, with baseline-associated regulators such as STAT3 and KLF2 and terminal modules involving ATF4 and JUND, alongside RELA within the high-response program. This pattern is consistent with known requirements for T cells to acquire metabolic fitness to function in nutrient-limited inflammatory settings.26 Along the inferred trajectory, STAT3 activity decreased in parallel with induction of metabolic programs, including upregulation of mitochondrial genes such as NDUFB8.27 In contrast, IRF1 remained relatively low during early MA, a pattern that may constrain interferon-associated transcriptional programs while metabolic capacity is being established.28 These observations support a staged model in which metabolic licensing and inflammatory transcription can be partially uncoupled, although direct causality will require targeted perturbation of these regulators and upstream sensors.

Changes in subtype-associated transcriptional signatures further suggest that metabolite-driven programs are coupled to functional remodeling. Central memory signatures increased across states, consistent with the possibility that some metabolites promote metabolic competence while preserving features linked to long-term persistence.29 Conversely, CD4-associated effector-memory signatures declined while CD8-associated effector-memory signatures increased, indicating non-uniform remodeling across CD4 and CD8 compartments. Because these are signature-based inferences rather than lineage-tracing measurements, they are best interpreted as shifts in program utilization rather than definitive differentiation pathways. Nonetheless, they reinforce that metabolite perturbations do not simply amplify generic activation, but reshape the balance of functional programs across T cell states. The gradual increase in Th17 signatures with relatively stable Th1 signatures similarly points to selective remodeling. This trend is compatible with established metabolic dependencies of Th17 differentiation and RORγt-associated programs,30 and it motivates focused testing of whether top perturbagens (e.g., folic acid) preferentially stabilize Th17-linked transcriptional modules under defined conditions.

A second major contribution of this study is the DPF model, which achieved strong performance in predicting metabolite-induced transcriptional perturbations (AUC = 0.987). Recent transcriptome-scale modeling studies have shown that self-attention-based architectures can learn high-order gene-gene relationships and shared regulatory patterns from high-dimensional expression data, providing a general framework for inference across perturbations and cellular contexts.31,32,33 Within this landscape, DPF functions as a practical prioritization layer for metabolite perturbation data: it integrates multi-task outputs that reflect activation depth and state progression, enabling systematic ranking of candidates from a large chemical space and supporting inference beyond experimentally assayed conditions. The biological relevance of this ranking was supported by the presence of well-characterized immunomodulatory metabolites within the high tier. These included histamine, which shapes T cell polarization34; ATP and succinate, which connect extracellular and metabolic cues to Th17 differentiation and inflammatory signaling35,36; and indole-3-carboxaldehyde and 3-hydroxyanthranilic acid, which regulate mucosal AhR-IL-22 signaling and activated T cell responses,37,38 respectively. These established signals anchor the DPF ranking in known immunometabolic biology, while the broader high-tier set extends prioritization to less-characterized candidates.

Importantly, the model’s utility is not limited to assigning a “perturbed” or “resting” label; it also captures compound-specific dynamics that can inform experimental design. In our dataset, DPF-ranked candidates such as indole-3-pyruvic acid and folic acid clustered at the extreme end of the perturbed manifold, consistent with large Euclidean distances from DMSO controls and concordant induction of activation modules. In follow-up concentration profiling of prioritized candidates, we observed distinct concentration-associated trends across metabolites, including apparent non-monotonic trends for folic acid and indole-3-pyruvic acid. These divergent patterns may reflect compound-specific concentration-dependent behavior potentially related to receptor-mediated sensing pathways, such as AhR-dependent signaling, or from concentration-dependent engagement of core metabolic processes, including one-carbon metabolism.39,40,41 Further profiling across a broader and more densely sampled concentration range will be important for defining the reproducibility and biological basis of these patterns.

The value of this prediction-to-validation loop was supported by in vivo testing. Representative DPF-prioritized metabolites were evaluated in a DSS-induced acute colitis model42; all four metabolites, 4-methylcatechol, folic acid, hippuric acid, and indole-3-pyruvic acid, showed measurable protection, reflected by lower disease activity, reduced colon shortening, and improved histopathology compared with DSS+PBS controls, supporting their potential as modulators of intestinal inflammation. These phenotypic outcomes firmly establish their in vivo efficacy but remain mechanistically agnostic. Several plausible upstream sensors have been proposed in previous studies, such as Nrf2-Keap1/NF-κB axes for 4-methylcatechol,43,44,45 AhR signaling for indole-3-pyruvic acid,46,47,48 one-carbon metabolism/MTHFD2 for folic acid,49,50,51,52 and context-dependent immunoregulation for hippuric acid.53 However, because none of these specific pathways were directly perturbed or pharmacologically validated in our current experiments, we regard these connections as hypothetical frameworks to guide future mechanistic dissection, rather than as definitive conclusions drawn from our study.

More importantly, our transcriptomic data revealed a convergent downstream output across the effective metabolites, namely a global transcriptional shift consistent with suppression of myeloid-driven inflammatory programs.54 We also observed restoration of expression patterns involving epigenetic regulators such as Mettl3,55 and across four validated anti-inflammatory metabolites, a shared signature consistent with re-engagement of the METTL3/14 and EZH2 regulatory axis emerged. While these findings nominate epigenetic regulation as a plausible convergence point, establishing whether these changes reflect direct, causal remodeling of chromatin or RNA modification will require orthogonal measurements at the protein and chromatin levels. Nonetheless, this shared transcriptional rebalancing suggests that these chemically diverse metabolites may share common state-regulatory programs, rather than operating through entirely distinct linear pathways implicated by previous researches. Defining whether the proposed upstream sensors that actually mediate these effects, and how they couple to the epigenetic and transcriptional shifts represents a prioritized avenue for subsequent mechanistic studies.

In summary, by integrating systematic perturbation profiling, deep-learning-based prioritization, and in vivo validation, this study delivers a scalable framework for mapping immunometabolic regulation. The atlas and DPF model together provide an experimentally grounded and computationally actionable foundation to identify metabolites that reshape T cell states, clarify regulatory programs underlying these transitions, and nominate candidates with translational potential.

Limitations of the study

Our study provides an experimentally grounded atlas and a practical prediction-to-validation framework for metabolite-driven T cell programs, and it also highlights several areas where additional work will further strengthen interpretation and broaden applicability. Because the atlas was generated in a standardized in vitro system using activated human T cells, it necessarily abstracts away some elements of the physiological environments in which metabolites act, including dynamic nutrient availability, cytokine gradients, and multicellular crosstalk with epithelial, stromal, and myeloid compartments. To enable systematic comparison across the metabolite library, all 364 metabolites were screened at a uniform concentration of 4 μM, providing a common reference for identifying and ranking metabolite-induced transcriptional perturbations. Because physiological abundance, tissue exposure, bioavailability, and dose-response relationships differ among metabolites, these rankings are best interpreted as relative perturbation strengths under the defined screening conditions. Extension to larger and more diverse donor cohorts, together with concentration-response profiling across physiologically relevant ranges, will further establish the generalizability of the identified metabolite-responsive programs and refine the biological interpretation of individual metabolites.

The transcriptome offers a sensitive and scalable readout of state remodeling, but translating transcriptional programs into definitive signaling, protein-level regulation, and functional phenotypes remains a recognized challenge in immunometabolic studies, making state-resolved functional characterization an important direction for our future work. Building on the transcriptional framework established here, our future work will therefore focus on systematic state-resolved functional characterization to determine how these molecular programs shape distinct T cell phenotypes and immune functions. Along similar lines, regulatory features suggested by trajectory and regulon analyses, as well as the apparent convergence on epigenetic regulators such as METTL3/14 and EZH2, are supported by consistent expression patterns in our data and represent strong mechanistic hypotheses. Establishing direct causality requires targeted perturbation experiments and orthogonal measurements at the chromatin and protein levels, which can be technically demanding in primary T cells. The DPF model achieves high performance within the chemical and biological space represented in training data, yet continued testing in additional activation contexts and across immune compartments will be important to define the scope of generalization for untested metabolites. Moreover, our in vivo validation was limited to a small set of prioritized metabolites in a DSS-induced colitis model and was intended as a proof of concept. Because the discovery screen was performed in activated human T cells whereas validation was conducted in mice, the two components provide complementary rather than directly equivalent evidence. Species-specific differences in immune organization, metabolite disposition, tissue exposure, microbiota, and inflammatory context may influence the observed responses. The exclusive use of male mice may further limit generalizability, given the established influence of sex in IBD (Inflammatory Bowel Disease) models. Future studies in human-relevant systems, both sexes, and additional inflammatory or malignant settings, together with characterization of proximal sensors and pharmacological constraints, will be needed to assess the translational potential of these findings.

Method details

T cells extraction and drug treatment

To mitigate the impact of individual variability on the analytical results, peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated from peripheral blood obtained from two healthy volunteers (male, aged from 21 to 29) using density gradient centrifugation. T cells were purified from PBMCs using the EasySep™ Human CD3 Positive Selection Kit II (STEMCELL Technologies, 17851) and cultured in X-VIVO® 15 medium (Lonza, 04-418Q) supplemented with 5% FBS (Vazyme, F102-01) and 400 U/mL Human Recombinant Interleukin 2 (STEMCELL Technologies, 78036). To induce activation, T cells were stimulated with T Cell TransAct™ (Miltenyi Biotec, 130-128-758) for 48 hours.

Following activation, the cell suspension was adjusted to 1 × 106 cells/mL, and 50 μL aliquots were dispensed into 384-well cell culture plates using a MultiDrop Combi dispenser (ThermoFisher, 5840300). A pre-configured compound library containing 364 metabolites and 20 DMSO controls was utilized for the screen. Metabolites (200 nL/well) were transferred using an Explorer G3 workstation (PerkinElmer) to achieve a final concentration of 4 μM. This uniform concentration was selected to enable standardized high-throughput screening and direct comparison across the metabolite library, while balancing the detection of biological effects with minimal cytotoxicity. Although it may exceed physiological circulating levels for certain metabolites, it was intended as a common screening condition rather than to reproduce the physiological exposure of each individual metabolite.

Cell lysis, library construction and sequencing for DRUG-seq

Fourty-eight hours after drug treatment, cells in each well were centrifuged at 2000 rpm for 10 minutes, and the supernatant was removed. Then, cell lysis and mRNA library construction for the T cells in each well were performed using a modified DRUG-seq library preparation method, starting with reverse transcription of cell lysates using barcoded primers, followed by pooled purification and exonuclease treatment to remove unwanted nucleic acids. cDNA is then amplified by PCR and purified. An aliquot of the preamplified material is tagmented, purified, and subjected to a second PCR with indexing primers. The final libraries are purified, size-selected for 200-600 bp fragments, and assessed for concentration and average size (∼300 bp) before sequencing.19,69 To uniquely identify transcripts from all 384 wells, a combinatorial indexing strategy was employed, utilizing 96 well-specific barcodes introduced during reverse transcription and four plate-specific indices added during the final PCR amplification.

Library size distribution and concentration were assessed using the 4200 TapeStation (Agilent Technologies) and Qubit 3 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific), respectively. After confirming the fragmentation profile and obtaining quantitative data for each library, libraries were pooled in proportion to their concentrations and subjected to sequencing on the MGISEQ-2000 platform (MGI).

RNA extraction and library construction for bulk RNA-seq

Mouse peripheral blood and T cells were lysed in RNA isolator Total RNA Extraction Reagent (Vazyme, R401-01). We used Mini Total RNA Extraction Kit (Jianshi Biotechnology, TR205) for total RNA extraction and Fast CE RNA-seq Lib Prep Kit (ABclonal, RK20311) for cDNA library synthesis according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The following steps are the same as above.

Animals and treatment

The mice were randomly divided into six groups on average: blank control group, model control group, and four treatment groups. Blank control mice received normal drinking water. For the DSS-induced colitis model, mice were administered with 2.5% Dextran sulfate sodium salt (MW 4500-5500, TargetMol, T13647) in drinking water for 6 days, followed by 2 days of regular drinking water. We obtained four types of drug powders: 4-Methylcatechol (TargetMol, T4779), Hippuric acid (TargetMol, T4815), Folic acid (TargetMol, T0062), and Indole-3-pyruvic acid (TargetMol, T4939). Four treatment groups were intraperitoneally injected with a certain dose (4-Methylcatechol: 20mg/kg mouse weight; Hippuric acid: 3mg/kg mouse weight; Folic acid: 20mg/kg mouse weight; Indole-3-pyruvic acid: 20mg/kg mouse weight) of drug solution dissolved in PBS every day, while the blank control group and the model control group were injected with an equal volume of PBS every day for solvent control. The dose of hippuric acid was limited by its poor solubility, while the other three metabolites could be readily formulated in PBS at higher concentrations and were therefore administered at a common exploratory dose of 20 mg/kg/day, chosen to provide a consistent proof-of-concept condition across these compounds informed by prior in vivo use70,71,72 where available.

The mice were sacrificed on day 8. Body weight loss, rectal bleeding, and diarrhea were monitored and recorded daily from Day 0 to Day 8. The scoring criteria were as follows: Weight loss: 0, none; 1, 1-5%; 2, 5-10%; 3, 10-20%; 4, >20%. Stool consistency: 0, well-formed pellets; 1, semisolid and not adherent to the anus; 2, semiformed and adherent to the anus; 4, liquid stools adherent to the anus with diarrhea. Bleeding score: 0, no blood in stool; 1, occult or faint blood; 2, clearly visible blood; 4, gross rectal bleeding. The Disease Activity Index (DAI) score for each mouse was calculated by summing the scores for weight loss, stool consistency, and bleeding to assess overall disease severity.73

Histological analysis

Colon tissues were fixed by immersion in a solution containing 4% paraformaldehyde and subsequently paraffin-embedded. Sections of 5 μm thickness were prepared, deparaffinized, rehydrated, and analyzed using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining to evaluate the extent of colon damage and inflammation. Histological alterations were assessed using a cumulative scoring system. Four scores were assigned based on the degree of epithelial damage: 0, normal morphology; 1, loss of goblet cells; 2, loss of goblet cells in large regions; 3, loss of crypts; 4, loss of crypts in large areas. Additionally, the degree of inflammatory cell infiltration was graded as follows: 0, no infiltration; 1, infiltration around the crypt base; 2, infiltration extending to the muscularis mucosa; 3, extensive infiltration into the muscularis mucosa accompanied by significant edema; 4, infiltration spreading to the submucosa.73

Bioinformatics workflow

Processing of raw reads for DRUG-seq

The raw paired-end sequencing data obtained from the DRUG-seq experiments were processed using a custom bioinformatics pipeline. Initially, data quality was assessed using FastQC (v0.12.1). To ensure accurate well-based demultiplexing, a comprehensive barcode whitelist was generated using a custom script, allowing for a one-base pair mismatch tolerance. Well barcodes and Unique Molecular Identifiers (UMIs) were extracted from the first read using UMI-tools (v1.1.2) based on this whitelist, with cell barcode error correction enabled. The reads were subsequently trimmed and filtered using Fastp (v0.23.2)57 to remove adapter sequences and low-quality bases. The resulting clean reads were aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38/hg38) using the STAR aligner (v2.7.11b). To quantify gene expression, featureCounts (v2.0.1)59 was employed to assign mapped reads to genomic features using the -R BAM parameter, which tags aligned reads with gene assignment information. Finally, the gene-tagged BAM files were sorted using SAMtools (v1.21),74 and UMI-tools count was utilized to deduplicate PCR artifacts and generate a digital gene expression matrix by counting unique molecules per gene for each well.

DRUG-seq transcriptomic analysis

The digital gene expression matrices generated by UMI-tools were processed and analyzed using the Seurat R package (v5.0).61 The two donor-derived screens comprised eight 96-well plates in total, with each plate containing 91 metabolite-treated wells and five DMSO-control wells (Table S1). To ensure data quality, wells with fewer than 150 detected Unique Molecular Identifiers (UMIs) were excluded from downstream analysis. To reduce plate-associated technical variation during dimensionality reduction and clustering, the RNA assay was split by physical plate, and each of the eight plate-level datasets was independently normalized and variance-stabilized using SCTransform with the glmGamPoi implementation and 3,000 variable features. Principal component analysis was performed on the SCT-normalized data, followed by integration of the plate-level datasets using reciprocal PCA. The resulting integrated representation was used for UMAP visualization and unsupervised clustering based on the shared nearest-neighbor graph. Cluster-specific marker genes were identified using the FindAllMarkers function on the SCT-normalized assay, with significance criteria set at an adjusted p-value < 0.05, an average log2 fold change > 1, and a minimum expression in 25% of wells within the cluster. Functional annotation of these markers was conducted using the clusterProfiler R package (v4.14.6)63 to identify enriched KEGG pathways (Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted p < 0.05). To define the transcriptomic baseline representing the homeostatic state, we evaluated the distribution of control samples (e.g., DMSO-treated wells) across the identified clusters; a Fisher’s exact test was applied to each cluster, and clusters showing a statistically significant enrichment of control wells (p < 0.001) were designated as the “Baseline” state, serving as the reference for characterizing drug-induced perturbations.

Identification of metabolite-induced transcriptomic perturbations

Iterative pairwise differential expression analyses were performed using the DESeq2 R package (v1.46.0)62 to characterize metabolite-induced transcriptional changes. Each metabolite-treated group was compared specifically against its corresponding intra-plate DMSO controls using raw UMI count matrices. This comparison-specific design was adopted to minimize plate-associated technical variation while preserving metabolite-specific transcriptional perturbation patterns. Genes with no expression were pre-filtered. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified based on a threshold of adjusted p-value < 0.05 and |log2 fold change| > log2(1.5). The lists of significantly up- or downregulated genes from all metabolite-specific comparisons were combined by union to construct the “Perturbed Gene Universe” (N = 2,541). This gene set represents an inclusive collection of genes responsive to at least one metabolite perturbation and was used as the feature space for downstream DPF model training. Because multiple-testing correction was performed separately within each metabolite-versus-DMSO comparison, the Perturbed Gene Universe should not be interpreted as a globally FDR-controlled catalogue of DEGs across all gene-metabolite tests. Functional states were quantified using Seurat's AddModuleScore: a T cell activation Score (Tscore) and Energy-metabolic activation score (MAscore) were calculated based on relevant GO gene sets, while a TCR Score (TCRscore) was derived from the expression of TRBC1, TRBC2, and TRAC.

Functional dynamics and regulatory network inference

To unravel regulatory mechanisms, transcription factor regulon activity was inferred using pySCENIC (v0.12.1),64 with Regulon Activity Scores (RAS) calculated based on co-expression modules. The relative abundance of 13 distinct T cell subtypes was estimated via ssGSEA using GSVA (v2.4.2)65 on VST-normalized data (DESeq2). To model differentiation trajectories, Monocle3 (v1.4.26)66 was utilized. UMAP coordinates from Seurat were imported directly to construct the trajectory graph. Cells from the Resting Cluster (Cluster 6) were designated as the root to calculate pseudotime. Additionally, cellular differentiation potential was independently assessed using CytoTRACE2 (v1.1.0)67 applied to the raw count matrix.

Deep learning modeling of transcriptomic perturbations

To predict phenotypic states and quantify the magnitude of metabolite-induced perturbations, we developed a multi-task deep learning framework named DRUG-seq-PerturbFormer (DPF). The model utilizes the VST-normalized expression profiles of the 2,541 perturbed genes (identified in the differential analysis step) as input features. We employed a gene-wise tokenization strategy where each gene is projected into a learnable embedding space and processed by a Transformer Encoder (hidden dimension = 128, attention heads = 4) to capture complex gene-gene interactions, resulting in a compact latent embedding (dimension = 16) for each well. The model was trained using a multi-task learning objective to simultaneously optimize the prediction of diverse phenotypic attributes: (1) regression of the three continuous functional scores (Tscore, CytoTRACE2, and Pseudotime) using Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss; and (2) binary classification of cell states (Resting vs. Perturbed) using Binary Cross-Entropy (BCE) loss. The total loss function was defined as the sum of these components, and the model was optimized using the AdamW optimizer (learning rate = 1 × 10-3, batch size = 32) for 50 epochs with an 80/20 train-validation split.

To construct a robust metric for ranking metabolite potency, we integrated two distance metrics derived from the latent space: Euclidean distance (representing perturbation magnitude) and Cosine distance (representing directional shift) relative to the DMSO centroid. A unified PerturbScore was defined as a weighted sum: PerturbScore= α ×d(Euc)+ (1 - α) ×d(Cos).The optimal weight α=0.25 was determined via a grid search strategy (step size 0.01) by maximizing the separation efficiency between Perturbed and Resting states, evaluated using the Area Under the Curve (AUC), Kolmogorov-Smirnov (KS) statistic, and Cohen’s D effect size. Metabolites were stratified into three tiers based on the empirical distribution of DMSO control scores (SDMSO). Let Qp denote the p-th percentile of SDMSO. The tiers were defined as: High-perturbation (S>Q0.75), Mid- perturbation (Q0.5<S<Q0.75), and Low-perturbation (S<Q0.75). The source code is available at https://github.com/Bingobin/DRUGseq-PerturbFormer.

Gene-level contributions to the DPF-derived perturbation score were estimated using Integrated Gradients. For each sample, attributions were calculated relative to four plate-level DMSO reference profiles covering all 16 C6 DMSO samples. The attribution values obtained from the four baselines were averaged for each gene and then normalized within each sample by the sum of the absolute attribution values across all 2,541 input genes. The mean signed Integrated Gradient value for each gene was calculated by averaging its normalized signed attribution across all analyzed samples. Positive and negative values therefore indicate contributions toward higher and lower model-derived perturbation scores, respectively. Genes were ranked by the mean signed attribution, and the complete ranked list was used for exploratory Reactome gene-set enrichment analysis.

Bulk RNA-seq data processing and analysis

Raw sequencing reads were quality-controlled and filtered using FastQC (v0.12.1) and Fastp (v0.23.2),57 then aligned to the Mus musculus genome (GRCm39) using STAR (v2.7.11b). Gene expression was quantified by featureCounts (v2.0.1).59 Differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 (v1.46.0)62 to compare DSS vs. BLANK and drug-treated groups vs. DSS. Significant DEGs were identified (adj. p < 0.05, |log2FC| > log2(1.5)). Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was conducted on the ranked gene lists, while Gene Ontology (GO) over-representation analysis was performed separately for significantly upregulated and downregulated genes using clusterProfiler (v4.14.6),63 with terms considered significant at an adjusted p value < 0.05. Finally, the relative abundance of infiltrating immune cells was estimated from TPM profiles using ImmuCellAI-mouse.68

Quantification and statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R (v4.2.1). Detailed descriptions of the statistical tests used are provided in the corresponding sections, figure legends, and supplementary tables. Technical reproducibility of the DRUG-seq experiments was assessed using two independent experimental batches. Bulk RNA-seq analyses and mouse validation experiments were conducted with at least three independent biological replicates, as specified in Tables S7, S8, S9, and S10.
