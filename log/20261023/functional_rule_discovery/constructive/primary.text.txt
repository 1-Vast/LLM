Abstract

Studying how antibacterials operate at subinhibitory concentrations reveals how they impede normal growth. While previous works demonstrated drugs can impact multiple aspects of growth, such as prolonging the doubling time or reducing the maximal bacterial load, a systematic understanding of this phenomenon is lacking. It remains unknown if common principles dictate how drugs interfere with growth. We monitored growth curves across thirty-eight drugs, spanning multiple mechanisms of action in Escherichia coli to deconvolve their impact on the lag, growth rate, and carrying capacity and developed a mathematical framework to quantitatively compare their effects. We discovered that drugs induced considerably different inhibition phenotypes, which were independent from the drugâs target. Functional assays of drug inactivation revealed that drug inactivation is a key shared factor underlying a lag-associated phenotype. Our work reveals that likely drug inactivation can be directly inferred from growth dynamics which is instrumental for rapidly identifying drug-inactivating bacteria.

Introduction

Antibiotic drugs target key cellular processes to arrest bacterial growth and, in some cases, kill the cells. Surveys of drug toxicity in different bacterial species have established that numerous non-antibiotic drugs, medications not administered to treat infectious diseases, can inhibit bacterial growth1,2,3. At subinhibitory drug concentrations, bacterial growth inhibition is incomplete, and compounds can influence different aspects of cell morphology and population growth dynamics. Such influences can be quantified by imaging single cells with a microscope4,5 and by monitoring changes in optical density in batch cultures growing in liquid media6,7. Interestingly, while changes in cellular morphology have been consistently associated with the mechanism of action underlying drug toxicity4, far less is known about the factors that determine how growth phenotypes of bacterial cultures are reshaped by different drugs. Resolving what can be learned about bacteriaâdrug interactions from bulk-culture phenotypes is valuable, given the ubiquity and high throughput of standard microplate-reader assays.

The population dynamics of bacteria growing in liquid batch cultures displays characteristic growth curves. These curves feature three typical growth phases with continuous transitions between them: an initial lag period, thought to reflect a physiological adjustment phase, followed by a transition to an exponential growth phase, and ultimately - with depletion of nutrients - a transition to a stationary phase of arrested growth8. Various mathematical models, such as the logistic growth model and the Gompertz equation, can be used to capture growth curves and extract useful parameters, such as the length of the lag period or the minimal generation time9,10. Fitting observed growth curves to such models is a standard approach for evaluating how perturbations, such as drugs11,12, stressors13, or nutrient changes14, influence specific features of growth dynamics.

The influence of drugs on population growth dynamics has been documented in multiple independent studies. Sub-inhibitory concentrations of antibiotics have been shown to impact growth rates in multiple bacterial species7,11,15,16, including E. coli11,15,16,17. Moreover, some of the same drugs, as well as additional compounds, have also been reported to influence the maximal bacterial load7,17,18 and the duration of the lag period7,11,17. Despite these observations, a systematic understanding of how drugs shape bacterial growth dynamics remains lacking. Specifically, it is still unclear whether drugs targeting the same cellular process similarly inhibit growth or whether other factors play a dominant role. Given that reports of drug influences on growth dynamics span different species, drug types, concentrations, and even measurement platforms, identifying overarching principles that govern drug-induced changes in growth dynamics is especially challenging. Ultimately, if such principles exist, uncovering them presents a powerful approach to rapidly characterize bacteria-drug interactions at scale by relatively straightforward and widespread assays.

Here, we aimed to systematically study how population growth dynamics are shaped by a broad panel of drugs in a single, well-characterized model system. We monitored the growth curves of E. coli exposed to twenty-one antibiotics and seventeen non-antibiotic drugs, that span diverse mechanisms, across a range of concentrations. Using the modified Gompertz equation9,10, we quantitatively characterized changes in key growth parameters across drugs and concentrations and developed a mathematical framework to infer drug-induced growth phenotypes at comparable toxicity levels. A comparison of phenotypes revealed that drugs elicit considerably different inhibition phenotypes, such as how they influence the growth rate, and that even drugs targeting the same cellular process can produce distinct effects on growth dynamics. By testing the impact of different antibiotic resistance cassettes on growth curves, we identified enzymatic inactivation as a key factor that can influence growth inhibition phenotypes. Follow up experiments, using a functional assay for detecting drug inactivation, revealed that drug inactivation is a key factor underlying variation in inhibition phenotypes observed across the drug panel. Taken together, our results suggest that drug inactivation, rather than the drugâs cellular target, is a major causal factor influencing growth inhibition phenotypes.

Results

A panel of 38 drugs reveals different effects on growth dynamics

To determine the impact of various drugs on bacterial proliferation, we evaluated the influence of thirty-eight drugs on growth curve dynamics. Specifically, we used a microplate reader to monitor the growth of the E. coli MG1655 strain over 21âh of growth across a wide range of sub-inhibitory drug concentrations (Fig. 1A, Table 2). The drug concentration range and the dilution ratio were chosen so we could observe multiple concentrations with a sub-inhibitory effect (typically 3â5 different concentrations). As might be expected, drug concentrations with detectable sub-inhibitory effects were considerably lower than those needed for complete growth inhibition (MIC), typically by 1â3 orders of magnitude (Table 2). The drug panel included twenty-one antibiotics, representative of the seven main mechanisms of action of standard antibiotics, and seventeen non-antibiotic drugs. We chose these non-antibiotic drugs since we previously observed that they have antibacterial activity and have concluded they operate by different toxicity modes compared to standard antibiotics3 (Fig. 1B).

The median growth curve, from three replicates, was fitted to a modified Gompertz equation. B Drug classes of the 38-drug panel (left panel) and the diagram of the mechanism of action of standard antibiotic classes (right panel). C Representative growth curves across a range of drug concentrations for 15 drugs (median of three replicates, smoothed over a one hour time window). To quantitatively characterize the impact of each drug on growth dynamics, we fitted the Gompertz equation9,10 to the median absorbance of three replicates across the entire experiment (right panel, Fig. 1A). Figure 1C shows representative drug sensitivity curves observed across fifteen drugs belonging to different drug classes (curves for all 38 drugs appear in Supplementary Fig. 1A). As expected, across all drugs, we observed increased growth inhibition with increased drug concentration. In agreement with previous reports of different growth inhibition phenotypes7,11,15,16,17,18, we also detected noticeable differences between the drugs. For example, trimethoprim, an antibiotic targeting folate biosynthesis, inhibited cells by slowing growth during the exponential phase of the growth curve. In contrast, furazolidone, a nitrofuran antibiotic, primarily prolonged the lag phase of the growth curve and had almost no impact on the growth rate during the exponential phase. Other drugs had notable impact on the bacterial load at the stationary phase (e.g., fosfomycin) while growth inhibition for some drugs impacted multiple aspects of the growth curve.

Quantitative analysis allows deconvolving key growth parameters

While conclusions about different growth inhibition phenotypes can be drawn directly from experimentally measured growth curves (Fig. 2A), they remain qualitative. Moreover, comparing phenotypes between drugs with mixed effects, e.g., simultaneously slowing growth and extending lag, is impractical without a mathematical framework. To systematically monitor how each drug influences specific features of the growth dynamics, we fitted a modified Gompertz equation9,10 to each of the growth curves. The equation can be adjusted to capture the growth curve by fitting three free parameters: the lag period, the maximal growth rate (or minimal generation time), and the maximal bacterial load. Figure 2A shows curves fitted to the absorbance measurements made in the presence of three drugs exhibiting clearly distinctive inhibition phenotype from one another. Across all tested concentrations, we observed that azacitidine primarily prolonged the lag period, sulfamethoxazole prolonged the generation time, and fosfomycin decreased the maximal bacterial load. As Fig. 2A shows, the Gompertz equation provided us with an excellent fit to the observed data. This fit allowed us to extract values of all the growth parameters from each curve experimentally measured at sub-inhibitory drug concentrations.

The shaded area marks the median curve +/- one standard deviation, and the solid lines mark the fitted Gompertz equation. B Interpolated growth curves corresponding to a range of normalized AUC values. The dashed line marks the growth curve corresponding to AUC50. C Values of Gompertz parameters fitted by the interpolated curves from (B) (empty circles). The Gompertz parameter values fitted to observed curves shown in (A) are marked by filled circles. The parameter values for the no-drug condition are shown by brown filled circles. Each of the three drugs influences, almost exclusively, only one Gompertz growth parameter.

Next, we wanted to qualitatively compare growth inhibition phenotypes across the drug panel. However, we could not directly compare parameter values from the fitted Gompertz equation since different curves reflected different intensities of growth inhibition. To facilitate a fair comparison between drugs, we sought to determine how each of the drugs reshapes the growth curve at an identical level of inhibition using a mathematical procedure: We used the area under the growth curve (AUC) as a metric for drug potency19 and used the fitted values of the Gompertz parameters to interpolate how each parameter changes across a wide range of AUC values (0.2â1 relative to the no-drug control). Indeed, as suggested by observations with the distinctive drugs (Fig. 2A), simulation with the Gompertz equations showed that each one of the growth parameters can individually account for the complete range of growth inhibition (Supplementary Fig. 2). This procedure allowed us to mathematically infer the growth curve for any AUC in that range.

Figure 2B shows interpolated curves for a normalized AUC range at 0.1 intervals (0.2â0.9) for each of the three representative drugs (curves for all 38 drugs appear in Supplementary Fig. 1B). The dashed curve, in Fig. 2B, corresponds to half maximal inhibition (termed AUC50 hereafter). Gompertz parameter values for AUC50 of all drugs and strains appear in Supplementary Table 1. Figure 2C shows the relationship between AUC and each of the Gompertz parameters for the empirical growth curves (shaded circles) and for interpolated inferred curves (empty circles) (curves for all 38 drugs are in Supplementary Fig. 1C). This analysis confirms the trends that we previously noticed: azacitidine prolonged lag period, sulfamethoxazole prolonged the generation time, and fosfomycin decreased the maximal bacterial load. Moreover, these distinctive examples also reveal that the three growth parameters can, in some empirical cases, be almost completely independent from each other since drugs can exclusively influence only one of them at a time.

Taken together, our combined experimental and computational approach allowed us to systematically quantify the impact of each drug on three distinct features of growth dynamics. Our analysis of distinctive drugs (Fig. 2) showed that in extreme cases, growth inhibition can be facilitated almost exclusively through a change in a single growth parameter (most noticeably in the AUC50 curves, Fig. 2B). Analysis of the entire panel revealed that growth inhibition for most drugs, however, operates by influencing more than one of the growth parameters (Supplementary Fig. 1C). The interpolation of parameter values in our mathematical framework will next allow us to compare how different drugs reshape the growth curve at comparable levels of potency.

Drug mechanism of action poorly predicts growth inhibition phenotypes

We used the Gompertz equation to interpolate the growth curves matching the AUC50 for the entire drug panel (Supplementary Fig. 1). To compare the different drugs, we plotted the values of the fitted Gompertz parameter values on a Barycentric coordinate system (Fig. 3A). In this system, growth inhibition facilitated by all three parameters is represented by the center of the triangle, as observed for pentamidine isethionate, while growth inhibition facilitated primarily by a single parameter is represented by positions close to the landscape vertices, as observed for azacitidine, sulfamethoxazole, and fosfomycin (insets in Fig. 3A). Overall, drugs were positioned continuously across almost the entire landscape. This wide spread indicated that different drugs impacted the growth dynamics in various ways.

Drugs are positioned by the strength of their association with the lag period, the generation time and the maximal bacterial load (the yellow, blue, and pink colors represent regions of strong association with a specific growth inhibition phenotype). The drugs corresponding to the numbers are shown in (B). The insets show the predicted growth curve corresponding to AUC50 for four drugs. Three drugs show an almost exclusive association with only one inhibition phenotype. B Hierarchical clustering of drugs by the standardized values of their Gompertz parameters. The colored frames define the clusters and the dendrogram above shows the clustering hierarchy. C Drugs in the Gompertz parameter landscape colored by their cluster association. D Drugs in the Gompertz parameter landscape colored by their mechanism of action. Co-localization of drugs with identical mechanisms of action is observed only for three antibiotics classes.

To determine if drug-induced growth inhibition can be divided into distinctive classes, we employed hierarchical clustering. In this analysis we standardized each parameter value by the dynamic range measured across all drugs and clustered drugs by the values of the three Gompertz parameters (Fig. 3B). The first principal cluster, colored in blue, was highly robust and represents drugs that primarily prolonged the lag period (shallow dendrogram branching suggests strong within-cluster cohesion). The next principal cluster, marked by orange-toned frames, represents drugs that primarily prolonged the generation time. The last principal cluster, represented in pink-toned frames, represents drugs that primarily impacted the maximal bacterial load. The latter two principal clusters were less robust and can be subdivided into smaller clusters representing drugs with combined effects. Figure 3C shows the cluster identity of each drug on the Barycentric landscape. This representation highlights that while clusters can be detected, their separation may be misleading since the data points reside on a continuous spread.

Lastly, we overlayed the mechanism of action and antibacterial activity (bactericidal or bacteriostatic) of each drug on the Barycentric landscape to test for associations with inhibition phenotypes. In Fig. 3D, we observed co-localization by mechanism for drugs from three antibiotics classes, targeting folate biosynthesis, DNA gyrase, and the large ribosomal subunit. However, we also noticed that drugs from three other antibiotic classes were spread across the landscape (nitrofurans, cell-wall targeting, and inhibitors of the small ribosomal subunit). Non-antibiotic drugs, likely operating through multiple different toxicity mechanisms3, were also spread across the landscape. Supplementary Fig. 3 shows the known antibacterial activity of all antibiotics in our panel. We observed that while bactericidal antibiotics are spread across the landscape, bacteriostatic antibiotics typically did not prolong the lag phase. This observation, however, did not extend to antibiotics that are both bactericidal and bacteriostatic.

To summarize, our computational methodology allowed us to compare how drugs inhibit growth by positioning all of them on a single coordinate system that reflected the contribution of each Gompertz parameter to growth inhibition. We observed that drugs were positioned continuously across almost the entire landscape. Overlaying the mechanism of action of antibiotics on the landscape revealed that drugs that operate by the same mechanism are not always co-localized on the landscape.

Enzymatic drug inactivation by resistance cassettes leads to a prolonged-lag

Since drug mechanism of action does not always correlate with growth inhibition phenotypes, we reasoned that growth inhibition may be caused by mechanisms that are independent from the drugâs cellular target. These could include drug-induced cellular responses, which confer resistance through a regulated response or drug inactivation over time by active drug biotransformation or passive chemical degradation. We expected that drug inactivation will manifest first in complete growth-arrest, due to initial high drug concentrations, and will be followed by a period of normal growth once drug concentration drops, due to its inactivation, below a critical threshold. Moreover, if drug inactivation depended on the number of cells in the culture, we would expect a rapid shift between these two periods due to positive feedback (as drug levels drop, the population size increases, and it collectively degrades more drug). We directly tested this hypothesis by genetically engineering the wild-type strain to express different drug resistance enzymes. Specifically, we wanted to test if enzymes that modify or degrade a drug will reshape the growth curve and prolong the lag period (Fig. 4A, left panels). Moreover, as a control, we wanted to validate that resistance that is independent from drug inactivation, such as that gained by efflux pumps, will not lead to a prolonged lag period since drug inactivation is not taking place (Fig. 4A, right panels).

A Mechanism of antibiotic resistance cassettes. CAT and ApmA are acetyltransferases that modify the antibiotic (chemical modification shown in red). Mef(B), QepA, and FloR are efflux pumps that remove the drug from the cytoplasm. B Landscape position shifts due to the expression of each resistance cassettes (marked by plus) relative to the wild-type strain (marked by a filled black circle). Expression of a cassette that shifted the drug position by more than 10% vertically towards the lag vertex was considered as evidence for a prolonged lag. C Drugs annotated as being inactivated are localized closer to the lag vertex. The white circles mark the position of drugs annotated as being inactivated by the functional assay. The black circles mark the position of drugs annotated as not being inactivated. Circles with a plus sign mark strains engineered to express resistance cassettes. The red dashed line is the optimal front for classifying drug inactivation. The left panel shows the skewness towards drug inactivation or no drug inactivation. D Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve represents the performance of the binary classifier for different positions of the front. The red circle marks the optimal position on the ROC curve.

We independently integrated six different drug resistance cassettes into the chromosome: three antibiotic efflux pumps encoded by mef(B)20, qepA21, and floR22, two antibiotic inactivation enzymes encoded by cat23 and apmA24, and an antibiotic target-altering enzyme encoded by ermC25. These cassettes were chosen since they are well-characterized and suitable for exploring our hypothesis. Since ermC expression resulted in almost complete resistance to its cognate antibiotic azithromycin, we removed ermC from our analysis. Supplementary Fig. 4 shows the growth curves observed across the six resistance cassettes and the wild-type strain in the relevant antibiotics.

To evaluate how expression of the cassettes impacts different features of the growth curve, we compared the position of the drug in the Barycentric landscape between the wild-type strain and in the genetically engineered strain. For these comparisons, we extracted the Gompertz parameters corresponding to the AUC50 for each of the strains to account for the increased resistance due to cassette expression. In agreement with our hypothesis, we observed the expected influence of cassettes on the position of the drugs in the landscape (Fig. 4B). Specifically, drug-modifying enzymes changed the lag phase without considerably influencing the growth rate or the bacterial load. In contrast, expression of efflux pumps had only a small impact on the drug position and local movements were not noticeably towards the lag vertex.

Drug inactivation is associated with a prolonged-lag phenotype in multiple drugs

Results from the heterologous expression of resistance cassettes suggested drug inactivation (by its biotransformation) leads to a prolonged-lag phenotype. We therefore further hypothesized that the position of multiple drugs in the Barycentric landscape may reflect some degree of drug inactivation in the wild-type strain. Such inactivation may arise from multiple mechanisms, including enzymatic degradation, drug modification, and irreversible binding to the cellular target. We used a functional assay for drug inactivation that we previously developed2,26 to evaluate this hypothesis across the entire drug panel. Briefly, the functional assay tested if incubation of the drug with bacteria for multiple hours reduced drug efficacy. This test is performed by filtering out the bacteria after incubation and aliquoting the conditioned supernatant into a fresh bacterial culture. As a control, drug was incubated without bacteria. Supplementary Fig. 5 shows results of the functional assay and the conclusion regarding drug inactivation.

Figure 4C shows the position of the drugs on the Barycentric landscape together with their annotation by the functional assay (inactivated or not inactivated). Consistent with our hypothesis, we observed that most of the drugs annotated as inactivated (white circles) were indeed positioned closer to the lag vertex relative to the rest of the drugs (black circles). This observation was supported by calculating an inactivation skewness score (Fig. 4C, left panel).

The inactivation assay also confirmed the existence of inactivation by the two drug-modifying enzymes (CAT and ApmA) and reassured that the resistance cassettes encoding for efflux pumps (Mef(B), QepA, and FloR) did not inactivate their cognate antibiotics.

Lastly, given the degree of consistency between drug inactivation and localization close to the lag vertex, we decided to use a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve to find the ideal threshold for determining if a drug is likely being inactivated by the values of the Gompertz parameters. We therefore established a binary classifier by moving a horizontal front across the landscape and calculating its sensitivity and specificity. This moving front is parallel to the edge connecting the âslow growthâ and âlow capacityâ vertices (Fig. 4D). The resulting ROC curve had an AUC of 0.83, which indicated that this simple classification method is powerful. Using the Youdenâs J statistic, we found the point on the ROC curve that maximizes the classifierâs sensitivity over a random model (shown by the red line in Fig. 4C). This Youdenâs J statistic (Jâ=â0.52) corresponds to a sensitivity of 0.89 and a specificity of 0.71. Taken together, our results from the functional assay and the ROC analysis, suggest that the shape of the growth curve at sub-inhibitory drug concentrations is a valuable indicator of bacteria-dependent drug inactivation.

Discussion

Antibacterial compounds, used at sub-inhibitory concentrations, can differently influence growth dynamics of bacterial batch cultures7,11,15,16,17,18. While widely reported, observations of this phenomenon remained largely unconnected since they were made across different species, media types, and measurement platforms. Our systematic analysis of 38 drugs in E. coli confirms and extends previous fragmented reports by quantifying how each drug alters lag time, growth rate, and carrying capacity in a uniform experimental framework (Fig. 1, Supplementary Fig. 2). Using a single model system enabled us to rule out cross-study technical differencesâresearchers, organisms, and measurement platformsâas the sole source of variation in growth dynamics at sub-inhibitory drug concentrations.

The mapped phenotypic landscape revealed that only a few drugs culminated in extreme phenotypes that were dominated by a single growth parameter change (Fig. 3A). Almost all drugs in the tested panel yielded complex inhibition phenotypes that reflect multiple growth inhibition effects. It is interesting to also note that the region of the phenotypic landscape representing a combination of low capacity and long lag remained relatively sparse. While the underlying mechanisms of complex inhibition phenotypes cannot be resolved by our study, we hypothesize that they reflect time-dependent processes that transpire over multiple hours of culture growth, such as gradual damage accumulation, time consuming reallocation of resources, or changes in gene transcription. Heterogeneity in how cells experience or respond to a drug is an additional possible mechanism underlying complex phenotypes since bulk population measurements may average out multiple subpopulations that feature different pure phenotypes.

Previous analysis of drug-induced phenotypic variation in response to different antibacterials revealed that changes in bacterial cell morphology, detectable by microscopy, were strongly associated with the pathways targeted by the drug4. This association served as the basis for multiple efforts to develop machine learning algorithms that classify morphological features to predict a compoundâs mechanism of action5. Inspired by these works, we tested if drug-induced changes in growth phenotypes are indicative of the drugâs cellular target, yet found multiple cases refuting this possibility. We noted multiple cases of drugs displaying dissimilar growth inhibition phenotypes while sharing an identical toxicity mechanism (Fig. 3D).

Our experiments with different drug resistance cassettes pointed to one biological factor that can considerably influence the growth inhibition phenotypes â drug inactivation (Fig. 4B). Follow up experiments, using a functional assay for detecting drug inactivation, further substantiated it as a major factor underlying a lag-associated phenotype (Fig. 4C). We note that multiple different cellular mechanisms may underly such drug inactivation, such as its degradation, modification, intracellular accumulation, and irreversible target-binding. The key shared feature of all these mechanisms is their capacity to detoxify the extracellular environment and allow cells that were transiently arrested to resume growth and eventually robustly replicate. While we found a strong association between a prolonged lag phenotype and drug inactivation (Fig. 4D), some drugs are notable exceptions (outlier black and white circles positioned remotely from their respective groups in Fig. 4C). While these outliers may be attributed to limited accuracy of the functional screen, they might also reflect interesting biological phenomena. For example, a drug inducing a long-lag phenotype that is not inactivated may reveal that after an initial shock, cells mounted an effective response that counteracts the drug. An additional exciting possibility is that the prolonged lag reflects a rare drug-resistant sub-population that continues to grow but remains below the detection limit at initial stages of the experiment.

While comprehensive, our study has clear limitations that are worth outlining. First, it focuses on a single Gram-negative model organism growing in a defined medium. It will therefore be important to continue this work and extend it to other species and growth conditions. Within E. coli, future work could test whether our method rapidly distinguishes ESBL-producing clinical isolates that hydrolyze Î²-lactams from other resistant isolates that instead rely on alternative mechanisms such as altered penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs)27, reduced permeability due to porin mutations28, or increased efflux29. Rapid distinction between these alternatives may have important implications, as further discussed in the next paragraph. Our experimental methodology, which relied on bulk-measurements, represents another limitation. While this method allowed high-throughput measurements across hundreds of conditions (drugs, concentrations, strains, replicates), it does not provide any insight into the molecular and cellular details.

Taken together, our results established a method to quantitatively compare drug-induced changes to population growth and revealed that drugs modulate different aspects of growth dynamics. Moreover, our work suggests that the shape of growth curves can serve as a powerful predictor of drug inactivation. Reliable prediction of drug inactivation, irrespective of mechanism, may be especially useful for studying communal drug resistance. Within single-species cultures, the cell density-dependent minimum inhibitory concentration, known as the âinoculum effectâ, is studied extensively since it can undermine accurate drug susceptibility testing30. Since drug inactivation is not the exclusive driver of this phenomenon, predication of drug inactivation can be useful for inferring the potential underlying cause of the inoculum effect. With mixed-species communities, drug inactivation has been observed to facilitate communal resistance against beta-lactam31, macrolide, tetracycline and chloramphenicol antibiotics32. In these complex communities, our work enables prediction of drug inactivation of each community member by analyzing drug-induced changes in the memberâs growth dynamics, which will allow for rapid identification of the specific organism underlying the communal resistance phenomenon.

Methods

Bacterial strains, media, and growth conditions

All bacterial strains used in this study are shown in Table 1. All growth experiments were performed in defined minimal synthetic M9 media: M9 salts (Fisher Cat#DF0485-17), supplemented with 0.2% amicase (Sigma Cat#82514-1KG) and 0.4% glucose (Fisher Cat#D16-1). Overnight cultures for all growth experiments were grown at 37âÂ°C with 200ârpm orbital shaking with 30âÎ¼g/mL of streptomycin antibiotic.

For all experiments measuring drug degradation (functional assay), the strains used for the incubation period were grown overnight in Lysogeny Broth (LB) media at 37âÂ°C with 200ârpm orbital shaking with 30âÎ¼g/mL of streptomycin antibiotic.

Cloning strains

The antibiotic resistance cassettes, cat, apmA, and ermC, were cloned from the Minimal Antibiotic Resistance Platform (Addgene Kit #1000000143) that was a gift from Gerard Wright33. The DNA encoding for the antibiotic resistance genes, mef(B), qepA, and floR were synthesized by TWIST Bioscience and sequences were obtained from the Comprehensive Antibiotic Resistance Database34. We made inserts containing one of these antibiotic resistance cassettes along with an 18âbp nucleotide barcode, streptomycin-resistance cassette, and 34 or 40âbp homology arms to the upstream and downstream sequences of lacZ gene of E. coli MG1655. Gene expression for these resistance genes was driven by constitutive promoters: Pbla promoter for cat, apmA, and ermC, and a T7 promoter for mef(B), qepA, and floR. We knocked out lacZ from MG1655 using lambda red recombination and replaced it with one of the inserts. For engineering the wild-type strain we completed the same cloning steps, except the insert did not contain an antibiotic resistance cassette nor a promoter sequence.

Monitoring growth curves

We grew cultures overnight in M9 media with 30âÎ¼g/mL of streptomycin antibiotic. We diluted bacteria to an OD600 of 0.005 and transferred them to 96-well plates (Nunc Cat# 167008) containing serial dilutions of the tested drugs in triplicate to reach a final volume of 120âÎ¼L. All drugs and concentrations used are shown in Table 2. We incubated the cultures at 37âÂ°C with 800ârpm shaking and continuously monitored growth of cultures with absorbance (600ânm) measurements every 20âmin for at least 21âh using an automated microplate reader (BioTek LogPhase 600). Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) was determined to be the lowest concentration at which the AUC of the growth curve is less than 5% of the no-drug growth curve or, for some drugs, the concentration where the growth curve flattens out.

Quantifying drug inhibition phenotypes

To quantify drug inhibition phenotypes, we fitted a modified Gompertz equation9,10 to the growth curve measured by the microplate reader (median of three same-plate replicate cultures). The modified Gompertz equation is used to describe the growth of bacteria with the dependent variable typically transformed into the logarithm of the relative population size (\(\mathrm{ln}(N(t)/{N}_{0})\) where \({N}_{0}\) is the initial population size at \(t=0\)). Where y(t) defines the relative population size at time point \(t\). We calculate this value by dividing by the blank-subtracted absorbance at time point t with the initial population blank-subtracted absorbance. Since the absorbance of the initial population is too low to be reliably measured, it was inferred from the OD600 prior to the dilution and the dilution factor. Using a custom MATLAB (MathWorks) script we implemented a specific version of the model to mitigate noisy absorbance measurements in the initial phase of the growth curve (close to the microplate readerâs detection limit). In our implementation, we added a preliminary procedure to reduce reliance of the fit to the initial absorbance measurements that typically include a high degree of noise due to the low amount of signal. In this procedure, we fitted a Gompertz equation to the blank-subtracted absorbance and found a fitted lag. We then generated a vector of weights using a sigmoid logistic function with this lag period.

In the next step, we fit a Gompertz model to the logarithm of the relative population size and we used the vector from the sigmoid function (w) to weigh the residuals when finding the optimal fit. We did not perform a Gompertz fit for highly inhibitory drug concentrations (final absorbance is lower than 0.2).

To infer the dependency between growth inhibition and each of the Gompertz parameters we performed the following procedure. First, we fitted a linear interpolation that connected parameter values from consecutive concentrations with a line to describe the relationship between the drug concentration and the values of each Gompertz parameter. We then sampled hundreds of points within the concentration range from this piecewise linear interpolation. For each sampled concentration, we generated a growth curve for 21âh by setting the matching parameter values in a Gompertz equation. To find the growth curve matching AUC50, we numerically calculated the AUC for each predicted curve (using the trapz function in MATLAB) and we identified the curve corresponding to 0.5*AUCcontrol (allowing up to 0.5% error).

For each drug, we predicted the value of the lag period, generation time and negative of the maximal bacterial load from the growth curve corresponding to AUC50. We standardized these values to a range of [0,1] by finding the value range of each parameter across all drugs (subtracting the minimum and dividing by the maximum minus the minimum). The Barycentric coordinate can be calculate by dividing three standardized values by their sum.

Functional assay to evaluate drug inactivation

We used a functional assay that we previously developed2,26 to determine if bacterial incubation inactivates the drug. In these experiments we grew the strain in question, the wild-type or cassette strain depending on the drug of interest, overnight and pelleted a 5âml culture. We washed this pellet three times in a buffer of water and M9 salts before incubating for an additional one hour in the same buffer. We then diluted this stock to an OD600 of 0.5 into 1âmL aliquots with drug in a deep-well 96-well plate. For the incubation period, we used each drug at a concentration of IC75 or IC99. The inhibitory concentrations (IC) were determined by comparing the absorbance of the culture after eight hours of growth across multiple drug concentrations and plotting the drug sensitivity curves. These IC values were determined for the drug-sensitive reporter strain (wild-type) used for evaluation of conditioned supernatant. We also included wells with the same concentration of drug, but without bacteria as controls for non-biological degradation (e.g., low stability at the incubation temperature and light sensitivity). To obtain conditioned supernatant, the plate was incubated in a shaker at 37âÂ°C, 900ârpm orbital shaking. 400âÎ¼L of the supernatant was sampled after 6 and/or 24âh and filtered by spinning down at 5000âÃâg using a 96-well plate 0.22âÎ¼m filters (PALL Corporation, Cat# 8119).

After we obtained the conditioned supernatants for each drug and strain in question (wild-type of genetically engineered), we evaluated the amount of residual drug left by monitoring the growth of a single drug-sensitive reporter strain (wild-type) in the presence of conditioned, or control, supernatant. An overnight culture was first diluted to OD600 of 0.004 into M9 media. We aliquoted 60âÎ¼L of this culture into a 96-well plate and added 60âÎ¼L of supernatant to each well. The plate was incubated at 37âÂ°C and 800ârpm shaking in an automated microplate reader (BioTek LogPhase 600). Absorbance (600ânm) was monitored every 20âmin for 24âh. We used a statistical test to identify drug inactivation. For this test, we calculated the area under the growth curve (AUC) after blank subtraction of three replicates and we used a one-tailed t-test to check whether the conditioned supernatant had consistently higher AUC as compared to the control supernatant (pâ<â0.05). We note that this assay will not detect drug degradation that is cell-independent (passive chemical degradation).

To calculate skewness toward inactivation or no-inactivation in the Barycentric landscape we used the position of each drug on the y-axis. We used a sliding window covering 10% of the axis and subtracted the number of drugs with no-inactivation (black dots) from the number of drugs with inactivation (white dots) and divided the result by the total number of drugs in that window. A skewness score of 1 shows that all drugs in the window are inactivation drugs, while a skewness score of -1 shows that all drugs in the window are no-inactivation drugs.
