2 Materials and methods
2 Materials and methodsThe overall architecture of scBalFlow is illustrated in Fig. 2. The framework consists of two main stages: (i) Response Prediction in the first stage, which utilizes a Residual Multi-Layer Perceptron (ResMLP) to predict the Rsim2 score, using a concatenated vector consisting of the encodings of the drug, cell type, and dosage as its input. (ii) Expression Prediction in the second stage, which employs a Flow Matching generative model constructed with multi-layer self-attention blocks. This stage uses Gaussian noise as the latent space and perturbation conditions as control signals via Adaptive Layer Normalization [AdaLN; Peebles and Xie (2023)] to iteratively generate perturbed gene expression profiles through ODE integration.Figure 2The overall architecture of scBalFlow framework. scBalFlow adopts a two-stage strategy to address the imbalance between weak and strong perturbation responses. The data are divided into matched control and perturbation groups. Cell-type representations are obtained by linearly projecting the mean expression profile of the corresponding control cells, drugs are represented using PubChem fingerprints, and dosages are encoded through a linear layer. In Stage 1, the precomputed coefficient of determination between the mean control and perturbed expression profiles, denoted as Rsim2, serves as the response-similarity label. The three embeddings are concatenated and fed into an MLP-based response predictor, which estimates Rsim2 and is optimized using GAI Loss. In Stage 2, the real Rsim2 values are used during training to route highly responsive conditions to the flow-matching generator, whereas weakly responsive expression profiles are generated by sampling from the matched control group. During inference, the predicted Rsim2 from Stage 1 is used for routing because post-perturbation expression profiles are unavailable for unseen conditions. The two stages use the same encoding schemes but do not share encoder parameters.A two-stage workflow begins with matched control cells and perturbation conditions. Stage 1 encodes the mean matched-control profile, a PubChem drug fingerprint, and dose, concatenates the embeddings, and predicts the condition-level Rsim2 using a residual MLP trained with GAI Loss. Stage 2 routes highly responsive conditions to a conditional flow-matching Transformer that maps Gaussian noise to perturbed expression, while weakly responsive expression profiles are produced by sampling matched controls. True Rsim2 is used for routing during training and the Stage-1 prediction is used at inference; the two stages do not share encoder weights.2.1 Response prediction stage2.1.1 Training dataIn this stage, the model does not directly process high-dimensional gene expression data. Instead, it takes the perturbation condition cond as input, which is composed of the drug molecule (SMILES), the target cell type (cell), and the drug dosage (dose). Given Nchem drugs, Ncell cell types, and Ndose dosages, the total number of unique perturbation combinations is Ncond=Nchem×Ncell×Ndose. We pre-calculate the Rsim2 ∈RNcond between the mean expression of the control group and the mean expression of the perturbed group for each condition (details are provided in Section 3, available as supplementary data at Bioinformatics online). The dataset is thus defined as (cond,Rsim2). Notably, Rsim2 is used to determine whether a perturbation condition requires flow-based generation, whereas evaluation R2 measures the agreement between model-generated expression profiles and the observed perturbed profiles in Stage 2.2.1.2 Condition encodingFor drug molecular encoding, we selected PubChem as the primary method, representing molecules as 881-dimensional binary vectors where each dimension indicates the presence or absence of a specific chemical substructure. To enable the model to generalize to unseen dosage levels and cell types, dosages are normalized using a reference dosage of 10 μM (i.e. xdose/10) and transformed into an embedding of dimension ddose through a linear layer. Cell types are encoded using the mean gene expression profile of their corresponding control cells and transformed into a cell-type embedding of dimension dcell. The resulting embeddings are concatenated with the drug representation to form the conditional input embedding hcond∈Rddrug+ddose+dcell.2.1.3 Response predictor architectureWe compared the performance of MLP and Transformer architectures. Results indicated that the ResMLP architecture is more suitable for this task, as excessively high model capacity can lead to overfitting on minority classes in imbalanced datasets. Our Response Predictor consists of multiple residual blocks, each containing a linear layer and a SiLU activation function. Notably, the final output R2¯sim is constrained within the range [−1,1] using an auxiliary output layer: (1) R2¯sim=tanh(h) It should be noted that while the theoretical range of Rsim2 is (−∞,1], in practice, most of the observed minimum values are approximately −1. This bounded output significantly improves training stability compared to direct Rsim2 regression.2.1.4 Imbalance trainingTo determine the optimal strategy for mitigating the severe data imbalance in the dataset, we evaluated several established techniques, including Oversampling, Balanced MSE, and GAILoss. Specifically, the Oversampling strategy operates at the data level by selectively duplicating minority-class instances—namely, those exhibiting low Rsim2 values, which correspond to strong biological responses, to construct more balanced training batches. Alternatively, balanced MSE addresses imbalanced regression from a probabilistic perspective, modifying the standard mean squared error into a statistically principled, weighted loss function that accommodates skewed target distributions. GAI serves as an advanced refinement of this concept; it incorporates Gaussian priors to smooth the target label space, thereby reducing generalization errors in sparse, high-response regions.Our empirical evaluations demonstrate that GAI consistently achieves superior performance compared to the other baseline strategies. Consequently, we integrate the GAI-based loss into our framework. First, GAI parameterizes the empirical training distribution ptrain(Rsim2) using a Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM): (2) ptrain(Rsim2)=∑k=1KπkN(Rsim2;μk,Σk) where K is the number of Gaussian components, and πk,μk,Σk represent the mixture weight, mean, and covariance matrix of the k-th component, respectively.By substituting the GMM into the Balanced MSE objective, the product of two Gaussian distributions yields an unnormalized Gaussian, enabling a strictly analytical closed-form solution. Thus, the final GAI Loss function optimized in our first stage is formulated as: (3) LGAI(Rsim2,R2¯sim)=(Rsim2−R2¯sim)22σnoise2 +log ∑k=1KπkN(R2¯sim;μk,Σk+σnoise2). where σnoise2 is a learnable parameter representing the noise variance.2.2 Gene expression prediction stage2.2.1 Training dataFor the generative stage, we exclusively retain perturbed gene expression samples where Rsim2 <threshold, denoted as xp∈RNgene.2.2.2 Condition encodingThe same drug encoding is reused in the prediction stage and transformed by a linear layer into hdrug∈Rd, where d denotes the target dimension. To support generalization to unseen dosages, the dosage value is normalized using a reference dosage of 10 µM (i.e. xdose/10) and projected into a dosage embedding hdose∈Rd through a linear layer. Instead of using discrete cell-type embeddings, each cell type is represented by the mean expression profile of its corresponding control cells and transformed into a cell embedding hcell∈Rd via a linear layer. The time step t is encoded using an MLP layer to obtain the temporal embedding ht∈Rd. These embeddings are combined through element-wise addition to form the final control signal: (4) hctrl=ht+hcell+hdose+hdrug. 2.2.3 Flow matching modelWhile conventional approaches typically utilize pre-perturbation samples from the same cell type as the initial state, our empirical observations during training suggest that the marginal differences between pre- and post-perturbation profiles result in excessively slow model convergence. To address this, we instead employ a standard Gaussian distribution x0∈RNgene as the initial state for the generation process. The model defines a path between x0 and the target distribution (perturbed expression xp) by learning a time-dependent velocity field vt over the interval t∈[0,1].We use hctrl to modulate the velocity prediction via AdaLN. The control vector hctrl is projected through a linear layer and split into four modulation parameters: scaling and gating factors for the Multi-Head Self-Attention block (smsa,gmsa) and the Feed-Forward Network (smlp,gmlp): (5) smsa,gmsa,smlp,gmlp=Split(Linear(hctrl)) To improve training stability by ensuring the model behaves as an identity mapping upon initialization, we apply the following transformations: (6) g¯msa=tanh(gmsa), g¯mlp=tanh(gmlp) (7) s¯msa=1+smsa, s¯mlp=1+smlp Let MSA(·) represent the block of Multi-Head Self-Attention, FFN(·) represent Feed-Forward Network, and LN(·) represent Layer Norm block. The computation within each block is defined as follows: (8) zt=ExpEmbedder(xt) (9) ht=zt+g¯msa·LN(MSA(LN(zt)⊙s¯msa)) (10) h¯t=ht+g¯mlp·LN(FFN(LN(ht)⊙s¯mlp)) where zt∈RNgene×d is the hidden state at time t, and ExpEmbedder(·) independently projects each scalar gene-expression value xt,g into a d-dimensional gene token. Finally, the hidden state h¯t∈RNgene×d is projected back to the gene expression dimension to predict the velocity vt∈RNgene: (11) vt=Linear(h¯t) The sample at the next time step is updated as xt+1=xt+vt. The final predicted expression x¯p is obtained through multi-step ODE integration from t=0 to t=1, optimized using the MSE loss.2.2.4 Datasets and preprocessingThe single-drug perturbation datasets include SciPlex3 and McFarland. SciPlex3 contains 9-drug OOD split and two dosage splits, while McFarland includes five drug splits and three cell-type splits. The combinatorial drug perturbation dataset, ComboSciPlex, is a scRNA-seq subset derived from the combinatorial perturbation experiments in SciPlex3 and includes one drug combination split. More details are provided in Section 1, available as supplementary data at Bioinformatics online.Gene expression values were normalized using log(x+1) and library size normali zation. We select the top 2000 Highly Variable Genes (HVGs) for SciPlex3 and McFarland dataset, and 1000 for ComboSciPlex dataset.For each condition, we selected the top 50 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) as a subset for additional testing (detailed selection methods are provided in Sections 1 and 2, available as supplementary data at Bioinformatics online).
